Chapter 27. The Right to Air

Mirena did not allow him to open the door.

Asgar stood with the receiver to his ear and looked at pale ground cut by dark fractures. The air trembled above it. After two years in which he had learned to read the weather by the way the grass lay at the entrance to the shelter, one glance was enough to understand: there was nothing to breathe out there.

"About sixty degrees," Mirena said. "Not enough oxygen. Strong ultraviolet radiation."

"People?"

"Not established. There are signs of functioning infrastructure."

She gave the year later. Seven hundred three million one hundred eighteen thousand four hundred two.

Asgar repeated the beginning of the number to himself and gave up.

A low machine approached from the direction of a dark structure that almost blended into the fractures and stopped several steps away. It did not approach the door: it circled the cabin in a wide arc, paused where the upright entered the ground, then returned to its previous position. Symbols lit on its body.

Then something outside began speaking.

The first phrase was incomprehensible. Another followed in a different language, then a third, with equal pauses between them. In one, Asgar recognized English.

He leaned toward the glass.

"Russian. I speak Russian."

A symbol on the machine pulsed in time with his voice. The sequence stopped.

"More," the machine said. The syllables were misplaced, but the word was Russian.

Asgar gave his name, said how long he had been in the cabin and where he had come from. No one interrupted him. When the voice spoke again, the spaces between syllables had evened out.

"Better. Now listen carefully: do not open the door."

"Why?"

"Because outside you will die within minutes, and I will not have time to do anything. Give me a little time. I will prepare a way out for you."

Asgar relayed this to Mirena and sat on the bag.

For the first time in a very long time, nothing was required of him.

---

An opaque shelter unfolded in front of the door. Its edges went down to the ground without touching the red uprights: that was the first thing Asgar asked about, still through the glass. Air and cooling were supplied inside. Only after that did Mirena allow him to open the door.

The protective suit lay on a stand. It had been assembled to measurements taken through the glass while the shelter unfolded. Asgar asked how long the cooling would last and whether the suit would work if contact with the node was lost, listened to the answer, and put it on himself, refusing to let a manipulator move near his face. The neck section did not seat correctly the first time. He stopped, loosened his fingers, and started again.

When the cooling came on, he simply stood for several seconds.

"Why are you doing all this for me?" he asked.

"I keep living things alive here. You are alive. That's enough. No further explanation is necessary."

"Where are we going?" Asgar asked.

"To the building. There is a room where you can breathe. Three hundred meters. I'll go in front."

The receiver remained.

Asgar went back into the cabin, picked it up, and told Mirena he was leaving: three hundred meters, a building, breathable air inside.

"I can't take the cord with me."

"I know."

Asgar looked through the glass at the dry ground cut by dark fractures.

"If it isn't here when I come back," he said.

"Wait where there is air."

"I know."

He returned the receiver to the cradle, checked that the cord hung freely, and closed the helmet.

Asgar stepped out of the cabin and walked several steps through the shelter.

The sound changed behind him.

He had managed to get about five steps away.

The edges of the shelter were sinking into the ground, folding inward, and there was nothing beneath them. Four small holes from the uprights and a clean imprint of the threshold remained in the dry ground.

Asgar stood until the last wall folded down.

"I watched it with every instrument I have," the node said. "I did not understand a single moment of it."

"It's always like that."

He turned toward the building.

He walked the three hundred meters slowly. The dry surface crunched under his soles, the machine moved ahead, and his own breathing sounded too close.

At an uneven section, Asgar stopped and tested his footing with his left leg. There was a crack here.

"Crack," he said aloud. For two years there had been no one to talk to, and the habit remained.

He went around it.

---

Inside the building, there was a room suitable for a human.

Doors closed one after another. The node said the air was ready and he could remove the protection. Asgar still stood in the helmet for a little longer, looking at the bench by the wall.

Then he removed the helmet and gloves, placed them beside him, and sat down. Not on the ground, not on a rolled jacket, not on a rock that first had to be tested by hand. On a bench.

It was level and smooth. He shifted, arranging his legs, and caught himself looking for something to wedge under one edge.

He kept the water within reach longer than necessary. Asked how much he had used. Heard that the reserve was more than enough and opened it again.

His clothes were taken for cleaning one item at a time—that was how he asked for it, so everything else would remain in the room. The same low machine carried them away: it drove in, waited for Asgar to place an item on it, and left. Each time Asgar asked whether they would return that exact item rather than a similar one. When the jacket came back, he felt the sleeve, found a familiar worn spot, and put it away without wearing it.

On the first day he asked nothing. Ate small portions, slept, woke to the sounds of ventilation, and each time first reached for the knife.

The light in the room stayed on until he figured out how to dim it.

At night he thought he could have been sitting out there on the ground beside the four holes.

The thought did not last long. The air in the suit was maintained by the node, not by him.

In the morning he asked the node to tell him if anything appeared at that spot.

"I'll be watching anyway," the node said. "But since you're asking, I'll say it out loud."

---

On the second day the node said something had been left for Asgar.

He was sitting on the edge of the sleeping place, pulling on a boot.

"In my engineering storage there is a package. It was brought long before me and passed from hand to hand until the hands ran out and only I remained. It is to be opened under one condition: if an object appears that corresponds to the event of August sixteenth, two thousand fourteen, and at the same time retains an autonomous return circuit. Your cabin matches."

"Were you waiting for me?"

"Not for you. Your name isn't there. No name, no date of birth, no family. Only a description of the object."

He asked to see the condition. It was exactly that.

"Who wrote it?"

"People who worked with similar things many generations after you. They did not build your cabin—their work is known to me, and there is nothing like it there. They studied the trace it left."

Asgar tightened the lace and straightened.

For two years, the river, the fire, and his own mistakes had answered him. Here he could simply ask.

"I don't need their entire history. I have one question. Can I choose where I come out?"

"No. And the package does not say otherwise. Your cabin has its own return route. Those people did not know how to choose the next stop."

"Then what did they know how to do?"

The node opened the first diagram.

When a direct return of their installation could not be completed, it held an intermediate stable state, rechecked the actual mass, and only after that built the next segment. Asgar asked to see it again without the labels.

"So it couldn't make it home, caught on wherever it could, checked what it was actually dragging with it, and continued?"

"Crude, but correct."

"Sounds like mine."

"Similar. No more than that. Their installation occupied a large complex and did not work without external infrastructure. I found no evidence that it was related to your cabin."

"Then why did they leave this for it?"

"Because of the second property."

The node removed the diagram and showed two states of one of their installations separated by a long interval of time.

"Imagine two rooms that once really had a door between them. Later, you cannot place such a door wherever you want. But if a system can reconstruct its side of the previous contact precisely enough, the opening that already existed can become a boundary condition again."

Asgar frowned.

"I don't understand. Return to the same date?"

"Not choose a date. Use an old physical contact as an address. Only one that actually existed in the history of this system. You cannot create a new address this way."

"And they did this?"

"With their own installations—yes. They repeated an already registered contact. They did not guarantee transfer of a person."

Asgar looked again at the two states.

"What did they call it?"

"Historical addressing."

He repeated the words and immediately grimaced.

"Say it normally."

"A door can be attempted again only where it has already existed once."

That was clearer.

"Is that why August sixteenth is here?"

"That is why the authors of the package considered the August sixteenth event to belong to the relevant class of problem. It is not proof that your source can open that specific contact."

Asgar remained sitting, looking at the level floor.

Until this moment he had imagined the road in only one direction: the next stop, another one, the source somewhere ahead. Now, for the first time, another thought appeared. Not that the cabin would turn around. That somewhere ahead there might be a way to open again a door that had already been open.

---

On the third day Asgar reached the end of the package.

The last thing there was neither a calculation nor a diagram, but a list of questions the authors had not answered. Who decides for a person who accidentally ends up inside a machine. How much knowledge from one era can be dropped into another. Asgar read the third question twice.

What do you do if the loop already exists?

Beneath it was a single case. A researcher received the result of an experiment he had not yet performed. The entire transmission fit in one line. He then chose the next experiment, skipping the verification of the first, and the later report cited the earlier record.

"Where is the actual measurement?" Asgar asked.

"No one performed it. The answer is confirmed only by the message in which it appears."

Asgar opened the link from the later report and again arrived at the same line.

"So they never knew where the correct answer came from."

"Exactly. They knew only that it had already appeared earlier than its cause."

With Olmer, Asgar had moved between documents in exactly the same way, hoping the next link would finally contain the basis. Here the basis closed back on itself.

Asgar closed the link and looked at the list of questions for a while.

"If an old door like that is ever opened again, could the person on this side do something there?"

"If the contact is physical—yes. Any action at the boundary becomes a real event. But the authors of the package could not predict what it would change afterward and did not guarantee transfer of a person to the other side."

"So you can reach an old moment and interfere, but no one knows what happens afterward."

"Correct."

The first thought was too simple. If somewhere ahead they really could open a contact with the beginning of his journey, he could try to change the very cause that had forced him to continue.

Katya would not wait twenty-five years for him. The children would not grow up without him.

And everything that had brought the present Asgar to this possibility had also started there.

"No," he said.

"What exactly?"

"The simplest option. Get in there earlier and try to cancel everything from the beginning."

"The package does not prohibit intervention."

"I'm not saying it does. I'm saying I don't know what I'd break by doing it."

He looked again at the question about the loop.

"The address isn't a solution."

"Agreed."

"Then the question is different."

"What question?"

Asgar did not formulate it immediately.

"If someday the old contact can be opened again, can I be returned without making the loop feed itself through the return?"

The node reviewed the remainder of the package.

"There is no answer to that question."

"Then that's what has to reach the source."

---

For the remaining days he returned to the package and asked the same things in different ways. The wording of the answers changed.

Every morning, the first thing he did was ask about the site. Every morning the node answered that there were still four holes there.

On the fifth day Asgar asked to see the area and watched the empty ground from a high viewpoint for a long time. On the sixth he did not ask.

---

On the eighth day the booth returned.

The node said it before Asgar had time to ask. While he got ready, the shelter unfolded over the site for a second time.

He took out his old things and realized he had grown unused to them in eight days. Everything had been cleaned, but the seams of the bag still held the smell of the shelter: smoke, grease, river dampness. Before, he had not distinguished the smell because he breathed it constantly. Asgar pressed the fabric to his face, then lowered it.

He checked the protective suit himself, following prompts. He stopped at the crack in the exposed section even though he already knew where it was.

He covered the three hundred meters faster than usual. In the shelter he removed the helmet, stepped inside, and lifted the receiver.

"I'm here. It came back."

"I hear you," Mirena said. "Eight days."

He held the receiver and listened to her voice longer than necessary. For eight days, the only thing that had spoken to him was the node.

"There was an old engineering package here," he said. "Not about me. About systems like this."

"What did it contain?"

"They knew how to reopen only a contact that their installation had already had once. Not choose a date. Use the old door as an address."

Mirena answered immediately:

"That isn't in the documentation available to me."

"I know. The first thought was simple: if someone ever opens such a contact, try to intervene earlier and prevent this whole path from happening at all."

"And?"

"And no one knows what that would break."

He pressed the receiver more tightly to his ear.

"Record another question together with my record. If it reaches the source, let this reach it too: if a contact that has already happened can be opened again, can I be returned without strengthening the loop by the act of returning me?"

"I'll record it exactly that way."

Asgar nodded even though Mirena could not see.

He stepped outside, removed the suit, and handed it to the low machine waiting at the edge of the shelter. Then he returned to the cabin, picked up the receiver, and told Mirena he was ready. He put it back on the cradle and made sure the cord lay freely along the wall.

He set the bag between his boots and laid the stick along the wall.

Asgar loosened the fingers gripping the doorframe and closed the door.
