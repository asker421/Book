Chapter 27. The Right to Air

Mirena would not let him open the door.

Asgar stood with the receiver to his ear and looked out at pale ground split by dark fissures.

The air above it shimmered.

After two years in which he had learned to read weather from the way grass lay at the entrance to his shelter, one glance was enough.

There was nothing outside he could live in.

“About sixty degrees Celsius,” Mirena said.

“Not enough oxygen.

“Strong ultraviolet radiation.”

“People?”

“Not established.

“There are signs of operating infrastructure.”

She gave him the year later.

Seven hundred three million one hundred eighteen thousand four hundred two.

Asgar repeated the beginning of the number in his head and abandoned the rest.

A low machine approached from a dark structure that nearly blended into the fissures.

It stopped several steps away.

It did not approach the door.

Instead it circled the booth in a broad arc, paused where one red upright met the ground, then returned to its former position.

Symbols lit on its body.

Then something outside began speaking.

The first sentence meant nothing to Asgar.

A second followed in another language.

Then a third.

The pauses between them were equal.

In one of them he recognized English.

He leaned toward the glass.

“Russian.

“I speak Russian.”

A symbol on the machine trembled in time with his voice.

The sequence stopped.

“More,” the machine said.

The syllables were misplaced, but the word was Russian.

Asgar gave his name, said how long he had been inside the booth, and where he had come from.

The machine did not interrupt.

When it spoke again, the syllables were spaced normally.

“That is better.

“Now listen carefully:

do not open the door.”

“Why?”

“Because outside you will die within minutes, and I will not have enough time to help.

“Give me a little time.

“I will prepare an exit for you.”

Asgar relayed that to Mirena and sat on the bag.

For the first time in a very long while, nothing was required of him.

---

An opaque shelter unfolded in front of the door.

Its edges sealed to the ground without touching the red uprights.

Asgar had asked about that first, while he was still behind the glass.

Air and cooling were supplied inside.

Only then did Mirena permit him to open the door.

A protective suit lay on a stand.

It had been assembled to the dimensions measured through the glass while the shelter deployed.

Asgar asked how long the cooling would last and whether the suit would continue functioning if the link to the node failed.

He listened to the answer.

Then put it on himself, refusing to let a manipulator move near his face.

The neck seal did not seat properly the first time.

He stopped.

Relaxed his hands.

Started over.

When the cooling engaged, he simply stood for several seconds.

“Why are you doing all this for me?” he asked.

“I keep living things alive here.

“You’re alive.

“That is enough.

“No further explanation is necessary.”

“Where are we going?”

“To the house.

“There’s a room where you can breathe.

“Three hundred meters.

“I’ll go ahead.”

The receiver remained.

Asgar went back into the booth, lifted it, and told Mirena he was leaving.

Three hundred meters.

Building.

Breathable room inside.

“I can’t take the cord with me.”

“I know.”

Asgar looked through the glass at dry ground split by black fissures.

“If the booth isn’t here when I come back…”

“Wait wherever there is air.”

“I know.”

He put the receiver back, checked that the cord hung freely, and sealed the helmet.

Asgar stepped out and took several steps through the shelter.

The sound behind him changed.

He had made it perhaps five paces.

The shelter walls were withdrawing into the ground, folding inward.

Nothing remained beneath them.

Four small holes in the dry surface marked where the uprights had stood.

A clean rectangular imprint showed the threshold.

Asgar stood until the final wall folded away.

“I watched it with every instrument I have,” the node said.

“And I understood none of the transition.”

“It’s always like that.”

He turned toward the building.

The three hundred meters took time.

The dry surface crunched under his soles.

The low machine moved ahead.

His own breathing sounded too close inside the helmet.

At one uneven patch, Asgar stopped and tested the ground with his left foot.

A fissure crossed the way.

“Crack,” he said aloud.

For two years there had been no one to talk to.

The habit remained.

He went around it.

---

Inside the building, there was a room for a human being.

Doors sealed one after another.

The node said the atmosphere was ready and he could remove the protection.

Asgar stayed in the helmet a little longer, looking at the bench against the wall.

Then he removed the helmet and gloves.

Set them beside him.

Sat down.

Not on dirt.

Not on a rolled jacket.

Not on a rock he had to test with one hand first.

A bench.

Level.

Smooth.

He shifted to make room for his legs and caught himself looking for something to wedge under one edge.

He kept water close longer than necessary.

Asked how much he had used.

He was told there was plenty in reserve.

He opened the container again.

His clothes were taken for cleaning one piece at a time.

That was his request.

He wanted the rest to remain in the room.

The same low machine collected them:

rolled in.

waited for Asgar to place one item inside.

rolled out.

Each time, Asgar asked whether they would return that exact object and not something similar.

When the jacket came back, he felt along the sleeve, found the familiar worn patch, and put it away without wearing it.

On the first day he asked nothing else.

He ate small portions.

Slept.

Woke at ventilation sounds and each time reached first for the knife.

The room light remained on until he worked out how to dim it.

That night he thought he could have been sitting outside beside the four little holes.

The thought did not last.

The air in the suit had been supplied by the node.

Not by him.

In the morning he asked the node to tell him if anything appeared at the site.

“I’ll be watching anyway,” the node said.

“But since you asked, I’ll say it aloud.”

---

On the second day, the node told him something had been left for him.

Asgar was sitting on the edge of the sleeping platform, pulling on one boot.

“There’s a package in my engineering archive.

“It arrived long before I existed.

“It was passed from hand to hand until there were no hands left and only I remained.

“It is to be opened under one condition:

if an object appears matching the event of August 16, 2014 and preserving an autonomous return circuit.

“Your booth matches.”

“You were waiting for me?”

“Not you.

“Your name is not in the package.

“No date of birth.

“No family.

“Only an object description.”

He asked to see the condition.

It was exactly as the node had said.

“Who wrote it?”

“People who studied similar phenomena many generations after you.

“They did not build your booth.

“I know their work.

“There is nothing like it there.

“They studied the trace it left.”

Asgar tightened the lace and straightened.

For two years the river, the fire, and his own mistakes had answered him.

Here he could simply ask.

“I don’t need their whole history.

“One question.

“Can I choose where I come out?”

“No.

“And the package says nothing otherwise.

“Your booth follows its own return route.

“These people did not know how to choose the next stop.”

“Then what did they know?”

The node opened the first diagram.

When the direct return of one of their systems failed, it held an intermediate stable state, rechecked its actual mass, and only then constructed the next segment.

Asgar asked it to show the process again without labels.

“So it couldn’t make it home.

“It caught somewhere stable.

“Checked what it was actually carrying.

“Then went on?”

“Crude.

“But correct.”

“That sounds like mine.”

“It resembles it.

“No more.

“Their installation occupied a large complex and could not operate without external infrastructure.

“I found no evidence that it was related to your booth.”

“Then why did they leave this for it?”

“Because of the second property.”

The node removed the diagram and showed two states of the same installation separated by a long interval.

“Imagine two rooms between which a door once truly existed.

“Later, you cannot place that door anywhere you like.

“But if the system can reconstruct its own side of the old contact precisely enough, the previously existing opening may serve as a boundary condition again.”

Asgar frowned.

“I don’t understand.

“Go back to the same date?”

“Not choose a date.

“Use an old physical contact as an address.

“Only one that truly existed in the history of that system.

“You cannot create a new address that way.”

“And they did this?”

“With their own installations, yes.

“They reopened previously registered contacts.

“They did not guarantee transfer of a person.”

Asgar looked again at the two system states.

“What did they call it?”

“Historical addressing.”

He repeated the words and grimaced.

“Say it normally.”

“A door can be attempted again only where that door once really existed.”

That was clearer.

“That’s why August sixteenth is in the package?”

“That is why the authors considered the event of August 16 an appropriate class of problem.

“It does not prove that your source can reopen that contact.”

Asgar remained seated, looking at the smooth floor.

Until that moment, he had imagined only one direction:

next stop.

another stop.

source somewhere ahead.

Now another possibility existed.

Not that the booth would turn around.

That somewhere ahead there might be a way to open a door that had already been open.

---

On the third day, Asgar reached the end of the package.

The last item was not a calculation or a diagram.

It was a list of questions the authors had not answered.

Who decides for a person who accidentally ends up inside a machine?

How much knowledge can one era drop into another?

Asgar read the third question twice.

What should be done if the loop already exists?

Below it was a single case.

A researcher received the result of an experiment he had not yet performed.

The entire transmission was one line.

Afterward, he selected the next experiment without repeating the first.

A later report cited the earlier record.

“Where is the measurement itself?” Asgar asked.

“No one made it.

“The result is confirmed only by the message in which it appears.”

Asgar opened the citation from the later report.

It led back to the same line.

“So they never knew where the correct answer came from.”

“Exactly.

“They knew only that the answer appeared before its cause.”

With Olmer, Asgar had followed links from document to document, always expecting to find a basis behind the next reference.

Here, the basis closed on itself.

Asgar closed the link and looked at the unanswered questions.

“If an old door like that is ever opened again, can someone on this side do something there?”

“If the contact is physical, yes.

“Any action at the boundary becomes a real event.

“But the package authors could not predict what that action would change afterward.

“They did not guarantee transfer of a person to the other side.”

“So you can reach an old moment and interfere.

“No one knows what follows.”

“Correct.”

The first idea was too simple.

If somewhere ahead they could truly reopen contact with the beginning of his journey, he could try to change the reason the journey ever continued.

Katya would not spend twenty-five years waiting.

The children would not grow up without him.

And everything that had brought this Asgar to the chance to intervene had begun there too.

“No,” he said.

“What exactly?”

“The simplest version.

“Get there earlier and try to cancel the whole thing from the beginning.”

“The package does not prohibit intervention.”

“I’m not saying it prohibits it.

“I’m saying I don’t know what I’d break.”

He looked again at the question about the loop.

“The address isn’t the solution.”

“Agreed.”

“Then the question is different.”

“What question?”

Asgar did not formulate it immediately.

“If an old contact can ever be opened again…

can I be returned without forcing the loop to feed itself through the act of returning me?”

The node searched the remainder of the package.

“There is no answer to that question.”

“Then that is the question that has to reach the source.”

---

During the remaining days, he returned to the package and asked the same things in different ways.

The wording of the answers changed.

Their meaning did not.

Every morning he asked first about the site.

Every morning the node said the same four holes remained.

On the fifth day, Asgar asked to see it and watched the empty ground for a long time from a high point.

On the sixth, he did not ask.

---

On the eighth day, the booth returned.

The node told him before he had time to ask.

While Asgar dressed, the shelter deployed over the site for a second time.

He took out his old things and realized he had already become unaccustomed to them in eight days.

Everything had been cleaned, but the seams of the bag still held the smell of the shelter:

smoke.

fat.

river damp.

Before, he had never noticed it because he breathed the same smell.

Asgar pressed the fabric to his face.

Then lowered it.

He checked the protective suit himself with prompts from the node.

At the fissure on the open ground he stopped even though he knew where it was.

He covered the three hundred meters faster than before.

Inside the shelter he removed the helmet, entered the booth, and lifted the receiver.

“I’m here.

“It’s back.”

“I can see you,” Mirena said.

“Eight days.”

He held the receiver and listened longer than necessary.

For eight days, the node had been the only thing that spoke to him.

“There was an old engineering package here,” he said.

“Not about me.

“About systems like this.”

“What was in it?”

“They could reopen only a contact that had already existed for their installation.

“Not choose a date.

“Use an old door as an address.”

Mirena answered immediately.

“That is not in the documentation available to me.”

“I know.

“My first thought was simple:

if someone eventually opens a contact like that, try to interfere earlier so none of this journey ever happens.”

“And?”

“And nobody knows what that breaks.”

He pressed the receiver harder to his ear.

“Send the source a different question.

“If an already-existing contact can be reopened, can I be returned without strengthening the loop through the return itself?”

“I’ll send it exactly that way.”

Asgar nodded though Mirena could not see him.

He stepped outside, removed the suit, and handed it to the low machine waiting at the edge of the shelter.

Then he returned to the booth, lifted the receiver, and told Mirena he was ready.

He replaced it and made sure the cord lay freely against the wall.

He set the bag between his boots.

Placed the walking stick along the side so it would not interfere with the door.

Asgar relaxed the fingers gripping the frame.

Then closed the door.
