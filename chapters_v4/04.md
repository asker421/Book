Chapter 4. Somewhere Else

On the first evening home, the younger son climbed onto Asgar's lap and stayed there for a long time. He sat sideways, got in the way while Asgar ate, reached across the table for bread even though there was bread in front of him too. Asgar held him around the stomach and ate with his free hand. The older boy was telling them something about the days spent with relatives, losing his place, correcting his brother. Katya listened from the kitchen until Asgar pulled a chair out for her.

No one asked about the booth during dinner.

Later the younger boy fell asleep leaning against his side. Asgar was afraid to move. His leg went numb, it grew hot beneath the child's head, but these were understandable discomforts. He stayed where he was until Katya came over to take their son.

"I'll do it," Asgar said.

He carried the child to bed, eased his arm out from under him, and stood there a while longer. When he came back, Katya was clearing the table. He took the plates from her, carried them to the sink, and turned on the water.

"What happens tomorrow?" she asked.

"I'll call Kerimov."

"And then?"

Asgar watched the water push crumbs toward the edge of the plate.

"I don't know."

Katya set a glass beside him.

"All right. Then we'll decide tomorrow."

On Thursday, he called Rauf several times. There was no news about the man. The investigator asked him to remember the clothes, the gait, the hands again; Asgar answered more and more cautiously. After the images he had been shown, he could no longer be sure where each detail had come from.

At home, he caught himself listening for the elevator behind the wall. In the evening, he stood under the shower for a long time, repeatedly looking at the corner where the ceiling met the tile. Katya knocked on the door: the children were waiting for him to come out. He turned off the water.

By noon on Friday, August twenty-second, the refrigerator had run out of the things no one normally noticed while they were there. Katya listed them from the kitchen, and Asgar entered them into his phone.

"And bread. Just look at which one you pick."

"I still know how to buy bread."

"Then get two."

He went over to kiss her before leaving. She kept hold of his hand, reread the list on the screen, added milk, and let him go.

The elevator went down to the underground parking garage.

Asgar stepped out and stopped immediately.

In the far row, about thirty meters away, stood the red booth.

He backed toward the elevator. The doors were already closing; Asgar put out a hand and made them open again. The usual light was on inside. He could step in and press the button for his floor. He kept his palm by the sensor and looked across the garage.

The red wall cut through a concrete column. Near the floor, a yellowish parking line ran up to the body and disappeared beneath it. A car stood nearby; the booth occupied a space where there was not enough room for it.

Asgar lowered his hand. The elevator doors closed behind him.

He took out his mobile and took a picture. He enlarged the photo on the screen. The red stayed red. The column passed through the rear section of the body.

To the left, halfway to the booth, was a door to the stairs. Asgar shifted his gaze to it, then to the lane toward the exit. No one was coming yet. Ventilation hummed beneath the ceiling.

He called Rauf.

"It's here."

"What is?"

"The booth. Downstairs at my place, in the parking garage. I'm sending you a photo now."

He sent the picture while staying on the line.

Rauf was silent for several seconds.

"Are you near it?"

"By the elevator. About thirty meters away."

"Stay there. I'll send people. If anyone approaches, warn them not to touch it."

Asgar looked at the red door. It was closed.

A ring came from inside.

He lowered the mobile and listened. The telephone rang in short bursts; the concrete threw the sound back from every direction. Last time, the woman on that line had known the door was locked.

"Asgar?" Rauf called.

"The phone inside is ringing."

"Don't go in."

"I'm not going to."

He ended the call. He messaged Katya: "I'm in the parking garage. The booth appeared again. I told Kerimov. Don't come down, I'll call you now."

The phone rang in his hand immediately.

"Where are you standing?" Katya asked.

"Far away from it. I'm outside, by the elevator."

"Come upstairs."

He looked at the closed doors behind him.

"Wait. Don't leave the apartment. I want to hear what they say."

"You said you're standing far away."

"I'll pick up the receiver through the doorway. I won't go inside."

"Asgar..."

"I understand. I won't go in. I'll call right away."

He ended the call and put the mobile in his pocket before Katya could object, then stayed where he was. The ringing continued. He could wait for Rauf. The booth might disappear before then. Asgar stood by the elevator for several seconds without moving, unable to make himself shorten those thirty meters.

Asgar took a few steps along the wall toward the stairwell. From there, the booth door was visible from the side. The car prevented it from opening wide; the black telephone hung right beside the opening. He could reach the receiver without crossing the threshold.

He approached in an arc, keeping the path back clear. He stopped by the red wall. Studied the handle, the gap between the door and the frame, the floor at the base. The ringing struck from very close now.

Asgar pulled the door. It opened about the width of a palm and touched the car parked beside it. He held it with his left hand and slipped his right through the gap. His fingers slid over the smooth side of the receiver and hooked beneath it.

Once he had lifted the receiver, he let go of the door. It swung back and stopped against the cord, leaving a narrow gap. Asgar stepped away; the cord went taut before he could complete even one full step. He stopped with his arm extended, then moved close enough to bring the receiver to his ear. Both feet remained on the concrete of the parking garage.

"I'm here. Outside. I'm not going in."

"Asgar?"

The same woman's voice.

"Yes. Do you know how long I was gone? Four days. You said there was a malfunction. They looked for me for four days."

"We weren't able to complete the process after the first exit."

"What process? I came home."

"You exited the cabin. The connection with you remained. That's why it appeared again."

Asgar turned his head toward the column. Through the red glass he could see its gray surface, at an impossible depth.

"You sent it here?"

"No. We don't choose the location."

"But you knew it would appear?"

"We expected it. We didn't know exactly when or where."

"And you couldn't call me either?"

"Not on your mobile. Only through this device."

He tightened his grip on the receiver. Its weight was slowly tiring his wrist; he had to hold his elbow out in front so he would not pull the cord any tighter.

"What do you need from me?"

"To continue the transition, you'll have to go inside. But before—"

"No."

For a second, only background noise remained in the receiver.

Asgar looked at the threshold. Part of his attention kept drifting toward it, as though the moment he stopped paying attention his feet would end up on the other side by themselves.

"I went inside once. I lost four days. If I go in now, when do I come out?"

"I don't know."

"Then why are you suggesting it?"

"Because the process isn't finished. As long as the connection holds, the cabin may keep appearing near you."

"And going inside will end it?"

A quiet knock sounded on the line.

"I don't know. It might. It might not."

Asgar stepped back another half-step. The cord jerked the receiver; he caught it with both hands. The door remained slightly open, the cable lying in the gap.

"So I'll go in again, disappear, and then you'll tell me you still haven't finished?"

"Yes. That can happen."

He pulled the receiver away from his ear and looked at it. He wanted to smash it against the red wall. He brought it back to his face.

"Then shut the whole thing down. I'm not going anywhere."

"We tried. It didn't work."

"What happened?"

"You managed to get out. The connection remained."

For several seconds, Asgar listened to the background. Someone near the woman was speaking, but he could not make out the words. There were other people there. What they were doing could not be determined from the background noise alone. He was standing in the parking garage of his own home with one arm stretched toward a telephone booth.

"What if I refuse?"

"It may appear again."

"Today? Tomorrow? My children are upstairs. Could it appear inside the apartment?"

"It could. We don't know exactly where it will appear next."

Asgar looked up at the parking garage ceiling. Above him were floors, apartments, the kitchen where Katya had dictated the shopping list.

"Is it dangerous to be near it?"

"When the boundary changes—yes. Right now the connection is stable. We don't know yet how long it will hold."

"What exactly is dangerous? Electric shock? Crushing?"

"The body has already intersected your column. If a person is at the boundary, I don't know what will happen to them. Move away when the state begins to change."

Asgar looked at the column. The red plane entered the concrete with no gap. He tried to imagine a person there instead and stepped back. The receiver twisted in his hand; the cord stopped his arm.

"If you're moving away, let go of the receiver," the woman said.

He forced himself to stay where he was.

"Can you tell where I'm standing?"

"No. I can't see you."

"I'm outside. Still outside."

"Good."

Asgar tilted his head and listened. He waited for her to tell him to go inside again, to say there was no other way. The woman asked:

"Are you prepared not to enter until the next appearance?"

"Yes. But somewhere farther from home."

"Then leave this area when the connection starts to break down."

"When will it come back?"

"Right now the estimate is about forty-seven hours."

He repeated the number to himself. Almost two days. Sunday.

"Could it be earlier?"

"It could."

"And you'll know about it and just wait until I pick up the receiver again?"

"Yes. We have no other channel to you."

Asgar rolled one shoulder. The mobile vibrated in his pocket. He looked down but did not reach for it and waited until it stopped ringing.

"You said you can't tell me the date I'll come out. Where does this thing even lead?"

"Wherever we can keep the connection stable. We don't set either the place or the date."

Asgar looked at the row of cars. One had a folded sunshade under the windshield. Another had a child's toy hanging inside.

"Time," he repeated. "You're saying these four days..."

"Four days really did pass outside."

He slowly ran his tongue over his dry lips. The calendar, Katya's signature, the camera recordings had already been in front of him. Still, hearing it from the person at the other end was harder.

"Send me back to Saturday."

"I can't."

"I don't need another year. I want the day I went in."

"I hear you. I still can't choose a date."

He went silent. Ventilation hummed overhead; somewhere on the upper level, a car drove past.

"And the man?" Asgar asked. "The one who came out before me. Did you find him?"

"No."

"He was inside with you. Before me."

"I don't know who he is or how he got inside."

Asgar closed his eyes. He could not get a single answer from her that would let him simply go back to the elevator.

"The connection is losing stability," the woman said.

He opened his eyes.

The edge of the red panel by the column began to double. Asgar immediately let go of the receiver. It hung from the cord, struck the body, and swung in the opening; the woman's thin voice was still coming from it.

He walked backward without turning his back on the booth. After passing the stairs, he sped up. By the elevator, he stopped and took out his mobile.

The red wall trembled. For an instant, the car showed through it. Then the booth was gone.

Asgar held the phone in front of him for several more seconds. The column stood where it had before. So did the car.

He called Katya.

"I didn't go in."

She exhaled loudly at the other end.

"Where are you now?"

"By the elevator. It disappeared."

"Come upstairs. I'm waiting for you."

He looked at the call button.

"Katya, I can't go home yet."

Rauf arrived after Asgar had already gone up the ramp and out onto the street. Parking security blocked access to the row after his report; Asgar waited until an employee put up a barrier, then walked away from the building. He stopped in an open stretch along the drive where he could see anyone approaching.

The investigator got out of his car and walked toward him.

"What did you do before it disappeared?"

"Picked up the receiver from outside."

"I told you to wait."

"It disappeared a few minutes later. We'd have been left with a photograph."

Rauf stopped in front of him.

"And now we have another conversation only you heard."

"I'm not hiding anything."

"I'm not saying you are. I've already had a case where details spread too early. The next day half the witnesses suddenly 'remembered' what they'd read. I spent a month cleaning up that mess afterward. That's why I'm keeping the recordings and access with me for now."

Asgar took out his mobile.

"I have a photo. Security is checking the cameras. I need somewhere with no people nearby."

He told him about the repeated appearances, the boundary of the body, the forty-seven hours. Rauf clarified the wording. Asgar answered more sharply each time, but repeated it. When he reached the risk at the boundary, the investigator asked him to go over the woman's exact words again.

"It already appeared here," Asgar said. "By my home. How much more confirmation do you need before I can stop standing under my children's windows?"

"We'll find a place. That's enough. But you won't be the one deciding who goes in there and when."

"Just not a hospital room or a hotel. There are people on the other side of the wall."

"I understand."

Asgar lowered the phone. Katya was calling again.

"Is she coming to you?" Rauf asked.

"She's at home. I told her not to come down."

"Then talk to her. I'll deal with the site for now."

He walked away toward the car.

Asgar answered.

"Have you decided where you'll stay?"

"Kerimov is looking for a place. Somewhere with no people nearby."

"And how long will you be there?"

"Until Sunday, probably. They're expecting the next appearance in about forty-seven hours."

"Who is expecting it?"

"The woman on the phone."

"The one after whom you were gone for four days?"

"Yes."

He could hear the children in the apartment. Someone asked whether Dad had bought the bread. Katya answered away from the phone. Then a door creaked, and the children's voices became quieter.

"I'm not asking you to go near that booth," she said. "I want to understand what you're going to do. You said: I'll pick up the receiver and call you. Now you're saying you aren't coming home."

"It may appear again. I saw the wall pass through the column. If someone is standing where it—"

"I understand why you're afraid. Why have you already decided everything while I'm waiting by the door?"

Asgar ran a hand over his face.

"Because we can't wait for it to appear in our kitchen."

"You don't know that it'll appear in the kitchen."

"I don't. Want to test it?"

Katya went silent.

He had time to realize how convenient that was: after a question like that, all she could do was agree. Asgar turned away from the building, which was still visible between the cars.

"Sorry. I said that wrong."

"That's exactly what you said."

"I'm afraid to go upstairs. That's it. I don't want you standing nearby if it comes back. I don't know what the right thing is."

There was a creak on the line. He wanted to be there beside her instead of guessing from sounds what she was doing.

"What should I bring you?" she asked.

"I don't want you coming close."

"Asgar. Charger. Clothes. What do you need?"

He looked over at Rauf. The man was still talking on the phone beside the car.

"Charger. And something to change into. Give it to Kerimov, all right?"

"All right."

She did not hang up.

Asgar waited, listening to her breathing. They had to tell the children. He had to decide how to explain a second departure within a few days. That very morning he had been writing down groceries, and it had seemed like the right sort of thing for a man who had come home to be doing.

"I didn't go inside," he repeated.

"I heard you."

"And I'm not going to now."

"If you decide otherwise, tell me first. Not when you're already at the door."

He looked at the building.

"I will."

Rauf came back and named a place where Asgar could temporarily stay away from other people. They had to wait until it was prepared. Asgar agreed to go.

By the car, he messaged Katya the address. Added: "Phone is on."

Then he opened the shopping list. Milk, bread, everything else was still there. Asgar sent it to Katya and put the mobile in his pocket.
