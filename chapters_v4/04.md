Chapter 4. A Place That Isn't Yours

On his first evening home, his younger son climbed onto Asgar's lap and stayed there for a long time. He sat sideways, got in the way while Asgar ate, reached across the table for bread even though there was bread in front of him too. Asgar held him around the stomach and ate with his free hand. The older boy was telling them something about the days spent with relatives, getting mixed up, correcting his brother. Katya listened from the kitchen until Asgar pulled out a chair for her.

No one asked about the booth over dinner.

Later, the younger boy fell asleep leaning against his side. Asgar was afraid to move. His leg went numb, it grew hot under the child's head, but these were understandable discomforts. He stayed where he was until Katya came to take their son.

"I'll do it," Asgar said.

He carried the boy to bed, pulled his arm out from under him, and stood there a little longer. When he came back, Katya was clearing the table. He took the plates from her, carried them to the sink, and turned on the water.

"What happens tomorrow?" she asked.

"I'll call Kerimov."

"And after that?"

Asgar watched the water push crumbs toward the edge of the plate.

"I don't know."

Katya set a glass beside him.

"All right. Then we'll decide tomorrow."

On Thursday, he called Rauf several times. There was no news about the man. The investigator asked him again to remember the clothes, the gait, the hands; Asgar answered more and more carefully. After the frames they had shown him, he could no longer be sure where each detail had come from.

At home he caught himself listening for the elevator behind the wall. In the evening he stood under the shower for a long time, checking the corner between the ceiling and the tile with his eyes. Katya knocked on the door: the children were waiting for him to come out. He turned off the water.

By noon on Friday, August twenty-second, the refrigerator had run out of the things no one normally noticed while they were there. Katya called items out from the kitchen, and Asgar typed them into his phone.

"And bread. Just look at which one you take."

"I still know how to buy bread."

"Then get two."

He went over to kiss her before leaving. She caught him by the hand, read the list on the screen to the end, added milk, and let him go.

The elevator descended to the underground parking garage.

Asgar stepped out and stopped at once.

In the far row, about thirty meters away, stood a red booth.

He backed toward the elevator. The doors were already closing; Asgar put out a hand and forced them open again. Inside, the normal light was on. He could step in, press the button for his floor. He kept his palm by the sensor and looked across the garage.

The red wall cut through a concrete column. At floor level, a yellowish lane marking ran up to the booth and disappeared beneath it. A car stood beside it; the booth occupied a gap too narrow to contain it.

Asgar lowered his hand. The elevator doors closed behind him.

He took out his mobile and took a picture. Zoomed in on it on the screen. The red stayed red. The column passed through the rear section of the booth.

On the left, halfway to the booth, was the stairwell door. Asgar looked at it, then at the lane leading to the exit. No one was coming yet. Ventilation hummed overhead.

He called Rauf.

"It's here."

"What is?"

"The booth. Down here in my parking garage. I'm sending you a photo now."

He sent the image while staying on the line.

Rauf was silent for several seconds.

"Are you near it?"

"By the elevator. About thirty meters away."

"Stay there. I'll send people. If anyone comes near, warn them not to touch it."

Asgar looked at the red door. It was closed.

A telephone rang inside.

He lowered the mobile and listened. The ringing came in short bursts; the concrete threw the sound back from every direction. Last time, the woman on that line had known the door was locked.

"Asgar?" Rauf called.

"The phone inside is ringing."

"Don't go in."

"I'm not going to."

He ended the call. Typed Katya a message: "I'm in the garage. The booth appeared again. Told Kerimov. Don't come down, I'll call now."

The phone rang in his hand immediately.

"Where are you standing?" Katya asked.

"Far from it. Outside, by the elevator."

"Come upstairs."

He looked at the closed doors behind him.

"Wait. Don't leave the apartment. I want to hear what they say."

"You said you're far from it."

"I'll pick up the receiver through the door. I won't go inside."

"Asgar..."

"I understand. I won't go in. I'll call right away."

He ended the call and put the mobile in his pocket before Katya could object, then stayed where he was. The ringing continued. He could wait for Rauf. The booth might disappear first. For several seconds Asgar did not move from the elevator, unable to make himself cut those thirty meters shorter.

Asgar took several steps along the wall toward the stairs. From there, the booth door was visible from the side. The car prevented it from opening wide; the black telephone hung right by the opening. He could reach the receiver without crossing the threshold.

He moved in an arc, keeping a clear route back. He stopped by the red wall. Examined the handle, the gap between the door and frame, the floor at the base. The ringing struck from very close now.

Asgar pulled the door. It opened about the width of a palm and touched the car beside it. He held it with his left hand and pushed his right through the gap. His fingers slid over the smooth side of the receiver, hooked under it.

Once he had lifted the receiver, he let go of the door. It swung back and stopped against the cord, leaving a narrow gap. Asgar stepped back; the cord went taut before he could move a full step away. He stopped with his arm extended, then moved close enough to bring the receiver to his ear. Both feet remained on the concrete of the garage.

"I'm here. Outside. I'm not going in."

"Asgar?"

The same woman's voice.

"Yes. Do you know how long I was gone? Four days. You said there was a malfunction. They looked for me for four days."

"We weren't able to complete the process during the first exit."

"What process? I went home."

"You exited the cabin. The connection with you remained. That's why it appeared again."

Asgar turned his head toward the column. Through the red glass, its gray surface was visible at an impossible depth.

"Did you send it here?"

"No. We don't choose the location."

"But you knew it would appear?"

"We expected it. We didn't know when or where."

"And you couldn't call me either?"

"Not on your mobile. Only through this device."

He tightened his grip on the receiver. His wrist was gradually tiring from the weight; he had to keep his elbow forward so as not to pull the cord any tighter.

"What do you need from me?"

"To continue the transition, you'll have to go inside. But first..."

"No."

For a second there was only background noise in the receiver.

Asgar looked at the threshold. Part of his attention kept drifting there, as though the moment he looked away his feet would end up on the other side by themselves.

"I went in once. I lost four days. If I go in now, when will I come out?"

"I don't know."

"Then why are you suggesting it?"

"Because the process isn't finished. As long as the connection holds, the cabin may keep appearing near you."

"And if I go in, that ends it?"

A quiet knock sounded on the line.

"I don't know. It may. It may not."

Asgar stepped back another half-step. The cord jerked the receiver; he caught it with both hands. The door remained slightly open, the cable lying in the gap.

"So I go in again, disappear, and then you tell me you still haven't finished?"

"Yes. That can happen."

He moved the receiver away from his ear and looked at it. He wanted to smash it against the red wall. He brought it back to his face.

"Then shut everything down. I'm not going anywhere."

"We tried. It didn't work."

"What happened?"

"You were able to get out. The connection remained."

For several seconds Asgar listened to the background. People were talking somewhere near the woman, but he could not make out the words. There were other people there. What they were doing could not be understood from the noise alone. He stood in the parking garage of his own home with one arm stretched toward a telephone booth.

"What if I refuse?"

"It may keep appearing."

"Today? Tomorrow? My children are upstairs. Could it appear inside the apartment?"

"It could. We don't know where exactly it will appear next."

Asgar looked up at the garage ceiling. Above him were floors, apartments, the kitchen where Katya had dictated the shopping list.

"Is it dangerous to be near it?"

"When the boundary changes—yes. Right now the connection is stable. We don't know how long it will hold."

"What exactly is dangerous? Electric shock? Crushing?"

"The body has already entered your column. If a person is on the boundary, I don't know what will happen to them. Move away when the state starts changing."

Asgar looked at the column. The red plane entered the concrete with no gap. He tried to imagine a person in its place and stepped back. The receiver twisted in his hand; the cord stopped his arm.

"If you're moving away, let go of the receiver," the woman said.

He forced himself to stay where he was.

"Can you tell where I'm standing?"

"No. I can't see you."

"I'm outside. Still outside."

"Good."

Asgar tilted his head, listening. He waited for her to tell him to go in again, to say there was no other way. The woman asked:

"Are you prepared not to enter until the next appearance?"

"Yes. But somewhere farther from home."

"Then leave this place when the connection starts to break down."

"When will it come back?"

"The current estimate is about forty-seven hours."

He repeated the number to himself. Almost two days. Sunday.

"Could it be sooner?"

"It could."

"And you'll know about it and wait until I pick up the receiver again?"

"Yes. We have no other channel to you."

Asgar rolled one shoulder. His mobile vibrated in his pocket. He looked down but did not reach for it and waited until the ringing stopped.

"You said you can't name the exit date. Where does this thing even lead?"

"Wherever we can maintain the connection. We don't choose either the place or the date."

Asgar looked at the row of cars. One had a folded sunshade beneath the windshield. Another had a child's toy hanging inside.

"Time," he repeated. "You're saying those four days..."

"Four days really did pass outside."

He slowly ran his tongue over dry lips. The calendar, Katya's signature, the camera recordings had already been in front of him. Even so, hearing it from the person at the other end was harder.

"Send me back to Saturday."

"I can't."

"I don't need another year. I want the day I went in."

"I hear you. I still can't choose the date."

He fell silent. Ventilation hummed overhead; somewhere on the upper level a car passed.

"And the man?" Asgar asked. "The one who came out before me. Did you find him?"

"No."

"He was inside with you. Before me."

"I don't know who he is or how he got inside."

Asgar closed his eyes. He could not get a single answer from her that would let him simply go back to the elevator.

"The connection is losing stability," the woman said.

He opened his eyes.

The edge of the red panel by the column began to split in two. Asgar immediately let go of the receiver. It hung from the cord, struck the body of the booth, and swung in the doorway; her thin voice was still coming from it.

He walked backward, never turning his back on the booth. Once past the stairs, he sped up. At the elevator he stopped and took out his mobile.

The red wall trembled. For an instant the car showed through it. Then the booth was gone.

Asgar held the phone in front of himself for several more seconds. The column was still in the same place. So was the car.

He called Katya.

"I didn't go in."

She exhaled loudly at the other end.

"Where are you now?"

"By the elevator. It's gone."

"Come upstairs. I'm waiting."

He looked at the call button.

"Katya, I can't go home yet."

Rauf arrived after Asgar had already walked up the ramp and outside. Parking security had blocked access to the row after his report; Asgar waited until an employee put up a barrier, then left the building on foot. He stopped by an open stretch along the driveway where he could see people approaching.

The investigator got out of his car and walked toward him.

"What did you do before it disappeared?"

"I picked up the receiver from outside."

"I told you to wait."

"It disappeared a few minutes later. We'd have been left with a photograph."

Rauf stopped in front of him.

"And now we have another conversation only you heard."

"I'm not hiding anything."

"I'm not saying you are. I've already had a case where details got out too early. The next day half the witnesses suddenly 'remembered' what they'd read. It took me a month to clean up that mess. That's why I'm keeping the recordings and access under my control for now."

Asgar took out his mobile.

"I have the photo. Security is checking the cameras. I need somewhere with no people nearby."

He told him about the repeated appearances, the boundary of the body, the forty-seven hours. Rauf clarified the wording. Asgar answered more sharply each time but repeated it. When he reached the danger at the boundary, the investigator asked him to go over the woman's exact words again.

"It already appeared here," Asgar said. "Beside my home. How much more confirmation do you need before I can stop standing under my children's windows?"

"We'll find a place. That's enough. But you don't get to decide who goes in there and when."

"Just not a hospital room or a hotel. There are people through the walls."

"I understand."

Asgar lowered the phone. Katya was calling again.

"Has she come to you?" Rauf asked.

"She's home. I told her not to come down."

"Then talk to her. I'll deal with finding a site."

He walked back toward the car.

Asgar answered.

"Have you decided where you'll stay?"

"Kerimov is looking for a place. Somewhere with no one nearby."

"And how long will you be there?"

"Until Sunday, probably. They expect the next appearance in about forty-seven hours."

"Who expects it?"

"The woman on the phone."

"The one after whom you were gone for four days?"

"Yes."

He could hear the children in the apartment. Someone asked whether Dad had bought the bread. Katya answered away from the phone. Then a door creaked, and the children's voices grew quieter.

"I'm not asking you to go near that booth," she said. "I want to understand what you're planning to do. You said you'd pick up the receiver and call me. Now you're saying you aren't coming home."

"It may appear again. I saw the wall pass through a column. If someone happens to be where it..."

"I understand why you're afraid. Why have you already decided everything while I'm waiting at the door?"

Asgar ran a hand over his face.

"Because we can't wait for it to appear in our kitchen."

"You don't know that it'll appear in the kitchen."

"I don't. Do we want to test it?"

Katya fell silent.

He immediately felt how convenient that question had been: after it, she had little choice but to agree. Asgar turned away from the house, still visible between the cars.

"Sorry. I said that wrong."

"That's exactly what you said."

"I'm afraid to go upstairs. That's what it is. I don't want you standing nearby if it comes back. I don't know what's right."

There was a creak on the line. He wanted to be there beside her, not guessing from sounds what she was doing.

"What should I bring you?" she asked.

"I don't want you coming close."

"Asgar. Charger. Clothes. What do you need?"

He looked over at Rauf. The investigator was still talking on the phone by his car.

"A charger. And something to change into. Give it to Kerimov, all right?"

"All right."

She did not hang up.

Asgar waited, listening to her breathing. They had to tell the children. They had to decide how to explain a second departure within a few days. That morning he had been writing down groceries, and it had seemed like an appropriate thing for a man who had come home to do.

"I didn't go inside," he repeated.

"I heard you."

"And I'm not planning to now."

"If you change your mind, tell me first. Not when you're already at the door."

He looked at the house.

"I will."

Rauf came back and named a place where Asgar could temporarily stay away from other people. They needed to wait until it was prepared. Asgar agreed to go.

By the car, he sent Katya the address. Added: "Phone is on."

Then he opened the shopping list. Milk, bread, everything else was still there. Asgar sent it to Katya and put the mobile back in his pocket.
