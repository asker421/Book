Chapter 4. Someone Else’s Place

On his first evening home, Asgar’s younger son climbed onto his lap and stayed there for a long time. He sat sideways, made it hard to eat, reached across the table for bread even though there was bread in front of him. Asgar kept one arm around the boy’s stomach and ate with the other hand. His older son talked about the days they had spent with relatives, losing the thread, correcting his brother. Katya listened from the kitchen until Asgar pulled out a chair for her.

No one asked about the booth over dinner.

Later, the younger boy fell asleep leaning against his side. Asgar was afraid to move. His leg went numb, the child’s head grew hot against him, but those were discomforts he understood. He stayed where he was until Katya came to carry their son away.

“I’ve got him,” Asgar said.

He took the boy to bed, slid his arm out from beneath him, and stood beside the bed for a while longer. When he came back, Katya was clearing the table. He took the plates from her, carried them to the sink, and turned on the water.

“What happens tomorrow?” she asked.

“I’ll call Kerimov.”

“And then?”

Asgar watched the water push crumbs toward the rim of a plate.

“I don’t know.”

Katya set a glass beside him.

“Fine. Then we’ll decide tomorrow.”

On Thursday he called Rauf several times. There was no news about the man. The investigator asked him again to remember the clothes, the gait, the hands; Asgar answered more carefully each time. After the footage they had shown him, he could no longer be sure where each detail had come from.

At home he kept catching himself listening to the elevator through the wall. That evening he stood under the shower for a long time, repeatedly checking the corner where the ceiling met the tiles. Katya knocked on the door: the children were waiting for him to come out. He shut off the water.

By noon on Friday, August twenty-second, they had run out of all the groceries no one ever noticed while they were still there. Katya called items from the kitchen while Asgar typed them into his phone.

“And bread. Just look at what kind you’re getting.”

“I still know how to buy bread.”

“Then get two.”

He went over to kiss her before leaving. She held on to his hand, read the list on his screen to the end, added milk, and let him go.

The elevator descended to the underground garage.

Asgar stepped out and stopped immediately.

In the far row, about thirty meters away, stood the red booth.

He backed toward the elevator. The doors were already closing; Asgar put out a hand and forced them open. Ordinary light burned inside. He could step back in, press the button for his floor.

He kept his palm by the sensor and stared across the garage.

A red wall cut through a concrete pillar. At floor level, a yellowish parking stripe ran up to the body of the booth and vanished beneath it. A car stood beside it; the booth occupied a space in which there was not enough room for the booth to exist.

Asgar lowered his hand. The elevator doors closed behind him.

He took out his mobile and photographed it. Zoomed in on the picture right there on the screen. The red remained red. The pillar passed through the rear section of the structure.

Halfway to the booth, on the left, was the stairwell door. Asgar looked at it, then at the lane leading toward the garage exit. No one was coming. Ventilation hummed under the ceiling.

He called Rauf.

“It’s here.”

“What is?”

“The booth. Downstairs in my parking garage. I’m sending you a picture now.”

He sent the image without ending the call.

Rauf was silent for several seconds.

“Are you next to it?”

“By the elevator. About thirty meters away.”

“Stay there. I’m sending people. If anyone comes near it, warn them not to touch it.”

Asgar stared at the red door. It was closed.

A telephone began ringing inside.

He lowered his mobile and listened. The phone rang in short bursts; the concrete threw the sound back from every direction. Last time, that line had connected him to a woman who knew the door was locked.

“Asgar?” Rauf said.

“The phone’s ringing in there.”

“Do not go inside.”

“I’m not planning to.”

He ended the call. Typed Katya a message: *I’m in the garage. The booth is back. Told Kerimov. Don’t come down. I’ll call in a minute.*

The phone rang in his hand at once.

“Where are you standing?” Katya asked.

“Far from it. I’m outside it, by the elevator.”

“Come back upstairs.”

He looked at the closed doors behind him.

“Wait. Don’t leave the apartment. I want to hear what they say.”

“You just said you’re far away.”

“I can take the receiver through the door. I won’t go inside.”

“Asgar…”

“I heard you. I’m not going in. I’ll call you right after.”

He ended the call and put the mobile into his pocket before Katya could object, then stayed where he was.

The ringing continued.

He could wait for Rauf.

The booth could disappear before Rauf arrived.

Asgar stood by the elevator for several seconds without moving, unable to make himself shorten those thirty meters.

Then he took a few steps along the wall toward the stairwell. From there he could see the booth door from the side. The car beside it prevented the door from opening wide; the black telephone hung just inside the opening. It looked possible to reach the receiver without crossing the threshold.

He approached in an arc, keeping his route back clear. At the red wall he stopped. Studied the handle, the gap between door and frame, the floor at the base.

The bell struck again, painfully close.

Asgar pulled the door. It opened about a hand’s width before touching the parked car. He held it with his left hand and slipped his right through the gap. His fingers slid over the smooth side of the receiver, then hooked beneath it.

Once he had lifted it, he let go of the door. The panel started to close and stopped against the cord, leaving a narrow gap. Asgar stepped back; the cord went taut before he had managed a full pace. He stopped with his arm extended, then moved close enough to bring the receiver to his ear.

Both feet remained on the garage concrete.

“I’m here. Outside. I’m not going in.”

“Asgar?”

The same woman’s voice.

“Yes. Do you know how long I was gone? Four days. You said something malfunctioned. They searched for me for four days.”

“We weren’t able to complete the process during your first exit.”

“What process? I went home.”

“You left the booth. Your connection to it remained. That’s why it appeared again.”

Asgar turned toward the pillar. Through the red glass he could see its gray surface at an impossible depth.

“Did you send it here?”

“No. We do not choose the location.”

“But you knew it would appear?”

“We expected it. We didn’t know exactly when or where.”

“And you couldn’t call me?”

“Not on your mobile. Only through this telephone.”

He tightened his grip on the receiver. Its weight was beginning to tire his wrist; he had to keep his elbow forward so he would not pull the cord harder.

“What do you need from me?”

“To continue the transition, you’ll have to go inside. But before—”

“No.”

For a second, only background noise remained in the receiver.

Asgar stared at the threshold. Part of his attention kept sliding toward it, as if the moment he looked away his feet would somehow be on the other side.

“I went in once. I lost four days. If I go in now, when do I come out?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then why are you telling me to do it?”

“Because the process isn’t complete. As long as the connection persists, the booth may keep appearing near you.”

“And if I go inside, that ends it?”

A soft knock sounded on the line.

“I don’t know. It may. It may not.”

Asgar stepped back another half pace. The cord jerked the receiver; he caught it with both hands. The door remained slightly open, the cable trapped in the gap.

“So I go in again, disappear again, and then you tell me you still haven’t finished?”

“Yes. That can happen.”

He took the receiver away from his ear and looked at it. He wanted to smash it against the red wall.

He brought it back to his face.

“Then turn the whole thing off. I’m not going anywhere.”

“We tried. It didn’t work.”

“What happened?”

“You were able to get out. The connection remained.”

For several seconds Asgar listened to the background. People were speaking somewhere near the woman, but he could not make out the words. There were other people there. What they were doing was impossible to tell from the noise alone.

He stood in the garage beneath his own home with one arm extended toward a telephone booth.

“What if I refuse?”

“It may appear again.”

“Today? Tomorrow? My children are upstairs. Could it appear inside the apartment?”

“It could. We don’t know where the next appearance will be.”

Asgar looked up at the garage ceiling. Above him were floors, apartments, the kitchen where Katya had dictated the shopping list.

“Is it dangerous to be near it?”

“When the boundary changes, yes. Right now the connection is stable. We don’t yet know how long it will remain that way.”

“What exactly is dangerous? Electric shock? Could it crush someone?”

“The body of the booth is already intersecting your pillar. If a person is on the boundary, I don’t know what will happen to them. Move away when the state begins to change.”

Asgar looked at the pillar. The red plane entered the concrete with no gap. He tried to imagine a person standing where it was and took a step back. The receiver twisted in his palm; the cord stopped his hand.

“If you’re moving away, let go of the receiver,” the woman said.

He forced himself to stay where he was.

“Can you tell where I’m standing?”

“No. I cannot see you.”

“I’m outside. Still outside.”

“Good.”

Asgar tilted his head, listening. He expected her to tell him again to go inside, to say there was no other way.

Instead the woman asked:

“Are you prepared not to enter until the next appearance?”

“Yes. But somewhere farther from my home.”

“Then leave this area when the connection starts to break down.”

“When will it come back?”

“The current estimate is approximately forty-seven hours.”

He repeated the number silently. Almost two days.

Sunday.

“Could it happen sooner?”

“Yes.”

“And you’ll know, and then you’ll wait for me to pick up the phone again?”

“Yes. We have no other channel to you.”

Asgar rolled one shoulder. His mobile began vibrating in his pocket. He looked down but did not reach for it, waiting until the call stopped.

“You said you can’t tell me when I’ll come out. Where does this thing even go?”

“Wherever we can hold the connection. We do not set the place or the date.”

“Time,” he repeated. “You’re saying these four days…”

“Four days did pass outside.”

He slowly ran his tongue over dry lips. The calendar, Katya’s signature, the security recordings had already been in front of him. Still, hearing it from the person on the other end was harder.

“Send me back to Saturday.”

“I can’t.”

“I don’t need another year. I want the day I went in.”

“I hear you. I still can’t choose the date.”

He went quiet. Ventilation droned above him; somewhere on an upper level, a car moved.

“And the man?” Asgar asked. “The one who came out before me. Did you find him?”

“No.”

“He was inside your booth. Before me.”

“I don’t know who he is or how he got inside.”

Asgar closed his eyes.

Not one answer from her gave him something he could take back to the elevator and be done with this.

“The connection is losing stability,” the woman said.

He opened his eyes.

The edge of the red panel by the pillar began to double.

Asgar dropped the receiver at once. It hung from the cord, struck the body of the booth, swung in the gap. Her thin voice was still coming from it.

He backed away without turning his back on the booth. Once he passed the stairwell, he moved faster. Near the elevator, he stopped and took out his mobile.

The red wall shivered. For an instant, the car showed through it.

Then the booth was gone.

Asgar stood for several more seconds with his phone held in front of him. The pillar was where it had always been.

So was the car.

He called Katya.

“I didn’t go in.”

She exhaled audibly.

“Where are you now?”

“By the elevator. It disappeared.”

“Come upstairs. I’m waiting for you.”

He looked at the call button.

“Kat, I can’t come home right now.”

***

Rauf arrived after Asgar had already walked up the ramp and out to the street. Garage security had blocked access to the row after his report; Asgar waited until an employee put up a barrier, then left the building on foot. He stopped in an open stretch beside the driveway where he could see anyone approaching.

The investigator got out of his car and walked toward him.

“What did you do before it disappeared?”

“I picked up the receiver from outside.”

“I told you to wait.”

“It disappeared a few minutes later. We would have been left with one photograph.”

Rauf stopped in front of him.

“And now we have another conversation only you heard.”

“I’m not hiding anything.”

“I didn’t say you were. I’ve had a case before where details got out too early. The next day, half the witnesses suddenly ‘remembered’ what they’d read. It took me a month to clear that garbage out. That’s why I’m keeping the recordings and access controlled for now.”

Asgar took out his mobile.

“There’s a photo. Security is checking the cameras. I need somewhere with no people nearby.”

He told Rauf about the repeated appearances, the boundary of the booth, the forty-seven hours. Rauf clarified the wording. Asgar answered more sharply each time, but repeated it. When they reached the danger at the boundary, the investigator asked him to go over the woman’s exact words one more time.

“It’s already appeared here,” Asgar said. “Beside my home. How much more confirmation do you need before I’m allowed not to stand under my children’s windows?”

“We’ll find a place. That’s enough. But you will not decide by yourself who goes inside or when.”

“Just not a hospital room or a hotel. There are people through the walls.”

“I understand.”

Asgar lowered the phone. Katya was calling again.

“Is she coming here?” Rauf asked.

“She’s at home. I told her not to come down.”

“Then talk to her. I’ll work on the location.”

He went back toward the car.

Asgar answered.

“Have you decided where you’ll stay?” Katya asked.

“Kerimov is finding a place. Somewhere away from people.”

“And how long will you be there?”

“Probably until Sunday. They expect the next appearance in about forty-seven hours.”

“Who expects it?”

“The woman on the phone.”

“The same one after whom you were gone for four days?”

“Yes.”

He could hear the children in the apartment. Someone asked whether Dad had bought the bread. Katya answered away from the phone. Then a door creaked and the children’s voices became quieter.

“I’m not asking you to go near that booth,” she said. “I’m trying to understand what you’re planning to do. You said you’d pick up the receiver and call me. Now you’re saying you’re not coming home.”

“It can appear again. I saw the wall pass through a concrete pillar. If someone is standing where it appears…”

“I understand why you’re frightened. I don’t understand why you’ve already decided everything while I’m waiting by the door.”

Asgar rubbed a hand over his face.

“Because we can’t wait for it to appear in our kitchen.”

“You don’t know that it will appear in our kitchen.”

“I don’t. Want to find out?”

Katya went silent.

He felt at once how neatly he had cornered her. After a question like that, the only possible answer was agreement.

Asgar turned away from the building, still visible between the cars.

“I’m sorry. I said that wrong.”

“That is exactly what you said.”

“I’m afraid to go upstairs. That’s what I mean. I don’t want any of you standing near me if it comes back. I don’t know what the right thing is.”

Something creaked on the line. He wanted to be there beside her instead of guessing from sounds what she was doing.

“What should I bring you?” she asked.

“I don’t want you coming close.”

“Asgar. Charger. Clothes. What do you need?”

He looked toward Rauf. The investigator was still speaking on the phone beside his car.

“A charger. And something to change into. Give it to Kerimov, okay?”

“Okay.”

She did not hang up.

Asgar waited, listening to her breathing. They needed to tell the children. They needed to decide how to explain a second departure within a few days. That morning he had been making a grocery list, and grocery shopping had seemed like the right thing for a man who had come home.

“I didn’t go inside,” he said again.

“I heard you.”

“And I’m not planning to now.”

“If you change your mind, tell me first. Not when you’re already standing at the door.”

He looked at the building.

“I will.”

Rauf came back and named a place where Asgar could stay temporarily, separated from other people. They would have to wait until it was prepared. Asgar agreed to go.

Beside the car, he sent Katya the address. Added: *Phone will be on.*

Then he opened the shopping list.

Milk, bread, everything else was still there.

Asgar forwarded it to Katya and put the mobile in his pocket.
