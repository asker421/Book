Chapter 9. Another Language

Asgar still had his palm pressed against the glass.

Leaves moved outside. Large, dark leaves on thin branches; beyond them stood a white wall cut by deep vertical openings. The light came from above and slightly to one side. A shadow lay across the lower pane, swaying slowly with the branch.

He turned toward the other window.

A narrow path ran between the wall and the plantings. Farther away, beneath a long canopy, people were walking. One woman pushed a low cart in front of her. A man beside her stopped, turned, and pointed toward the booth.

Asgar took his hand from the glass and raised the receiver, still in his other hand, to his ear.

“I’m here.”

A faint hiss.

Then the woman’s voice.

“I hear you. Can you stand?”

“Yes. Where am I?”

“We’re checking where you arrived.”

He watched the people. The woman with the cart was already moving back beneath the canopy. The man remained where he was, holding something in front of him.

“There are people here. They can see me.”

“Don’t open the door yet. I need to check the boundary first.”

Asgar put a hand on the handle but did not press it.

He wanted to hear that this was another district.

A few hours later.

Tomorrow.

He would have accepted tomorrow, though not long ago he had been demanding 2014.

“What year is it?” he asked.

The woman was silent for a moment.

“By your calendar, May eighteenth, 2191.”

Asgar slowly let go of the handle.

“Say that again.”

She repeated it.

He turned toward the back wall of the booth. The red paint was worn where shoulders had brushed it. A narrow strip of grime darkened the lower edge.

“Bring the door back.”

“Asgar?”

“That hall. It was there. I just saw it. Bring it back.”

“I can’t restore the previous point.”

He pressed the receiver so hard against his ear that his cheek hurt.

“Not the whole year. One room. I left it a minute ago.”

The woman did not answer at once.

Asgar waited, staring into the corner.

“It’s no longer accessible,” she said.

He lowered the receiver.

Outside, the man moved farther away from the booth. The path emptied; several people gathered beneath the canopy. They were watching, but no one came closer.

A voice sounded from the receiver.

Asgar lifted it again.

“Repeat that.”

“You can open the door now.”

He stood for a while longer.

Then he replaced the receiver, took the handle, and pulled the door toward himself.

Hot air smelling of damp earth struck his face.

Asgar crossed the threshold and stopped on the path. To his right, behind dense leaves, water moved quietly. Above him came a steady hum like ventilation in a large building. He kept one hand on the door as long as he could reach it, then let go.

---

The man beneath the canopy spoke when Asgar had moved several steps away.

Asgar caught individual familiar sounds, but the sentence fell apart. He tried answering in Azerbaijani.

The man frowned, raised one hand, and said something into a device on his wrist.

The device gave a short reply. The man ran a finger down a list that appeared, stopped at one item, and spoke again.

After a brief pause, a question came in Russian.

“Do you understand Russian?”

Asgar nodded.

“Yes.”

The device repeated his answer in an unfamiliar voice.

The man listened, then spoke again. This time Asgar waited for the translation.

“Do not approach the structure. City services are already on their way. Is anyone still inside?”

“No.”

He looked back.

The booth was still there. Its red roof looked almost black beneath the shade of the canopy. A branch in front of it trembled in the moving air.

Asgar took one step back, but the man called out.

The translation came late, after Asgar had already stopped.

“Please remain here.”

“It may disappear.”

The man asked something quickly, but Asgar never heard the translator’s answer.

The pale wall began to show through the glass panes.

He lunged toward the booth, managed several steps, then braked with both hands out.

An unbroken paving tile lay in front of him.

A seam ran along the wall that had not been visible a second earlier.

Asgar stood over it.

Behind him, shoes slid against the surface. The man stopped off to one side without touching him.

“Are you injured?”

Asgar shook his head.

“Is this Baku?”

The translator delivered the question.

The man answered immediately.

“Yes. Baku.”

Asgar looked up at the white wall.

He tried to work out which direction the sea was in.

He could not.

---

The woman who arrived asked everyone to move away from the path. She spoke quickly, pointing toward the passage beneath the canopy. The man who had called the service answered her, pointed at the place where the booth had vanished, then at Asgar.

The woman listened, came closer, and a small device fastened to her clothing said:

“My name is Inessa Wald. I’m responsible for site safety. Can you tell me what happened?”

Asgar pointed at her device.

“Does that translate everything?”

She answered and waited for the Russian voice.

“Only our conversation. If the translation sounds wrong, stop me. Don’t guess.”

Asgar nodded.

Inessa asked whether he had touched the wall, whether anything had remained on the boundary of the structure, whether he had noticed any damage. Asgar told her about the door, the path.

Then he went back to the previous hall and the broken piece of concrete.

She stopped him with one hand.

“That happened here?”

“No. Before the transition.”

Inessa asked how much time had passed for him personally.

“A few minutes.”

“And according to the external dates?”

Asgar looked at the place where the booth had been.

“One hundred and fifty-two years.”

She went silent.

The translator had already finished, but Inessa was still looking at him.

Then she asked the question another way: where had he obtained both dates?

Asgar told her about the citizen center, the archive building, Katya. About the woman’s voice that had given him the new year.

Inessa checked something on her device.

“The date matches. We’ll verify the rest of your transition story separately.”

“Verify it.”

It came out sharper than he intended.

Inessa’s expression did not change.

“First, your name. Then I want a doctor to examine you. After a transition like the one you’re describing, I don’t know what may have happened to your body.”

Asgar almost refused.

Then he looked at his hands.

They looked the same.

“All right.”

---

The room they brought him to had windows overlooking an inner courtyard. Pale panels moved above it, slowly covering and uncovering strips of sunlight. The room brightened and darkened in turns, but the heat did not get inside.

Asgar sat on an examination couch.

The doctor fastened a sensor to his arm and asked him to look straight ahead. Translation came from a device on the table. Sometimes Asgar had to wait for the local speech to finish. He would begin answering too early, hear his own words over the other language, and lose his place.

“More slowly, please,” the doctor said through the translator. “I want to hear your full answer.”

Asgar pressed his lips together and nodded.

They examined him and took blood. Before every action, the doctor showed him what he was about to do and waited for consent.

When he touched Asgar’s shoulder, Asgar flinched.

“Does it hurt here?”

“I hit it against the wall. Earlier.”

The doctor asked him to raise his arm, then lower it.

For the identity check Inessa had requested, the doctor prepared a scanner. Asgar asked whether his old fingerprints had survived. The doctor read the accompanying note: the comparison request was using identification records from 2039.

Asgar placed his palm where indicated, then turned toward a camera at the doctor’s request.

He could still remember the tissue he had used to dry his fingers before the other scanner.

After the examination, Asgar remained seated.

Outside the window, one of the panels closed another section of sky. A broad shadow traveled across the floor.

“How old am I?” he asked.

The doctor turned away from his display.

“You can’t determine someone’s age to the year from one examination.”

“I don’t mean exactly. Could I have lived through all of it and forgotten?”

The doctor sat opposite him and answered.

“This does not look like the body of someone who has lived another century and a half. I see neither that degree of aging nor signs of intervention that could have concealed it. As far as your body is concerned, your account of only a few minutes passing is more plausible.”

Asgar looked down at the inside of his elbow where a small patch remained.

“So nothing happened to me.”

“Something happened,” the doctor replied after the translation pause. “You’re exhausted, you’ve had almost no rest, and you’ve been through several severe shocks. I’m speaking only about aging.”

He offered Asgar water.

Asgar took the cup, drank, then set it between his knees.

His body had not had time to pass through those years.

Neither had the sore shoulder.

Neither had the hunger.

Katya had.

---

In the temporary room, they showed him how to lock the door, where to get water, and how to call a staff member. The woman who brought him there spoke through a wall-mounted unit. After several of his replies, text appeared on the panel:

*Russian. Early twenty-first century.*

Asgar read it twice.

“Can I ask questions myself now?”

“Yes. The appropriate language setting is active here.”

The woman pointed to a circular symbol beside the door. If he needed a person, he should touch it.

Asgar waited until she left.

The room held a bed, a table, and an armchair. A folded blanket lay near the window. He ran a hand over the fabric, then sat at the table.

“Find a person.”

The panel asked for a name.

Asgar gave Katya’s. Added her surname and date of birth. At first he gave only the day and month, then forced himself to say the year.

The search did not finish immediately.

He watched the pale screen as records appeared.

Several people with the same surname.

Other cities.

One match on date of birth.

Asgar opened it.

Her full name was there.

Below it was a death record.

He did not read farther.

Closed the record. Returned to the list. Checked the name again.

Then the date of birth.

Then he opened the link to the source document.

The system displayed a preserved extract. Different format. Different field layout.

Same name and date of birth.

Same entry.

Asgar pulled his chair closer to the table.

“Are you sure this is her?”

The panel listed the matching data.

“That’s not what I asked.”

The voice suggested that he refine the query.

Asgar pushed the chair away and stood.

He walked to the window but did not look into the courtyard. Braced both palms on the sill and lowered his head.

At the shoulder of his T-shirt was a small crease made by Katya’s fingers.

He smoothed it out.

Then tried to put it back.

People walked past beyond the wall. Someone laughed quietly.

Asgar waited until their footsteps faded and still could not breathe properly.

Her last words repeated in his head:

*I love you too. That’s why I’m asking you to stay.*

He wanted to answer her.

Now he knew what to say, how to approach her, at which point to stop explaining.

He could simply have come back from the booth and sat opposite her. Katya would have brought him more water. The day after tomorrow his sons would have arrived.

Asgar bent over with his fist pressed to his mouth.

When he finally sat on the floor, his back was against the bed. He drew up his knees, buried his face against them, and stayed there.

---

The knock on the door came again.

Asgar lifted his head. His mouth was dry and one leg had gone numb from sitting.

“Wait.”

He got up holding the edge of the bed. Washed his face at the small sink, pressed a towel against it, and opened the door.

The employee who had brought him stood outside.

She spoke.

Asgar did not understand.

The woman noticed, glanced at the wall panel, and repeated herself more slowly.

The device translated.

“You didn’t respond to the message. I brought food. May I put it inside?”

He stepped aside.

She left the tray on the table. The archival extract was still open on the display.

The woman saw it, then looked at Asgar.

“Would you like someone to stay here with you?”

He shook his head.

She did not press him. She pointed out the water and the call symbol beside the door, then left.

Asgar sat in front of the tray.

The food was warm.

He picked up the spoon, held it for a moment, put it back down, and returned to the search.

He started with his older son.

Full name.

Date of birth.

Asgar spoke it aloud and heard himself automatically add the day of the week, though he no longer remembered it.

The record opened in full.

Fields ran down the screen one beneath another.

Name.

Years.

Location.

Occupation: municipal networks, water supply and distribution.

Below that, a list of infrastructure projects associated with him, with dates.

Asgar reread the occupation line.

In their hallway they had kept a toolbox, and once the older boy had taken everything out and arranged the tools by size. Asgar had been angry because he was looking for a screwdriver and it was not where he expected.

He remembered his own voice from the kitchen.

He could not remember how the argument ended.

He opened the project list.

The names meant nothing to him. Some, judging by the dates, had been built after his disappearance. One facility had been rebuilt three times, the last time forty years after his son’s death.

Asgar went back to the years.

Seventy-nine.

He calculated again, tracing a finger along the edge of the table because the first calculation had come too quickly to trust.

An image was attached to the record.

Asgar opened it before he had time to decide whether he wanted to.

The resolution was low.

A man in his fifties, hair cut short, wearing a pale shirt, photographed indoors against something institutional.

Asgar leaned closer.

The chin.

The line of the eyebrows.

He searched for the boy who used to take stairs two at a time and found a grown man who held his head differently.

He tried to enlarge the image.

The face broke into patches.

Asgar stared at them until he realized he was doing exactly what he had once done in the security room with the blurred face of a stranger.

He removed the zoom and closed the image.

The younger son was harder to find. At first the system offered people with the same surname, then a date-of-birth match.

Asgar opened the record and checked the field in full before allowing himself to read farther.

August sixteenth.

Occupation: education, work with historical collections.

Asgar leaned back.

The boy who had been waiting for him beside a birthday cake that evening had grown into a man who worked through other people’s papers and made sure they did not disappear.

The record was shorter than his older son’s.

He opened the sources.

There were only a few surviving materials in which the younger one had participated. Some had vanished along with their repositories; others survived only as descriptions.

Asgar opened them one by one and each time arrived at a notice about a transferred collection.

One link opened.

It was a later excerpt from someone else’s work. It quoted a person who had lost his father in circumstances that had entered the city chronicle.

The author preserved one sentence.

“I barely remember him myself; I remember how people spoke about him.”

His son’s surname stood beneath it.

Asgar read the line several times.

Closed it and opened it again to make sure it had not changed.

Then he asked whether there was more.

The system asked him to clarify.

Asgar named both sons and asked what was known after them.

The answer took longer to assemble.

Both had families.

For the next generation, the records still held together. After that they began to split apart: some were moved, some were lost when repositories merged, some survived only as references to things no longer preserved.

“And after that?” he asked.

The available data contained no reliable continuation.

Asgar placed both hands on the table.

Somewhere in this city there might be people carrying his blood and not one word about him.

He tried to imagine what he would do if the system produced an address.

Walk up.

Say what?

He had nothing to bring them except his own need.

He closed the search.

By the time he finished, the light beyond the glass had changed.

He had heard the younger one once—through a partition, for a few minutes.

He had never heard the older one at all.

He had been one morning short.

Asgar took out the dead phone and set it beside the display.

It held photographs he could still remember individually: failed shots, blurred ones, several almost identical pictures in a row.

He pressed the button with his thumb.

The glass remained black.

---

Inessa came back later.

She remained at the door until Asgar invited her to sit. The device on her clothing translated the first words with the same slight delay. In that delay, he could see her expression before he knew what it meant.

“They found records of both disappearances under your name. And a description of the structure. I sent a request to the group working on that history.”

She opened a report on her device and placed it in front of him.

His fingerprints matched those preserved in the 2039 material; facial comparison gave the same result.

Inessa waited for the translator to finish reading.

“When the result came back, I called the archive myself. Asked them to verify the date of the original record. They confirmed it.”

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the food tray.

“They confirmed it last time too.”

“I read that. Requests will start coming here now as well. No one from that research group will enter this room today without your consent.”

Asgar pushed the tray away.

“You know about the booth?”

“I do now. Before today’s call, it wasn’t my responsibility.”

“Who was working on it?”

“A research group. Martin wants to speak with you. He works with the surviving measurements.”

Asgar looked at the display.

“Let me see.”

Inessa sent him a collection.

A list of materials opened on the wall panel.

The stadium.

2014.

The technical hall in 2039.

He selected the stadium.

Instead of video, a file-description card appeared. The system offered an available copy.

“Where’s the original?”

Inessa read from her device.

“The primary file isn’t here.”

Asgar opened the copy.

The corridor was cropped at the edges. The red structure. A figure beside it. Several seconds of movement. Captions ran along the bottom, added long after the recording had been made.

He paused it.

“They had the full recording. I saw it.”

“It may be in another repository. It isn’t in this collection.”

He found the link to the previous archive and opened it. A notice said the collection had been transferred, followed by another link. On the next page, only an inventory remained.

Asgar went back.

The man who had come out of the booth was described here as unidentified.

No name.

No confirmed explanation.

He read the paragraph about himself and recognized several of his own sentences, compressed into someone else’s summary.

“Even this disappears,” he said.

Inessa looked at the screen.

“I’ll ask Martin to prepare whatever still has source data.”

“Did you build one? A booth. In all this time?”

Inessa looked up.

“No. The group says they never managed to reproduce those capabilities.”

Asgar was silent for a while.

“No one did?”

“They don’t know of any working equivalent. Ask Martin about the rest.”

Outside the window, the panels turned slowly. A bar of sunlight fell across the table and crossed the empty cup.

Here they could speak to him in a language nearly two centuries old.

They had found his name, tested his blood, given him a room with clean water.

People in the corridor were going about their lives.

Someone had brought him hot food without knowing whether he would eat it.

Asgar looked at the extract about Katya, still open in the neighboring window.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

Inessa waited for the translation.

“You want to speak with Martin tomorrow?”

“Yes. I can’t do any more today.”

Inessa nodded.

“Tomorrow, then. You can stay here. If you notice any changes, call me through the panel. We won’t place anyone in the adjoining area for now.”

Asgar looked toward the door.

“Can I sleep? You won’t…”

“Sleep. We won’t wake you without a reason.”

When she left, he closed the archive.

The tray was still on the table. Asgar ate several spoonfuls of cold food and drank some water.

Then he took off his shoes and set them beside the bed with the toes toward the door.

He put the phone on the table.

Thought about it, then moved it closer, to where he could reach it without getting up.

“How do I turn off the light?” he asked.

The panel answered in Russian.

Beside the answer, a short phrase in the local language appeared, followed by its pronunciation.

Asgar did not repeat it immediately.

The system failed to understand him the first time, so he said it again more slowly.

The light went out.
