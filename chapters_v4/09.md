Chapter 9. Another Language

Asgar still had his palm against the glass.

Leaves moved outside. Large, dark, on thin branches; behind them, a white wall with deep vertical slits. Light fell from above and slightly from the side. A shadow lay across the lower pane, swaying slowly with the branch.

He turned his head toward the other pane.

A narrow path ran between the wall and the plantings. Farther on, beneath a long canopy, people were walking. One woman pushed a low cart in front of her. A man near her stopped, turned, and pointed at the booth.

Asgar took his hand off the glass and raised the receiver he was still holding in the other hand to his ear.

"I'm here."

Faint noise. Then the woman's voice:

"I hear you. Can you stand?"

"Yes. Where am I?"

"We're checking where you ended up."

He looked at the people. The woman with the cart was already moving back beneath the canopy. The man remained where he was, holding something in front of him.

"There are people here. They can see me."

"Don't open the door yet. I'll check the boundary first."

Asgar put a hand on the handle but did not press it.

He wanted to hear that this was another district. A difference of several hours. Tomorrow. He would have accepted tomorrow even though not long ago he had been demanding the year two thousand fourteen.

"What year is it?" he asked.

The woman was silent for a moment.

"By your calendar—May eighteenth, two thousand one hundred ninety-one."

Asgar slowly let go of the handle.

"Again."

She repeated it, and he turned toward the rear wall of the booth. The red paint was worn where shoulders had rubbed against it. A narrow strip of dirt darkened along the bottom edge.

"Bring the door back."

"Asgar?"

"There was a hall. I just saw it. Bring it back."

"I can't restore the previous point."

He pressed the receiver to his ear so hard his cheek hurt.

"Not the whole year. One room. I came out of it a minute ago."

The woman did not answer immediately. Asgar waited, looking into the corner.

"It's no longer accessible," she said.

He lowered the receiver. Outside, the man moved away from the booth. The path was empty; several people had gathered beneath the canopy. They were looking this way, but no one approached.

A voice came from the receiver. Asgar raised it to his ear again.

"Repeat that."

"You can open the door now."

He stood there for a while, then put the receiver on the cradle, took the handle, and pulled the door toward himself.

Hot air carrying the smell of damp earth struck his face. Asgar stepped over the threshold and stopped on the path. To the right, behind thick leaves, water flowed quietly. Overhead was an even hum like ventilation in a large building. He held the door with one hand as long as he could reach it, then let it go.

---

The man beneath the canopy spoke when Asgar had moved a few steps away.

Asgar could make out individual familiar sounds, but the sentence fell apart. He tried answering in Azerbaijani. The man frowned, raised a hand, and said something into a device at his wrist.

The device answered him briefly. The man ran a finger down a list that appeared, stopped at one item, and said several more words.

After a short pause, a question sounded in Russian:

"Do you understand Russian?"

Asgar nodded.

"Yes."

The device repeated his answer in another voice. The man listened, then spoke again. This time Asgar waited for the translation.

"Do not approach the structure. City services are already on their way. Is anyone still inside?"

"No."

He looked back. The booth was still there. The red roof looked almost black beneath the shade of the canopy. A branch in front of it trembled in the moving air.

Asgar took a step back toward it, but the man called out to him. The translation came late, after Asgar had already stopped:

"Please remain here."

"It may disappear."

The man asked something quickly, but Asgar never heard the translator's answer.

A pale wall showed through between the glass panels.

He lunged toward the booth, managed several steps, then stopped hard with his hands out. The paving tile in front of him was whole. A seam ran along the wall that had not been visible a second ago.

Asgar stood over it. Soles scraped the surface behind him. The man stopped to one side without touching him.

"Are you injured?"

Asgar shook his head.

"Is this Baku?"

The translator relayed the question. The man answered immediately:

"Yes. Baku."

Asgar raised his eyes to the white wall. He tried to work out which direction the sea was and could not.

---

The woman who arrived asked everyone to move away from the path. She spoke quickly, pointing toward the passage beneath the canopy. The man who had called the service answered her, pointed to the place where the booth had vanished, then at Asgar.

The woman listened to him, came closer, and a small device attached to her clothing spoke:

"My name is Inessa Wald. I'm responsible for site safety. Can you tell me what happened?"

Asgar pointed at her device.

"Does that translate everything?"

She answered and waited for the Russian voice:

"Only our conversation. If the translation sounds strange, stop me. Don't guess."

Asgar nodded, and Inessa asked whether he had touched the booth itself, whether any of his belongings had remained near it, and whether he had noticed any damage to the path or wall. He told her about the door, about the path. Then he went back to the previous hall and the piece of broken concrete.

She stopped him with a gesture.

"Did that happen here?"

"No. Before the transition."

Inessa frowned.

"Before what?"

Asgar pointed at the place where the booth had stood.

"Before the transition here. I entered it somewhere else."

"In Baku?"

"Yes. Only in two thousand thirty-nine."

The translator finished the sentence. Inessa did not answer immediately.

"Wait. So for you it's still two thousand thirty-nine?"

"For me only a few minutes have passed since then."

She looked at the empty path, then back at Asgar.

"And how do you know what year it is now?"

"The woman on the phone told me. Two thousand one hundred ninety-one."

Inessa lowered her eyes to her device. She checked the screen, stayed on it slightly longer than necessary, then looked up.

"Today really is May eighteenth, two thousand one hundred ninety-one."

Asgar said nothing.

"All right," she said more quietly. "Then we'll start with the things I can verify. Tell me again: where were you before you found yourself here?"

Asgar told her about the center, the archives building, Katya. About the woman's voice on the telephone.

Inessa listened without interrupting.

"First we'll establish who you are. We'll check everything else separately."

"Go ahead."

He said it more sharply than he meant to. Inessa's expression did not change.

"And I want a doctor to examine you. You appeared here from an unknown location, and I don't know what happened to you before that."

Asgar wanted to refuse. Then he looked at his hands.

They were the same.

"All right."

---

The room they brought him to had windows overlooking an inner courtyard. Pale panels moved above it, slowly blocking strips of sunlight. The room grew brighter, then darker, but the heat did not come through.

Asgar sat on an examination couch. The doctor fastened a sensor to his arm and asked him to look straight ahead. The translation voice came from a device on the table. Sometimes he had to wait until the local speech ended. Asgar would start answering early, hear his own words over the foreign voice, and lose his place.

"More slowly, please," the doctor said through the translator. "I want to hear your entire answer."

Asgar pressed his lips together and nodded. They examined him and took blood. Before each action, the doctor showed him what he was about to do and waited for consent. When he touched Asgar's shoulder, Asgar flinched.

"Does it hurt here?"

"I hit it against the wall. Earlier."

The doctor asked him to raise his arm, then lower it.

For the identity check Inessa had requested, the doctor prepared a scanner. Asgar asked whether old fingerprints had survived. The doctor read the accompanying record: the verification request referenced identification materials from the year two thousand thirty-nine. Asgar placed his palm where indicated, then turned toward the camera at the doctor's request. He still remembered the tissue he had used to wipe his fingers before another scanner.

After the examination, Asgar remained sitting. Outside the window, a panel covered another piece of sky; a broad shadow moved across the floor.

"Is everything actually all right with me?" he asked.

The doctor turned from the screen.

"So far I don't see anything wrong."

"That's not what I mean. I was in two thousand thirty-nine. A few minutes passed for me, and now it's already two thousand one hundred ninety-one. Could that have done something to me?"

The doctor looked at the results.

"You haven't aged, if that's what you mean."

Asgar looked at his hands.

"I can see that. What about inside?"

The doctor gave a slight shrug.

"These tests don't show anything unusual. But after one examination, I won't tell you that you're definitely completely fine."

"So for now I'm fine?"

"For now—yes."

He offered Asgar water. Asgar took the cup, drank, and held it between his knees.

Neither his body, nor the injured shoulder, nor his hunger had passed through those years.

Katya had.

---

In the temporary accommodation room, they showed him how the door locked, where the water was, and how to call an employee. The woman who brought him there spoke through a wall device. After several of his answers, text appeared on the panel: "Russian. Early 21st century."

Asgar read it twice.

"Can I ask questions myself now?"

"Yes. The appropriate language setting is active here."

The woman pointed at a circular symbol near the door. If he needed a person, he had to touch it.

Asgar waited until she left.

There was a bed, a table, and an armchair in the room. A folded blanket lay by the window. He ran his hand over the fabric, then sat at the table.

"Find a person."

The panel asked for a name.

Asgar gave Katya's. Added her surname, date of birth. At first he gave only the day and month, then forced himself to say the year.

The search did not finish immediately.

He watched the pale screen as records appeared. Several people with the same surname. Other cities. A match by date of birth.

Asgar opened it.

The full name was there.

Below it was a death record.

He did not read further. Closed the record, returned to the list, and checked the name again.

Then the date of birth.

Then he opened the link to the source document.

The system showed a preserved extract. A different kind of record, different arrangement of fields. The same name and date of birth. The same notation.

Asgar pulled the chair closer to the table.

"Is that definitely her?"

The panel listed the matching data.

"That's not what I'm asking."

The voice offered to refine the query. Asgar pushed the chair away and stood.

He walked to the window but did not look into the courtyard. Braced both palms on the sill and lowered his head. There was still a small crease in his T-shirt at the shoulder where Katya's fingers had crushed it.

He smoothed it out.

Then tried to put it back.

People passed beyond the wall. Someone laughed quietly. Asgar waited for the footsteps to fade and still could not breathe properly.

Her last words kept repeating in his head: "I love you too. That's why I'm asking you to stay."

He wanted to answer.

Now he knew what to say, how to approach her, where to stop explaining. He could simply have come back from the booth and sat down opposite her. Katya would have brought him more water. The sons would have come the day after tomorrow.

Asgar bent over, pressing his fist to his mouth.

When he finally sat on the floor, his back was against the bed. He drew his knees up, buried his face in them, and remained that way.

---

The knock at the door came again.

Asgar raised his head. His mouth was dry, and one leg had gone numb from sitting too long.

"Wait."

He stood, holding the edge of the bed. Washed his face at the small sink, blotted it with a towel, and opened the door.

The employee who had brought him there stood outside.

She spoke. Asgar did not understand. The woman noticed, looked at the wall panel, and repeated herself more slowly. The device translated:

"You didn't answer the message. I brought food. May I put it down?"

He stepped aside. She set the tray on the table. The extract was still open on the screen. The woman saw it, then looked at Asgar.

"Would you like someone to stay here?"

He shook his head. The woman did not press him: she pointed to the water, to the call symbol near the door, and left.

Asgar sat in front of the tray. The food was warm. He picked up the spoon, held it, set it down, and returned to the search.

He began with the older son.

Full name. Date of birth. Asgar said it aloud and heard himself automatically add the day of the week, which he did not remember.

The record opened in full.

The fields ran one beneath another. Name. Years. Place. Occupation: urban networks, water supply and distribution. Below was a list of facilities he had been connected with, with dates.

Asgar reread the line about occupation.

There had been a toolbox in their entryway, and once the older boy had taken everything out of it to arrange it by size. Asgar had gotten angry because he was looking for a screwdriver and could not find it where it belonged. He remembered his own voice from the kitchen and did not remember how it ended.

He opened the list of facilities.

The names meant nothing to him. Judging by the dates, some had been built after his disappearance. One node had been rebuilt three times, the last time forty years after his son's death.

Asgar returned to the years.

Seventy-nine.

He calculated again, tracing a finger along the edge of the table because the first calculation had come too quickly to believe.

An image was attached to the record.

Asgar opened it before deciding whether he wanted to.

The resolution was low. A man over fifty, with short hair, in a pale shirt, photographed indoors against something utilitarian. Asgar moved closer. Chin. Line of the eyebrows. Asgar tried to find in him the boy who could not stay still even for a photograph beside his brother, and found an adult man who held his head completely differently.

He tried to enlarge it.

The face broke into regions.

Asgar looked at them until he realized he was doing the same thing he had once done in the security room over a stranger's blurred face. He removed the enlargement and closed the image.

The younger son was not found immediately. First the system offered people with the same surname, then a date-of-birth match. Asgar opened the record and checked the entire field before allowing himself to read further.

August sixteenth.

Occupation: education, work with historical collections.

Asgar leaned back in the chair.

The boy who had waited for him to come home for the cake that evening had grown into a man who sorted other people's papers and made sure they did not disappear.

The record was shorter than his older brother's.

He opened the sources. There were not many materials in which the younger son had participated. Some had disappeared along with a repository, others survived only as descriptions. Asgar opened them one by one and each time reached a notice about a collection transfer.

One link opened.

It was a later excerpt from someone else's work. It quoted the words of a man who had lost his father under circumstances that became part of the city's chronicle. The author cited one sentence.

"I barely remember him myself; I remember how people talked about him."

The surname stood beneath it.

Asgar read the line several times. Closed it and opened it again to make sure it had not changed.

Then he asked whether there was more.

The system asked him to refine the query. Asgar named both sons and asked to show what was known afterward.

The response took longer to assemble than the previous ones. Both had families. Records still held through the next generation, then began to diverge: some were transferred, some lost in repository consolidations, some remained only as references to material that no longer existed.

"And after that?" he asked.

There was no reliable continuation in the available data.

Asgar put his palms on the table.

Somewhere in this city there might be people who carried his blood and not a single word about him. He tried to imagine what he would do if the system gave him an address. Walk up. Say what. He had nothing for them except his own need.

He closed the search.

By the time he was done, the light beyond the glass had changed.

He had heard the younger one once—through the partition, for a few minutes. He never heard the older one at all. One morning had not been enough.

Asgar took out the dead phone. Put it beside the screen. It contained photographs he could still remember one by one: bad ones, blurred ones, several almost identical pictures in a row.

He pressed the button with his thumb.

The glass remained black.

---

Inessa came back later.

She remained by the door until Asgar invited her to sit. The device on her clothing translated the first words with the same slight delay. During that delay, he had time to see her expression before he knew its meaning.

"Materials on two disappearances were found under your name. And a description of the structure. I sent a request to the group that works with this history."

She opened a report on her device and placed it in front of him. The fingerprints matched those preserved in the 2039 materials; the facial comparison gave the same result. Inessa waited for the translation to finish reading.

"When the answer came back, I called the archive myself. Asked them to verify the date of the source record. They confirmed it."

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the tray.

"They confirmed it last time too."

"I read that. Requests will come here now as well. No one from the group will enter today without your consent."

Asgar pushed the tray away.

"Do you know about the booth?"

"Now I do. Before today's call, it wasn't my case."

"Who was working on it?"

"A research group. Martin wants to speak with you. He works with the surviving measurements."

Asgar looked at the screen.

"Let me see."

Inessa sent him a collection. A list of materials opened on the panel. Stadium. Two thousand fourteen. Technical hall, two thousand thirty-nine.

He selected the stadium.

Instead of video, a card appeared with a description of the file. The system offered an available copy.

"Where's the original?"

Inessa read the text on her device.

"The primary file isn't here."

Asgar opened the copy.

The corridor was cropped at the edges. The red body, a figure beside it, several seconds of movement. Captions added much later than the recording ran along the bottom. He paused it.

"They had the full version. I saw it."

"Maybe it's in another repository. It isn't in this collection."

He found a link to the former archive. Opened it. Received notice that the collection had been transferred, then another link. On the next page, only an inventory remained.

Asgar went back.

The man who had emerged from the booth was described here as unknown. No name, no confirmed explanation. He read the paragraph about himself and recognized several of his own phrases reduced to someone else's summary.

"Even this gets lost," he said.

Inessa looked at the screen.

"I'll ask Martin to prepare whatever still has source data."

"Did you build a cabin? In all this time?"

Inessa looked up.

"No. The group says they were unable to reproduce those capabilities."

Asgar was silent for a while.

"No one did?"

"They don't know of a working analogue. Ask Martin about the rest."

The panels beyond the window slowly turned. A strip of sunlight fell across the table and crossed the empty cup.

Here they could speak to him in a language that was almost two centuries old. They found his name, checked his blood, gave him a room with clean water. People went about their business in the corridor. Someone had brought him hot food without knowing whether he would eat it.

Asgar looked at the extract about Katya still open in the neighboring window.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Inessa waited for the translation.

"You want to speak with Martin tomorrow?"

"Yes. I can't do any more today."

Inessa nodded.

"Then tomorrow. You can stay here. If you notice any changes, call me through the panel. We're not placing anyone in the neighboring section for now."

Asgar looked at the door.

"Can I sleep? You won't—"

"Sleep. We won't wake you without a reason."

When she left, he closed the archive.

The tray was still on the table. Asgar ate several spoonfuls of the cooled food, drank water. Then took off his shoes and set them beside the bed, toes toward the door.

He put the phone on the table.

Thought about it and moved it closer, where he could reach it without getting up.

"How do I turn off the light?" he asked.

The panel answered in Russian. A short local phrase appeared beside it and its pronunciation sounded aloud.

Asgar did not repeat it immediately. The system did not understand him the first time, and he said it again, more slowly.

The light went out.
