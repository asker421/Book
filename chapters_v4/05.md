Chapter 5. In a Few Seconds

One leg of the folding cot kept collapsing. Asgar found a broken piece of tile under the table, wedged it beneath the hinge, and sat down carefully. The metal held. The moment he lay back, something scraped by the wall, and he had to get up again.

He was no longer sleepy.

He pulled on his jeans, slipped the phone into his pocket, and stepped out of the trailer. Dust lay on the steps; overnight it had worked its way even into the grooves of the rubber mat. Asgar went down, holding the door so it would not slam.

Sunday morning at the repair depot began without people. An empty concrete yard, closed bays, brown gates at the far end. Some motor was running beyond the fence. Sometimes the sound choked off, and in the silence that followed the pigeons on the roof became audible.

On Friday they had brought in the cot, water, and a small refrigerator that at first smelled stronger than the food inside it. Katya's bag stood under the table. Yesterday Asgar had found a pack of cookies between the T-shirts—the edge had already been opened and clipped shut with a binder clip. He ate two and hid it again.

Rauf was coming from the gate with two paper cups.

"You're early," Asgar said.

"It's almost ten."

"I meant for a Sunday."

Rauf handed him the coffee. One side of the lid had not snapped down; Asgar pressed it with his thumb.

"Sleep?"

"The furniture wouldn't let me."

The investigator looked at the open trailer door.

"You should have said something yesterday."

"Yesterday it stayed up."

They moved away from the steps. A track from an old wheel ran across the concrete and ended at a welded drain grate. In two days Asgar had learned the cracks around it by heart. He walked from the trailer to the far bay, turned around, and walked back. Someone stayed on duty by the perimeter, but they tried not to come too close to him.

A man in a gray shirt was crouching by the camera. He was tightening a mount on the tripod. The camera had been set at the edge of the yard, aimed toward the area where Asgar spent most of the day.

"Will he leave afterward?" Asgar asked.

"When he's done, he'll move away."

The man looked up.

"All set."

He stood, brushed off one knee, and carried the empty case toward the gate. Asgar watched him go. Ever since the woman had said forty-seven hours, he had constantly been estimating distances: to another person, to a wall, to a place where a car could stop. Even in his sleep he saw the parking garage, except every time someone was standing between the column and the booth.

Rauf took a sip of coffee.

"Did your wife call?"

"I'll call her myself now."

"I'll be by the entrance. If you notice anything, move away immediately and call out. You don't need to approach it for the sake of the recording."

Asgar nodded and waited until the investigator had moved off. Then he called Katya.

She did not answer at once.

"You're awake?"

"Been up a while. What's going on there?"

"We're arguing about whether breakfast can be skipped on a Sunday."

In the distance, the younger boy objected to something. Katya's voice moved away, but Asgar still heard:

"Cookies count as food too. That's exactly why I asked you not to take them yet."

He smiled, looking at his cup.

"Who's winning?"

"No one yet. How are you?"

Asgar told her about the cot. Katya said there should be a brace on the side: it had to be lowered all the way, otherwise the leg was held only by its own weight.

"How do you know?"

"Because you just told me about the tile. Look at the side, there should be a brace."

He turned toward the trailer.

"Found it."

"Well, then we're making progress."

Asgar laughed. Then she asked whether everything was still quiet, and he said yes.

For a while they talked about the children. The older boy needed new shoes, the younger wanted to come visit Dad because he had decided Dad was living in a real construction cabin and was allowed to touch everything there. Asgar listened to Katya moving around the kitchen, turning on the water, shifting dishes. There was no natural way to insert into this conversation what he had been thinking about since Friday.

"Today it'll become clearer," he finally said.

The water stopped running.

"What exactly?"

"Whether it comes back or not."

"Will you call me?"

"Of course."

He almost added don't worry, but instead asked whether she wanted to put the younger boy on. His son took the phone and spent some time breathing directly into the microphone.

"Dad, is there a faucet there?"

"No. Just garages."

"When are you coming back?"

Asgar traced one of the cracks in the concrete with the toe of his shoe.

"I don't know yet."

"Mom says that too."

"Because we still don't know."

The boy was quiet for a moment, then started telling him about a toy car whose hood opened. Asgar asked questions until the voice moved away from the phone—the boy had run off to show the car to his brother.

By the time the call ended, the coffee was cold.

---

When the camera fell, Asgar's first thought was that the man had not secured the tripod properly.

He was sitting on the bottom step of the trailer. The shade had just moved off his knees, and he was about to shift the chair to the other side when he heard a sharp metallic snap.

The camera tilted. One of the legs jerked upward, the others slid across the concrete.

Behind it, a vertical red stripe appeared.

Asgar jumped to his feet.

For several moments he could see the gates and Rauf walking from them through the stripe. Then the red widened, hid part of the fence. A wall appeared. The sky reflected as a white patch in the glass.

The tripod toppled sideways. The camera struck the concrete and rolled as far as the mounting allowed.

"Back!" Rauf shouted.

The man in the gray shirt had already started running toward it. At the shout he stopped, took one more step from momentum, then backed away.

Asgar moved away from the trailer toward the gates, watching the booth. It stood at the edge of the yard. The nearest tripod leg lay in front of it, the other two turned off to the side.

The lower end of the third was gone.

Asgar stopped beside Rauf.

"Did you see that?"

"Let's move farther back."

"Did you see the leg?"

The investigator took him by the elbow and led him away. He let go only at the fence.

The booth was no longer wavering. The roof of the bay reflected in its glass. Down below, near the red base, the hollow tube of the tripod leg showed pale—open and shortened. The rubber foot had vanished along with part of the metal.

The man in the gray shirt took out his phone, photographed the stump, and zoomed in on the image.

"It didn't fold," he said.

Rauf looked at the screen.

"Save that. And stay here."

Asgar leaned toward the phone too. The cut was diagonal. A thin bright edge and dark emptiness inside were visible.

A minute ago that leg had been resting on the concrete.

He straightened and looked at the man. At the dirt on his knee, at the case he was still holding by the handle.

A telephone rang inside the booth.

No one moved.

Rauf took out his own phone and gave a short instruction not to let anyone onto the site. Asgar stood beside him; with every new ring his jaw tightened harder. For two days he had walked across empty concrete, left food by the door, spoken to the children by phone. All that time he had imagined that choosing the place correctly and keeping others away would be enough.

"Can I stay here longer?" he asked.

Rauf put the mobile away.

"You stay here today."

"A week? A month?"

"No. Today we move everyone beyond the gate. Tomorrow we think further. Next time it can appear anywhere relative to you; we can't keep an empty circle of unknown size clear for weeks."

"What if next time it appears by the gate?"

The investigator looked at the booth.

"Then we clear the gate too. As long as we can, we'll keep everything around you empty."

He sent the man with the case toward the car, then turned to Asgar.

"You can wait. I'm not forcing you inside."

Asgar rubbed the back of his neck.

"I know."

He walked along the fence and called Katya.

"It appeared," he said as soon as she answered. "I'm far away. Still far."

"What happened?"

Asgar told her about the camera. Katya asked him to explain again what he meant by "part of the leg disappeared." He repeated it, trying to speak more slowly.

"The man got away in time?"

"He was already away. Before it appeared."

The other end went quiet.

"I'm thinking about going in," Asgar said.

He looked down at the concrete. A flattened bottle cap lay by the toe of his shoe.

"You can stay there."

"Today I can."

"Then stay. Not now. You've just seen this, you don't even know what happened."

"On Friday she said it could be dangerous near the boundary. I thought then... I don't know what I thought. The column stayed intact."

"And now you want to go inside?"

He heard his own decision the way it sounded to her: metal had been severed beside a dangerous thing, and now he intended to shut himself inside it.

"If I stay, we just shift the risk to the next appearance. Today they got people out in time. Next time we may not."

"That still doesn't mean you have to go in. Asgar, you don't even know it."

"I don't. First I'll ask what going in actually changes."

"And you don't know when you'll come out either."

He did not answer.

Katya drew in a sharp breath.

"Are you calling to tell me or to ask me?"

Asgar raised his head. Rauf was standing off to the side, facing the yard.

"To tell you I want to try. I promised I'd call before I went near it."

"I remember what you promised. Calling doesn't make this any easier."

"Katya..."

"Wait. Let me at least think."

He waited. The ringing inside the booth stopped, and the motor beyond the fence became audible again. Asgar gripped the chain-link mesh with his fingers.

"Maybe talk to her from outside again?" Katya asked. "Like in the parking garage. Find out first."

"I will."

"And if she says something different, you don't have to do what you've already decided."

"All right."

Katya was silent for a moment.

"I don't agree. You understand?"

"Yes."

"Just don't say later that we decided this."

Asgar closed his eyes.

"I won't."

He wanted to hear the children. He had already drawn breath to ask and couldn't. He would have to answer again when will you come back.

"Do you have the charger?" Katya asked.

"In the trailer."

"Is the phone charged?"

He looked at the screen.

"Almost full."

"Take it with you."

"It's on me."

She did not say anything, but she did not hang up either. Asgar listened to her breathing until standing by the fence became unbearable.

"I love you."

"I love you too. Call me when you come out. Immediately."

"Immediately."

He waited until Katya ended the call and kept the phone by his face for a little longer.

---

Rauf listened without interrupting.

"First I'll talk to her," Asgar finished. "If it's the same, I'll go in."

The investigator glanced toward the trailer.

"Do you have your documents?"

Asgar patted his back pocket.

"Yes."

"What should I tell your wife if contact is lost?"

The question made him feel cold despite the sun.

"That I'll call her."

Rauf did not look away.

"All right."

Asgar put away the mobile. Before the screen went dark, he caught the time: 11:11.

He walked across the yard.

As he approached, a small scratch beside the door handle became visible. He had not noticed it at the stadium. Not in the parking garage either. His gaze lingered on it longer than on anything else: an ordinary mark in the paint, as if someone had once missed with a key.

The fallen camera lay to the left. Asgar kept to the right, away from the tripod. At the door he stood for a moment, listening, then took hold of the handle.

The door opened heavily. Warm paint smell drifted from inside.

The telephone hung on the right. Asgar held the door with his shoulder, lifted the receiver, and stayed in front of the threshold.

"It's me."

"Asgar, I can hear you."

The familiar voice made him want to shout.

"Did you see what happened there?"

"Tell me."

"Your booth cut off a piece of a metal leg. From the camera. A man had set it up here."

The woman did not answer at once.

"Was anyone hurt?"

"No. Because he moved away."

"Step away from the walls. And don't touch the leg—leave it as it is."

Asgar looked across the empty yard. Rauf was by the fence, far away.

"If I go in, will it stop appearing here?"

"While you're inside, the cabin won't be here. What happens after you exit, I don't know."

"So it all starts over?"

"Maybe. We're trying to bring it to an end."

He squeezed the door handle so hard his fingers hurt.

"Do you even understand that I'm leaving my family here?"

"Yes."

"No, you'll just tell me 'possibly' again, and that's it. And I'll come out... When will I come out?"

The woman spoke more quietly.

"I don't know. If I knew, I'd tell you."

Asgar looked at his shoes. Only a few centimeters separated them from the threshold. He could hang up, go back to Rauf. By evening ask them to bring another folding cot. Tomorrow call home again from this yard.

To the left, the open end of the metal tube glinted on the concrete.

"What do I do?" he asked.

"If you've decided to go—step fully inside. Close the door from the inside. Don't hang up the receiver yet."

He moved his shoulder away from the door, held it with one hand, and stepped across the threshold.

Inside, he had to turn sideways. His elbow brushed the wall, the cord lay across his chest. Asgar shifted one foot and turned toward the opening.

Rauf raised a hand.

Asgar nodded to him and pulled the door.

Just before the click, he could still see a narrow strip of the yard: concrete, the far gates, the edge of a white car. Then the door settled into the frame.

"Closed."

"Stay where you are."

Asgar had only just touched the mobile in his pocket when the floor seemed to drop briefly beneath his heels.

He swayed, hit his shoulder against the glass, and threw out his free hand.

"What just..."

The voice in the receiver broke into several dry clicks.

Asgar pressed it tighter to his ear.

"Can you hear me?"

No more words came from the receiver.

He waited, counting breaths, lost count, and started again. Beyond the glass was milky light. His own reflection made it hard to see the yard; Asgar leaned toward the window and shaded his eyes with one hand.

A shadow passed outside.

He jerked toward the handle.

The door opened.

The receiver was silent. Asgar put it back on the cradle and opened the door wider.

---

The first thing he saw was a tree.

Its trunk stood almost directly in front of the exit. Around it was a circular grate clogged with dry leaves. Asgar stopped, still holding the door, and looked up.

The crown covered several floors of an unfamiliar building.

He stepped out.

Instead of concrete, pale paving stones were under his feet. People were moving across them. A woman carrying a bag went around him, giving the open door an annoyed look. A few steps later she looked back at the red wall, as though only then noticing it stood right in the middle of the walkway. Beyond her shoulder stretched a row of trees; farther away was a road.

Asgar turned left, toward where the gates should have been.

Glass entrance doors. A man with a paper cup. A bicycle at a rack.

"Rauf!"

The man with the cup looked around.

Asgar started toward him, took two steps, and stopped. He turned back toward the booth.

It was already gone.

Paving stones continued through the place where the red body had stood. He stepped there with his palms out. Took another step, turned around. The woman who had gone around him was standing at the crossing and looking back. Her bag hung from her lowered hand.

"Wait!"

She was already looking at him. Then she shifted her gaze to the empty place beside the tree and took out her phone.

Asgar rushed back to the trunk. Went around it, looked behind it, though there was no way a booth could hide there. A crumpled receipt lay by the grate.

He raised his eyes to the facade as if the red door might somehow be higher up.

"Damn it..."

The man with the cup set it on the edge of a trash bin and came a few steps closer.

"Was that just standing here?"

Asgar did not answer. He took out his phone and called Katya. The call did not go through. He tried again, then opened Rauf's number. Same result.

The sun warmed the back of his neck. The collar of his T-shirt stuck to his skin. The shoulder he had hit inside still hurt.

He needed a network. Someone else's phone. Anyone who could tell him where he was.

Asgar approached the man.

"Excuse me. Can I make a call? I have no service."

The man looked at his mobile.

"To whom?"

"My wife. One call."

"Do you know the number?"

Asgar started to dictate it and stopped.

Across the road stood a tower that had not been there before. He recognized the curve of the street, the distant slope, but so much new construction had appeared between them that the familiar had to be picked out in pieces.

"What's today's date?"

The man leaned back slightly.

"The twenty-fourth."

"August?"

"Yes."

Asgar exhaled. Only the place, then. Maybe only the place.

"And the year?"

Now the man looked at him carefully.

"Are you feeling all right?"

"Tell me the year."

"Two thousand thirty-nine."

Asgar waited for him to correct himself.

A long, low car passed on the road. Two girls laughed by the crossing. The man held his cup by the top rim, little finger angled away from the hot cardboard.

"What?" Asgar asked.

"Thirty-nine."

"No. Today is August twenty-fourth, two thousand fourteen."

The man set his cup on the edge of a street bin.

"Why don't you sit down. There's..."

Asgar stepped back.

"No."

He looked at his own phone. The same date remained on the screen. Sunday. August twenty-fourth. He shoved it almost into the man's face.

"Look."

The man looked at the screen. His eyes moved over the body of the phone and returned to the numbers.

"You can set any date on a phone."

Asgar lowered his hand.

Behind the man, under the shelter at a bus stop, a display glowed. Asgar walked toward it without answering the question of whether he needed help. He waited for the advertisement to change. Above the route list, a date appeared.

24.08.2039.

He stood there until the advertisement returned.

Then waited again.

The numbers stayed the same.

Asgar pressed a palm over his mouth. Tried to subtract fourteen from thirty-nine and could not hold the numbers in his head. Katya was in front of him at the kitchen table; he could hear her asking whether he'd checked the charger. That had been minutes ago.

Twenty-five years.

He sat on the edge of the bench, missing the empty space and bumping someone else's bag. The woman beside him pulled it closer. Asgar apologized, but almost no sound came out.

The younger boy would already be an adult.

The thought made him stand up.

He opened Katya's number again and pressed call. Watched the screen until it said the connection could not be made. Tried again.

Then turned toward the tree.

People moved between it and the bus stop. There was no red anywhere. Asgar went back, stood in the place where he had let go of the door, and did not move for several seconds.

He was supposed to call immediately.

Asgar put the phone back in his pocket and looked along the street, trying to work out which direction home was.
