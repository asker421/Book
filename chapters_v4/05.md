Chapter 5. In a Matter of Seconds

One leg of the folding cot kept collapsing. Asgar found a broken piece of tile under the table, wedged it beneath the hinge, and sat down carefully. The metal held. As soon as he lay down, something scraped by the wall, and he had to get up again.

He was no longer sleepy.

He pulled on his jeans, slipped the phone into his pocket, and stepped out of the trailer. Dust lay on the steps; overnight it had worked its way even into the grooves of the rubber mat. Asgar went down, holding the door so it would not slam.

Sunday morning at the repair depot began without people. An empty concrete yard, closed service bays, brown gates at the far end. Some kind of engine was running beyond the fence. Sometimes the sound choked off, and in the silence that followed, pigeons on the roof could be heard.

On Friday they had brought a folding cot, water, and a small refrigerator that at first smelled stronger than the food inside it. Katya's bag stood under the table. Yesterday Asgar had found a packet of cookies between the T-shirts—the edge had already been opened and clipped shut with a binder clip. He ate two and hid it back.

Rauf was walking from the gate with two paper cups.

"You're early," Asgar said.

"It's almost ten."

"I meant for a Sunday."

Rauf handed him a coffee. The lid had not snapped shut on one side; Asgar pressed it down with his thumb.

"Did you sleep?"

"The furniture wouldn't let me."

The investigator looked at the open trailer door.

"You should have said something yesterday."

"Yesterday it stayed up."

They moved away from the steps. A mark from an old wheel ran across the concrete and ended at a welded drainage grate. In two days, Asgar had managed to memorize the cracks around it. He walked from the trailer to the far service bay, turned around, and walked back. There were people on duty by the perimeter fence, but they tried not to come close to him.

A man in a gray shirt was crouching beside the camera. He was tightening a fitting on the tripod. They had set the camera at the edge of the yard, pointing it toward the area where Asgar spent most of the day.

"Will he leave afterward?" Asgar asked.

"When he's done, he'll move away."

The man looked up.

"All set."

He stood, brushed off his knee, and carried the empty case toward the gate. Asgar watched him go. Ever since the woman had said forty-seven hours, he had constantly been estimating distances: to another person, to a wall, to a place where a car could be stopped. Even in his sleep he saw the parking garage, except someone was always standing between the column and the booth.

Rauf took a sip of coffee.

"Has your wife called?"

"I'll call her now."

"I'll be by the entrance. If you notice anything, move away immediately and call out. No need to approach just for the recording."

Asgar nodded and waited until the investigator had moved away. Then he called Katya.

She did not answer right away.

"Are you awake?"

"Long ago. What's going on there?"

"We have an argument about whether it's allowed not to eat breakfast on Sunday."

In the distance, the younger boy objected to something. Katya's voice moved away, but Asgar still heard her:

"Cookies count as food too. That's exactly why I asked you not to take them yet."

He smiled, looking at his cup.

"Who's winning?"

"No one yet. How are you?"

Asgar told her about the folding cot. Katya said there should be a brace on the side: it had to be pushed all the way down, otherwise the leg was supported only by its own weight.

"How do you know?"

"Because you just told me about the tile. Look on the side, there should be a brace."

He turned toward the trailer.

"Found it."

"Well, then there's progress."

Asgar laughed. Then she asked whether everything was still quiet, and he said yes.

For a while they talked about the children. The older boy needed new shoes, the younger one wanted to visit Dad because he had decided Dad was living in a real construction trailer where he was allowed to touch everything. Asgar listened to Katya moving around the kitchen, turning on the water, shifting dishes. There was no way to fit what he had been thinking about since Friday into this conversation.

"Today it'll become clearer," he said at last.

The water stopped running.

"What exactly?"

"Whether it comes back or not."

"You'll call me?"

"Of course."

He almost added "don't worry," but instead asked whether she could put the younger boy on. His son took the phone and spent some time breathing directly into the microphone.

"Dad, is there a crane there?"

"No. Just garages."

"When are you coming back?"

Asgar traced a crack in the concrete with the toe of his shoe.

"I don't know yet."

"Mom says that too."

"Because we don't know yet."

The boy was silent for a moment, then started talking about a toy car whose hood opened. Asgar asked questions until the voice moved away from the phone—the boy had run off to show the car to his brother.

By the time the call ended, the coffee had gone cold.

---

When the camera fell, Asgar's first thought was that the man had secured the tripod badly.

He was sitting on the bottom step of the trailer. The shade had just moved off his knees, and he was about to move the chair around to the other side when he heard a sharp metallic click.

The camera tilted. One of the legs jerked upward, the others slid across the concrete.

A red vertical strip appeared behind it.

Asgar jumped to his feet.

For several moments he could see the gates and Rauf walking from them through the strip. Then the red widened and hid part of the fence. A wall appeared. The sky reflected as a white patch in the glass.

The tripod toppled sideways. The camera struck the concrete and rolled as far as the mount allowed.

"Back!" Rauf shouted.

The man in the gray shirt was already running toward it. At the shout he stopped, took one more step from momentum, then backed away.

Asgar moved away from the trailer toward the gates, watching the booth. It stood at the edge of the yard. The nearest tripod leg lay in front of it, the other two turned off to the side.

The lower end of the third was gone.

Asgar stopped beside Rauf.

"Did you see that?"

"Let's move farther back."

"Did you see the leg?"

The investigator took him by the elbow and led him away. He did not let go until they reached the fence.

The booth was no longer wavering. The roof of the service bay reflected in its glass. Down by the red base, the hollow tripod tube showed pale—open, shortened. The rubber foot had disappeared along with part of the metal.

The man in the gray shirt took out his phone, photographed the stump, and enlarged the image.

"It didn't fold," he said.

Rauf looked at the screen.

"Save that. And stay here."

Asgar leaned toward the phone too. The cut was slanted. A thin bright edge and the dark hollow inside were visible.

A minute ago, that leg had been resting on the concrete.

He straightened and looked at the man. At his dirty knee, at the case he was still holding by the handle.

The booth began to ring.

No one moved.

Rauf took out his own phone and briefly ordered that no one be allowed onto the site. Asgar stood beside him; with every new ring, he clenched his teeth harder. For two days he had been walking across empty concrete, leaving food by the door, speaking to his children over the phone. All that time, he had imagined it was enough to choose the right place and keep everyone else away.

"Can I stay here longer?" he asked.

Rauf put the mobile away.

"You're staying here today."

"A week? A month?"

"No. Today we'll move everyone beyond the gates. Tomorrow we'll think further. Next time it may appear anywhere in relation to you; we can't keep an empty circle of unknown size clear for weeks."

"What if next time it appears by the gates?"

The investigator looked at the booth.

"Then we'll clear the gates too. As long as we can, we'll keep everything around it empty."

He sent the man with the case toward the car, then turned to Asgar.

"You can wait. I'm not forcing you inside."

Asgar rubbed the back of his neck. The booth was ringing across the entire yard.

"I know."

He walked along the fence and called Katya.

"It appeared," he said as soon as Katya answered. "I'm far away. Still far away."

"What happened?"

Asgar told her about the camera. Katya asked him to explain again what he meant by "part of the leg disappeared." He repeated it, trying to speak more slowly.

"Did the man get away in time?"

"He left earlier. Before it appeared."

The other end went quiet.

"I'm thinking of going in," Asgar said.

He looked at the concrete beneath his feet. A flattened bottle cap lay near his shoe.

"But you can stay there."

"Today—I can."

"Then stay. Don't do it now. You just saw this, you don't even know what happened."

"On Friday she said it could be dangerous near the boundary. At the time I thought... I don't know what I thought. The column stayed intact."

"And now you want to go inside?"

He heard his own decision the way it sounded to her: metal had been cut beside a dangerous thing, and now he was going to shut himself inside it.

"If I stay, we just move the risk to the next appearance. Today we managed to move people away. Next time we may not."

"That still doesn't mean you have to go in. Asgar, you don't even know what it is."

"I don't. I'll ask first what it would actually change."

"And you don't know when you'll come out either."

He did not answer.

Katya drew in a loud breath.

"Are you calling to tell me or to ask me?"

Asgar raised his head. Rauf was standing off to the side, facing the yard.

"To tell you that I want to try. I promised I'd call before I went near it."

"I remember what you promised. The fact that you called doesn't make this easier."

"Katya..."

"Wait. Let me at least think."

He waited. The ringing in the booth stopped, and the engine beyond the fence became audible again. Asgar gripped the wire mesh with his fingers.

"Maybe talk to her again from outside?" Katya asked. "Like in the parking garage. Find out first."

"I will."

"And if she tells you something different, you don't have to do what you've decided just now."

"All right."

Katya was silent for a moment.

"I don't agree. You understand?"

"Yes."

"Just don't say afterward that we decided this."

Asgar closed his eyes.

"I won't."

He wanted to hear the children. He had already drawn breath to ask and could not. He would have to answer again when are you coming home.

"Do you have the charger?" Katya asked.

"In the trailer."

"Is your phone charged?"

He looked at the screen.

"Almost full."

"Take it with you."

"It's on me."

She said nothing, but did not hang up either. Asgar listened to her breathing until standing by the fence became unbearable.

"I love you."

"I love you too. Call me when you come out. Right away."

"Right away."

He waited until Katya ended the call, then kept the phone near his face for a little longer.

---

Rauf listened without interrupting.

"I'll talk first," Asgar finished. "If nothing has changed, I'll go in."

The investigator glanced toward the trailer.

"Do you have your documents with you?"

Asgar patted his back pocket.

"Yes."

"What should I tell your wife if the connection drops?"

Despite the sun, the question made him cold.

"That I'll call her."

Rauf did not look away.

"All right."

Asgar put the mobile away. Before the screen went dark, he caught the time: 11:11.

He walked across the yard.

As he got closer, a small scratch beside the door handle became visible. He had not noticed it at the stadium. Not in the parking garage either. His gaze stayed on it longer than on anything else: an ordinary mark in the paint, as if someone had once missed awkwardly with a key.

The fallen camera lay to the left. Asgar kept to the right, away from the tripod. He stood by the door for a moment, listening, then took hold of the handle.

The door opened heavily. Warm paint smell drifted from inside.

The telephone hung on the right. Asgar held the door with his shoulder, lifted the receiver, and remained in front of the threshold.

"It's me."

"Asgar, I can hear you."

The familiar voice made him want to shout.

"Did you see what happened over there?"

"Tell me."

"Your booth cut off a piece of a metal leg. From the camera. A man was setting it up here."

The woman did not answer immediately.

"Was anyone hurt?"

"No. Because he moved away."

"Step away from the walls. And don't touch the leg—leave it as it is."

Asgar looked at the empty yard. Rauf was by the fence, far away.

"If I go in, will the appearances here stop?"

"While you're inside, the cabin won't be here. I don't know what will happen after you come out."

"So then it starts all over again?"

"Maybe. That's exactly what we're trying to bring to an end."

He gripped the door handle so tightly that his fingers hurt.

"Do you even understand that I'm leaving my family here?"

"Yes."

"No, you're going to tell me 'maybe' again, and that's it. And I'll come out... When will I come out?"

The woman spoke more quietly.

"I don't know. If I knew, I'd tell you."

Asgar looked at his shoes. Only a few centimeters remained to the threshold. He could put the receiver back, return to Rauf. By evening, ask them to bring another folding cot. Tomorrow, call home from this yard again.

To the left, the open end of the metal tube gleamed on the concrete.

"What do I do?" he asked.

"If you've decided to go—step fully inside. Close the door from within. Don't hang up the receiver yet."

He moved his shoulder away from the door, held it with his hand, and stepped over the threshold.

Inside he had to turn sideways. His elbow brushed the wall, the cord lay across his chest. Asgar shifted one foot and turned toward the exit.

Rauf raised a hand.

Asgar nodded to him and pulled the door.

Just before it clicked shut, he could still see a narrow strip of the yard: concrete, the far gates, the edge of a white car. Then the door settled into the frame.

"Closed."

"Stay where you are."

Asgar had only touched the mobile in his pocket when the floor seemed to drop briefly beneath his heels.

He swayed, struck his shoulder against the glass, and put out his free hand.

"What just—"

The voice in the receiver broke into several dry clicks.

Asgar pressed it closer to his ear.

"Can you hear me?"

No more words came from the receiver.

He waited, counting breaths, lost count, and started again. Milky light stood beyond the glass. His own reflection made it hard to see the yard; Asgar leaned toward the window and shielded his eyes with his hand.

A shadow passed outside.

He jerked toward the handle.

The door gave.

The receiver was silent. Asgar hung it on the cradle and opened the door wider.

---

The first thing he saw was a tree.

The trunk stood almost directly opposite the exit. Around it was a circular grate packed with dry leaves. Asgar stopped, still holding the door, and looked higher.

The crown covered several floors of an unfamiliar building.

He stepped out.

Instead of concrete, there were pale paving tiles beneath his feet. People were moving across them. A woman carrying a bag walked around him, looking irritably at the open door. After taking several steps, she glanced back at the red wall as though only now noticing that it stood directly in the middle of the walkway. Behind her stretched a row of trees; farther on, a road was visible.

Asgar turned left, toward where the gates should have been.

Glass entrance doors. A man with a paper cup. A bicycle by a rack.

"Rauf!"

The man with the cup turned around.

Asgar started toward him, took two steps, and stopped. He went back to the booth.

It was already gone.

The paving continued through the place where the red body had stood. He stepped onto it with his hands held out. Went a little farther, then turned around. The woman who had walked around him had stopped by the crossing and was looking back. The bag hung from her lowered hand.

"Wait!"

She was already looking at him. Then she shifted her gaze to the empty space by the tree and took out her phone.

Asgar rushed back to the trunk. He went around it and looked behind it even though there was no way the booth could be hidden there. A crumpled receipt lay beside the grate.

He looked up at the facade as if the red door might somehow be higher.

"Damn it..."

The man with the cup set it on the edge of a trash bin and came a few steps closer.

"Was it just standing here?"

Asgar did not answer. He took out his phone and called Katya. The call did not go through. He tried again, then opened Rauf's number. Same result.

The sun warmed the back of his head. The collar of his T-shirt stuck to his neck. The shoulder he had hit inside still hurt.

He needed to find a network. Someone else's phone. Anyone who could tell him where he was.

Asgar walked over to the man.

"Excuse me. Can I make a call? I have no service."

The man's gaze shifted to his mobile.

"To whom?"

"My wife. One call."

"Do you know the number?"

Asgar started to dictate it and stopped.

Across the road stood a tower that had not been there. He recognized the curve of the street, the distant slope, but so much new construction filled the spaces between that he had to search for familiar things piece by piece.

"What's today's date?"

The man leaned slightly away.

"The twenty-fourth."

"August?"

"Yes."

Asgar exhaled. Only the location. Maybe only the location.

"And the year?"

Now the man looked at him closely.

"Are you feeling all right?"

"Tell me the year."

"Two thousand thirty-nine."

Asgar waited for him to correct himself.

A long, low car passed along the road. Two girls laughed by the crossing. The man held his cup by the upper edge, little finger bent away from the hot cardboard.

"What?" Asgar asked.

"Thirty-nine."

"No. Today is August twenty-fourth, two thousand fourteen."

The man set the cup on the edge of a street bin.

"Why don't you sit down. There's a—"

Asgar stepped back.

"No."

He looked at his own phone. The old date was still on the screen. Sunday. August twenty-fourth. He shoved it almost into the man's face.

"Look."

The man looked at the screen. His eyes moved over the phone's casing and back to the numbers.

"You can set any date on a phone."

Asgar lowered his hand.

Behind the man, beneath the shelter of a bus stop, a display was glowing. Asgar walked toward it without answering the question about whether he needed help. He waited for the advertisement to change. Above the route list, a date appeared.

24.08.2039.

He stood there until the advertisement came back.

Then waited once more.

The numbers stayed the same.

Asgar pressed a hand over his mouth. He tried to subtract fourteen from thirty-nine and could not hold the numbers in his head. He saw Katya at the kitchen table; he could hear her asking him to check the charger. That had been only minutes ago.

Twenty-five years.

He sat on the edge of the bench, missed the open space, and bumped someone else's bag. The woman sitting beside it pulled it closer to herself. Asgar apologized, but almost no voice came out.

The younger one would already be an adult.

The thought made him stand.

He opened Katya's number again and pressed call. He watched the screen until it told him the connection had failed. Tried again.

Then he turned toward the tree.

People moved between him and the bus stop. There was no red anywhere. Asgar went back, stood in the place where he had let go of the door, and did not move for several seconds.

He was supposed to call immediately.

Asgar put the phone in his pocket and looked down the street, trying to work out which direction home was.
