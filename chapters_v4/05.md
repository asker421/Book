Chapter 5. In a Few Seconds

One leg of the folding cot kept collapsing.

Asgar found a broken piece of tile under the table, wedged it beneath the hinge, and sat down carefully. The metal held. The moment he lay back, something scraped against the wall and he had to get up again.

He was no longer sleepy.

He pulled on his jeans, slipped his phone into his pocket, and stepped out of the site trailer. Dust lay across the steps; overnight it had worked itself even into the grooves of the rubber mat. Asgar went down, holding the door so it would not slam.

Sunday morning at the maintenance yard began without people. An empty concrete lot, closed bays, brown gates at the far end. Some kind of motor was running beyond the fence. Every so often it coughed and died, and in the silence that followed he could hear pigeons on the roof.

On Friday they had brought him the cot, water, and a small refrigerator that at first smelled stronger than the food inside it. Katya’s bag stood under the table. Yesterday Asgar had found a packet of biscuits tucked between the T-shirts; the top was already open and folded shut with a binder clip. He had eaten two and hidden it again.

Rauf was walking from the gate with two paper cups.

“You’re early,” Asgar said.

“It’s almost ten.”

“I meant for a Sunday.”

Rauf handed him a coffee. The lid had not snapped down on one side; Asgar pressed it into place with his thumb.

“Sleep?”

“The furniture wouldn’t let me.”

The investigator looked toward the open trailer door.

“You should’ve said something yesterday.”

“Yesterday it was standing up.”

They moved away from the steps. The mark of an old wheel ran across the concrete and ended at a welded-over drain grate. In two days Asgar had memorized the cracks around it. He would walk from the trailer to the far bay, turn around, walk back. People remained posted by the perimeter, though they tried not to come close.

A man in a gray shirt was crouched beside the camera, tightening the mount on a tripod. They had placed the camera at the edge of the lot and aimed it toward the area where Asgar spent most of the day.

“He’ll leave when he’s done?” Asgar asked.

“When he’s finished, he’ll move back.”

The man looked up.

“All set.”

He stood, dusted off one knee, and carried an empty case toward the gate. Asgar watched him go. Ever since the woman had said forty-seven hours, he had been constantly measuring distance in his head: to the nearest person, to the wall, to any place where a car could be stopped.

Even in sleep he saw the parking garage, except every time there was someone standing between the pillar and the booth.

Rauf drank some coffee.

“Your wife call?”

“I’ll ring her now.”

“I’ll be by the entrance. If you notice anything, move away immediately and call out. Don’t go closer just to get it on camera.”

Asgar nodded and waited until the investigator had walked off. Then he called Katya.

She took a while to answer.

“You awake?”

“Have been for ages. What’s happening there?”

“We’re having an argument about whether Sunday means breakfast is optional.”

Somewhere in the background, the younger boy objected. Katya’s voice moved farther from the phone, but Asgar still heard her:

“Biscuits count as food too. That is exactly why I asked you not to take them yet.”

He smiled at his coffee cup.

“Who’s winning?”

“No one so far. How are you?”

Asgar told her about the cot. Katya said there should be a brace on the side: he had to push it all the way down, otherwise the leg was only holding under its own weight.

“How do you know that?”

“Because you just told me about the tile. Look at the side. There should be a brace.”

He turned toward the trailer.

“Found it.”

“Well, that’s progress.”

Asgar laughed. Then she asked whether everything was still quiet, and he said yes.

For a while they talked about the children. The older one needed new shoes. The younger one wanted to visit Dad because he had decided Dad was living in a real construction cabin where he was allowed to touch everything.

Asgar listened to Katya moving around the kitchen, turning on the tap, shifting dishes. There was no way to insert what he had been thinking about since Friday into that conversation without tearing it apart.

“Today should make things clearer,” he said at last.

The water stopped.

“What exactly?”

“Whether it comes back.”

“You’ll call me?”

“Of course.”

He nearly added *don’t worry*, but instead asked whether she wanted to put the younger one on. His son took the phone and spent a while breathing directly into the microphone.

“Dad, is there a crane?”

“No. Just garages.”

“When are you coming home?”

Asgar rubbed the toe of his shoe along a crack in the concrete.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Mom says that too.”

“Because we don’t know yet.”

The boy went quiet, then started talking about a toy car with a hood that opened. Asgar kept asking questions until the voice drifted away from the phone; the child had run off to show the car to his brother.

By the time the call ended, the coffee was cold.

---

When the camera fell, Asgar’s first thought was that the man had set the tripod up badly.

He was sitting on the bottom step of the trailer. The shade had just moved off his knees, and he was about to shift the chair around to the other side when he heard a sharp metallic snap.

The camera tilted.

One tripod leg jerked upward; the others skidded across the concrete.

Behind it, a vertical red strip appeared.

Asgar jumped to his feet.

For several moments he could still see the gate and Rauf walking from it through the red. Then the color widened and covered part of the fence.

A wall formed.

The sky reflected in the glass as a white patch.

The tripod toppled sideways. The camera struck the concrete and rolled as far as its mount allowed.

“Back!” Rauf shouted.

The man in the gray shirt had already started running toward it. At the shout, he stopped, took one more step on momentum, then retreated.

Asgar was moving away from the trailer toward the gate, eyes fixed on the booth. It stood near the edge of the lot. The nearest tripod leg lay in front of it; the other two had twisted sideways.

The lower end of the third leg was gone.

Asgar stopped beside Rauf.

“Did you see that?”

“Farther back.”

“Did you see the leg?”

The investigator took him by the elbow and moved him away. He let go only when they reached the fence.

The booth no longer flickered. The roof of one of the bays reflected in its glass. Low beside the red base, the hollow tube of the tripod leg showed pale and open—shorter now.

The rubber foot had vanished along with part of the metal.

The man in the gray shirt took out his phone, photographed the severed end, and zoomed in.

“It didn’t fold,” he said.

Rauf looked at the screen.

“Save that. And stay here.”

Asgar leaned toward the phone too. The cut was diagonal. A thin bright rim showed around the dark hollow inside.

A minute earlier, that leg had been resting on the concrete.

He straightened and looked at the man. At the dirty knee of his trousers, at the case he was still carrying by its handle.

A telephone began ringing inside the booth.

No one moved.

Rauf took out his own phone and gave a brief instruction not to let anyone onto the grounds. Asgar stood beside him; with every new ring his jaw tightened harder.

For two days he had paced empty concrete, left food by the door, spoken to his children by phone. All that time he had been telling himself that if they chose the right place and kept everyone else far enough away, that would be enough.

“Can I stay here longer?” he asked.

Rauf put his phone away.

“You’re staying here today.”

“A week? A month?”

“No. Today we move everyone outside the gate. Tomorrow we think again. Next time it can appear anywhere relative to you. We can’t keep an empty circle of unknown size around you for weeks.”

“What if next time it appears at the gate?”

The investigator looked at the booth.

“Then we clear the gate too. As long as we can, we clear everything around you.”

He sent the man with the case toward the car, then turned back to Asgar.

“You can wait. I’m not forcing you inside.”

Asgar rubbed the back of his head.

“I know.”

The booth rang across the whole yard.

He walked along the fence and called Katya.

“It appeared,” he said as soon as she answered. “I’m far away. Still far.”

“What happened?”

Asgar told her about the camera. Katya asked him to explain again what he meant by part of the leg being gone. He repeated it, trying to speak more slowly.

“The man got away?”

“He was already clear. Before it appeared.”

Silence on the other end.

“I’m thinking of going in,” Asgar said.

He stared at the concrete beneath his shoes. A flattened bottle cap lay beside his toe.

“You can stay there.”

“Today I can.”

“Then stay. Don’t do anything now. You only just saw this. You don’t even know what happened.”

“She told me on Friday it could be dangerous near the boundary. Back then I thought… I don’t know what I thought. The pillar was still whole afterward.”

“And now you want to go inside?”

He heard his own decision the way it sounded to her: metal had just been severed beside a dangerous thing, and now he wanted to shut himself inside it.

“If I stay, we just move the risk to the next appearance. Today they got people out in time. Next time they might not.”

“That still doesn’t mean you have to go in. Asgar, you don’t even know that woman.”

“I know. I’ll ask first what going in is actually supposed to change.”

“And you don’t know when you’ll come out.”

He did not answer.

Katya drew in a hard breath.

“Are you calling to tell me or to ask me?”

Asgar lifted his head. Rauf stood off to one side, facing the lot.

“To tell you I’m thinking of trying. I promised I’d call before I went near the door.”

“I remember what you promised. The fact that you called doesn’t make this easier.”

“Kat…”

“Wait. Give me at least a second to think.”

He waited.

The ringing inside the booth stopped, and the motor beyond the fence became audible again. Asgar hooked his fingers into the mesh.

“Maybe talk to her from outside again?” Katya said. “Like you did in the parking garage. Find out first.”

“I will.”

“And if she tells you something different, you don’t have to do what you’ve already decided in your head.”

“Okay.”

Katya was quiet for a moment.

“I don’t agree. You understand that?”

“Yes.”

“Then don’t tell yourself afterward that we decided this together.”

Asgar closed his eyes.

“I won’t.”

He wanted to hear the children. He even drew breath to ask, then could not do it. He would have to answer the same question again: *When are you coming home?*

“You have your charger?” Katya asked.

“In the trailer.”

“Phone charged?”

He looked at the screen.

“Almost full.”

“Take it with you.”

“It’s on me.”

She said nothing, but she did not hang up either. Asgar listened to her breathing until standing at the fence became unbearable.

“I love you.”

“I love you too. Call me when you come out. Immediately.”

“Immediately.”

He waited for Katya to end the call, then held the phone by his face for a little longer.

---

Rauf listened without interrupting.

“I’ll talk to her first,” Asgar finished. “If nothing’s changed, I’ll go in.”

The investigator glanced toward the trailer.

“You have your documents?”

Asgar patted his back pocket.

“Yes.”

“What do I tell your wife if the connection drops?”

The question made him cold despite the sun.

“That I’ll call her.”

Rauf did not look away.

“All right.”

Asgar put the mobile away. Before the screen went dark, he saw the time: 11:11.

He crossed the lot.

As he approached, a small scratch beside the door handle came into view. He had not noticed it at the stadium. Or in the parking garage. His eyes stayed on it longer than on anything else: an ordinary mark in the paint, as though someone had once missed with a key.

The fallen camera lay to the left. Asgar kept to the right, away from the tripod. He stopped by the door and listened, then took hold of the handle.

The door opened heavily.

Warm paint breathed out from inside.

The telephone hung on the right. Asgar held the panel with his shoulder, lifted the receiver, and remained outside the threshold.

“It’s me.”

“Asgar, I hear you.”

The familiar voice made him want to shout.

“Did you see what just happened?”

“Tell me.”

“Your booth cut through a metal tripod leg. A camera tripod. A man was setting it up here.”

The woman did not answer immediately.

“Was anyone hurt?”

“No. Because he’d moved away.”

“Step away from the walls. And do not touch the tripod leg. Leave it where it is.”

Asgar looked across the empty lot. Rauf was by the fence, far away.

“If I go inside, will the appearances here stop?”

“While you’re inside, the booth won’t be here. What happens after you come out, I don’t know.”

“So it may all start again?”

“It may. We’re trying to finish the process.”

He gripped the door handle until his fingers hurt.

“Do you have any idea what I’m leaving behind here? I have a family.”

“Yes.”

“No. You’re going to say ‘possibly’ again and that’ll be the end of it. And I’ll come out… When will I come out?”

The woman’s voice became quieter.

“I don’t know. If I knew, I would tell you.”

Asgar looked at his shoes. The threshold was only a few centimeters away.

He could put the receiver back. Walk to Rauf. Ask for another cot by evening. Call home from this lot again tomorrow.

To his left, the open end of the severed metal tube shone on the concrete.

“What do I do?” he asked.

“If you’ve decided to proceed, step fully inside. Close the door from within. Keep the receiver off the hook for now.”

He moved his shoulder away from the panel, held it with one hand, and crossed the threshold.

Inside, he had to turn sideways. His elbow brushed the wall; the cord lay across his chest. Asgar shifted one foot and turned toward the opening.

Rauf raised a hand.

Asgar nodded and pulled the door.

Just before it clicked shut, he could still see a narrow slice of the yard: concrete, the far gate, the edge of a white car.

Then the panel settled into the frame.

“Closed.”

“Stay where you are.”

Asgar had only touched the mobile in his pocket when the floor seemed to drop briefly beneath his heels.

He swayed, struck his shoulder against the glass, put out his free hand.

“What just—”

The woman’s voice broke into several dry clicks.

Asgar pressed the receiver harder to his ear.

“Can you hear me?”

No more words came through.

He waited, counting breaths, lost count, started again. Milky light stood beyond the glass. His reflection made the yard difficult to see; Asgar leaned toward the pane and shaded his eyes with one hand.

A shadow moved outside.

He jerked toward the handle.

The door opened.

The receiver remained silent. Asgar hung it back on the cradle and opened the door wider.

---

The first thing he saw was a tree.

Its trunk stood almost directly opposite the opening. Around it was a circular metal grate clogged with dry leaves. Asgar stopped, still holding the door, and looked up.

The crown obscured several floors of an unfamiliar building.

He stepped out.

Instead of concrete, pale paving lay beneath his feet. People were walking across it. A woman carrying a bag went around him, irritated by the open door in her way. Several steps later she looked back at the red wall, as though only then realizing it stood directly in the middle of the walkway. Beyond her shoulder stretched a line of trees, and farther away was a road.

Asgar turned left, toward where the gates should have been.

Glass entrance doors.

A man with a paper cup.

A bicycle at a rack.

“Rauf!”

The man with the cup looked over.

Asgar started toward him, took two steps, and stopped.

He turned back to the booth.

It was already gone.

Paving continued across the place where the red structure had stood.

He stepped into that space with both hands held out. Took another step, turned around. The woman who had gone around him had stopped by the crossing and was staring back. Her bag hung from one lowered hand.

“Wait!”

She was already looking at him. Then she shifted her gaze to the empty space beside the tree and took out her phone.

Asgar rushed back to the trunk. Went around it. Looked behind it, even though there was nowhere a booth could have hidden.

A crumpled receipt lay beside the grate.

He raised his eyes to the building façade as though the red door might somehow have moved upward.

“For God’s sake…”

The man with the paper cup set it on the edge of a trash bin and came a few steps closer.

“Was that just standing here?”

Asgar did not answer.

He took out his phone and called Katya.

The call would not go through.

He tried again, then opened Rauf’s number.

Same result.

The sun warmed the back of his head. The collar of his T-shirt clung to his neck. His shoulder still hurt where he had struck the booth.

He needed a network.

Someone else’s phone.

Anyone who could tell him where he was.

Asgar approached the man.

“Sorry. Can I make a call? I’ve got no service.”

The man glanced at his mobile.

“To whom?”

“My wife. One call.”

“You know the number?”

Asgar began reciting it and stopped.

Across the road stood a tower that had not been there before. He recognized the curve of the street, the distant slope, but so much had grown between them that he had to search for the familiar in pieces.

“What’s the date today?”

The man leaned back slightly.

“The twenty-fourth.”

“August?”

“Yes.”

Asgar exhaled.

Only the place, then.

Maybe only the place.

“And the year?”

Now the man studied him.

“Are you all right?”

“Tell me the year.”

“Two thousand thirty-nine.”

Asgar waited for him to correct himself.

A long, low car passed on the road. Two girls laughed near the crossing. The man held his cup by the rim, little finger lifted away from the hot cardboard.

“What?” Asgar asked.

“Thirty-nine.”

“No. Today is August twenty-fourth, two thousand fourteen.”

The man put his cup down on the edge of the street bin.

“Why don’t you sit down. There’s a—”

“I don’t need to.”

Asgar stepped back.

He looked at his own phone. The old date was still on the screen.

Sunday.

August twenty-fourth.

He thrust it almost into the man’s face.

“Look.”

The man did.

“You can set any date you want on a phone.”

Asgar lowered his hand.

Behind the man, beneath the shelter of a bus stop, a display was glowing. Asgar walked toward it without answering when the man asked whether he needed help.

He waited for an advertisement to change.

Above the route list, the date appeared.

24.08.2039.

He stood there until the advertisement returned.

Then he waited through it again.

The numbers did not change.

Asgar pressed a hand over his mouth. Tried to subtract fourteen from thirty-nine and could not keep the figures in place. He saw Katya at the kitchen table; heard her asking whether he had checked the charger.

That had been minutes ago.

Twenty-five years.

He sat on the edge of the bench, missed the open space, and knocked into someone’s bag. The woman sitting beside it pulled it closer. Asgar apologized, but almost no voice came out.

His younger son would be grown.

The thought forced him upright.

He opened Katya’s number again and pressed call. Watched the screen until it told him the connection had failed.

Tried once more.

Then he turned back toward the tree.

People moved between him and the bus stop.

No red anywhere.

Asgar returned, stood in the place where he had let go of the door, and did not move for several seconds.

He had promised to call immediately.

Asgar put the phone in his pocket and looked along the street, trying to work out which way home was.
