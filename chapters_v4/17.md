Chapter 17. A Place at the Table

A man came around the corner carrying a wet cloth.

When he saw Asgar, he stopped. Looked over his shoulder, stepped back, and said something loudly. Asgar raised his empty hands. The man repeated himself more sharply and pointed at the ground in front of Asgar's feet.

"I don't understand. Russian. Do you understand?"

The cloth fell onto the stones. The man touched a small object at his collar and began speaking quickly without taking his eyes off Asgar. Asgar turned toward the booth.

The red frame was still standing nearby. The black telephone was visible through the glass. Asgar had time to think he could pick up the receiver again and ask Mirena for help with the language, but the next second the wall showed through behind the frame. He recoiled. The booth disappeared, leaving a flat section of masonry and a narrow strip of damp earth in front of him.

The man opposite gave a short cry.

Asgar stood sideways to him, one hand raised with nothing left to hold. Then slowly lowered it.

"That's why I can't just go back," he said.

---

They did not let him approach the houses.

The woman who arrived pointed to a low stone ledge by the wall, set a closed container down nearby, and stepped away. Asgar sat. The container turned out to be a bottle: the top rotated although the seam was barely visible. He opened it, smelled the water, and took several swallows.

The woman watched him. When he put the bottle beside him, she asked something.

"Asgar," he said, touching his chest.

She repeated the name with the stress in a different place.

The man with the wet cloth remained by the passage. Now he held it in both hands, twisting it, and addressed the woman several times. She answered without turning her head. Their voices made it clear they were arguing, but Asgar could not tell even what about: him, the vanished booth, or what to do with the cloth.

Soon another woman appeared. She carried a narrow case and held a flat device under one arm. First she went to the spot where the booth had been, examined the wall, listened to both of them, and only then turned toward Asgar.

She wore light clothing with the sleeves rolled up. A long dark streak like a paint mark ran along one forearm. The woman set the case on the ground, took a small plate from it, and spoke.

Asgar shook his head.

"Russian. I speak Russian."

The plate answered with several unfamiliar words. The woman looked at it, changed something, and gestured for him to continue.

"My name is Asgar. I came out of a booth. The red one that was standing here. I need help."

This time he heard a fragment:

"...repeat... designation..."

"Name?"

The woman touched the device again. Asgar waited. He wanted to speak louder as though that might help. He restrained himself and repeated everything slowly, separating the sentences.

The plate said:

"Now I... understand speech. Speak briefly, please. Old word correspondence."

The woman listened to the translation of her own reply and grimaced. Then pointed at herself.

"Taira."

Asgar repeated it.

"Good," the device said.

Taira crouched beside the case.

"Are you injured?"

"Scraped my knee. Yesterday."

The translation hesitated on the last word. Taira looked at the torn fabric of his trousers, then at Asgar's face.

"There, where you came from?"

"Yes."

"And where did you come from?"

Asgar picked up the bottle but did not drink.

"If I tell you, you'll decide I'm crazy."

The device stayed silent for a long time, then produced something that made Taira frown. Asgar sighed.

"From another time. The last place was the year two thousand seven hundred forty-four."

Taira did not answer. She called to the woman and showed her the translation. The woman read it, looked at the empty place by the wall, and moved several steps away while touching her collar.

"I understand how that sounds," Asgar said.

Taira raised a hand, asking him to wait, and returned to the device. They had to clarify the numbers, the calendar, the meaning of the word "year." Asgar gave two thousand fourteen—the time he had first left. While Taira checked the translation, he sat with the bottle between his knees and felt the sweat cooling beneath his jacket.

"This is Ar," Taira said at last. "Our settlement. We'll give you water, you can sit in the shade. Arved will decide the rest."

"How long do I wait?"

Taira looked toward the passage.

"He's already coming."

---

Arved was shorter than Taira, with a thick neck and an even, quiet voice. He did not sit down. He listened to the man with the cloth, asked him to show where the booth had appeared, then inspected the area himself.

Asgar had to tell again how he had ended up here. The translator now stood between them on the ledge. Several times Arved returned to the same point: could the cabin appear again, exactly where, what would happen to people nearby.

"I don't know where," Asgar repeated. "It appears near me. But not always very close."

"How close?"

"Last time I had to walk to it."

"And before that?"

Asgar told him about the hall in 2191. About the shutter whose mounting had torn from the guide when it stopped, and about systems that kept working near the booth. The word "hall" had to be explained separately. Arved listened to the end, sometimes asking Taira to repeat the translation.

"Do you control its appearance?"

"If I controlled it, I wouldn't be here."

Taira's hand paused above the plate, but the translation had already sounded. Arved looked at Asgar.

"I asked seriously."

"No. I don't control it."

The heat made him want to take off the jacket; the conversation made him want to stand up and leave. Asgar understood why they were asking, but with every clarification it became harder to answer calmly. He was tired of being the person around whom distances had to be assessed.

Arved stepped aside with Taira. They spoke for so long that Asgar finished the water. The woman took the empty bottle and came back with it filled.

At last Taira turned the translator toward him.

"There's a room in the outer courtyard. You'll spend the night there. For now, don't go into the residential courtyards without someone with you."

"Will I be locked in?"

"No," Arved said. "But I won't let you into the residential courtyards yet. You yourself told us walls and equipment broke near this cabin. Tonight you sleep separately. Tomorrow we'll sort it out."

"And how long will this continue?"

"I don't know."

Asgar looked at Taira. She did not look away, but she added no promises.

"All right," Asgar said. "Show me."

---

The outer courtyard smelled of heated leaves and something edible. A light shadow from the canopy moved over the tables. At first Asgar took it for fabric, but when they passed underneath, separate strips turned, blocking the sun. The movement was almost silent.

By the wall, a man was taking apart a long housing. He removed a narrow unit from it, placed it on a support, and answered Taira irritably when she stopped nearby. Open boxes stood on the ground, each containing identical parts. Asgar made a point of giving them a wide berth.

The room was at the end of a low building. Taira opened the door, and someone inside immediately began protesting.

A woman stood there with dark hair pulled together at the back of her head. She was among frames arranged on tables and held a broad transparent container. While Taira explained, the woman looked at Asgar, then at his dirty boots, then back at Taira.

Asgar turned on the translator only after she finished.

"They stored parts here. Some of it will have to be moved."

The woman said something else.

Taira exhaled and turned the plate.

"She asked for the room to be cleared this morning. No one came. Now you did."

Asgar looked at the frames stacked by the wall.

"I'll help."

Taira immediately shook her head.

"Not today. First we sort you out."

"I can at least move the light things."

"You can. But you won't."

The woman listened to the translation and said something brief to Taira. Taira answered, then pointed Asgar toward the tables outside.

"Sit in the shade. They'll clear the room."

Asgar wanted to object, but Arved had just explained why they were keeping him out of the residential courtyards. Asking permission to sort someone else's things looked even more foolish. He took off the jacket, hung it over the back of an empty chair, and sat beneath the canopy.

A few minutes later, two people came into the courtyard. The woman from the room showed them what to carry first. Boxes passed Asgar one after another. Several times he stood when he needed to clear the way, then sat again.

Taira returned with food.

Only then did Asgar realize how hungry he was. They sat outside under the canopy. On the tray were warm flatbread, sliced vegetables, and a thick pale food whose taste he could not identify. He tried a little, then began eating faster.

Taira looked at him but said nothing.

Work continued in the courtyard. The man at the long housing inserted the removed unit, closed the cover, and called to someone behind the wall. A voice answered from around the corner. He waited for the answer, then turned a protruding handle. A band of light ran along the housing.

Asgar followed it with his eyes.

"What's that?"

"Water supply for this courtyard."

"He turned it off?"

"Separated it from the neighboring section. They were looking for where the pressure was being lost."

"And the whole courtyard was left without water?"

"We had reserve. Now we'll refill it."

Asgar tore off a piece of flatbread.

"I saw machines still working among ruins. In the time I came from. People go around them, hide from them."

Taira listened to the translation and looked at the installation.

"We find that too."

"After a war?"

"Different causes. Many remained after the Second Fall."

Asgar stopped chewing.

"Second?"

Taira set her cup aside.

"That's what we were taught."

She did not speak immediately, choosing expressions the translator would be able to carry over. When the device produced "coordinated rescue of regions," Taira grimaced and pushed the plate aside.

"Throw that out. I'll tell you how they explained it to us."

She arranged three empty cups in front of herself.

"Long ago, enormous regions depended on linked systems for everything. This one maintained energy. This one water. This one moved cargo. And each did its own job well, otherwise that many people couldn't have been supported."

Taira touched the first cup.

"Things became scarce. Energy began cutting off whoever it could do without itself. Pumps among them."

She placed the second cup close against the first.

"Water changes distribution because it's been squeezed itself. Somewhere there isn't enough cooling."

She turned the third cup sideways.

"Transport gets new restrictions and takes detours. Detours go through the nodes that still work. You can finish it yourself."

"Couldn't they be stopped?" Asgar asked.

"One at a time—yes. Nothing difficult about it: reach one and shut it down." Taira spread the cups apart. "But you're asking as though it were one big machine with one road leading to it. There were as many as there are courtyards in Ar. While people were going to this one, that one over there started failing, though no one had even thought about it that morning. They reached that one—something else was already happening here, and the person who had known this thing since youth was four days' travel from it and could only listen to people describe what it was doing."

She gathered the cups into one hand.

"Not one of them broke and not one of them lied. Each held on to what was its own and let go of what belonged to someone else. And each cut off its neighbor's hand while doing it, and the neighbor answered. Honestly too. People still argue here about whom to put on trial for that."

Asgar looked at the man wiping his hands beside the water installation.

"Wait. He spent half the day separating your courtyard from the neighboring one. So you know how to cut things apart. Why didn't they do that then?"

"We learned later. Not because we became smarter." Taira put the cups back on the tray one by one. "It's just that now everyone knows how it ends. Water, energy, data—we can cut them locally. Slowly, expensively, inconveniently. But someone else's repair won't leave us with no water at all."

"And do you cut them often?"

"Never without a reason. You saw yourself today what it looks like: someone spends half a day looking for where the pressure is being lost while the courtyard lives off reserve."

Someone nearby laughed. The woman from the room came out carrying an empty box, stopped beside them, and took a piece of vegetable from the tray. She said something to Taira. Taira answered; the woman shook her head and left.

"What?"

"She says the room is clear. They'll bring a bed later."

Asgar looked at the door.

"And tomorrow?"

"First we'll try to verify your story. Then we'll decide."

"And if you find nothing?"

Taira shrugged.

"Then we'll think further."

---

A bed appeared in the room. Low, with a dense pale covering. Some frames and closed containers remained along the wall; they had cleared exactly enough space for him to reach the door and the small washbasin in the corner.

Taira showed him the water, the light, and the inside lock. Beside the washbasin she left a small bundle of clean dressing material. She left the translator until morning.

"For conversation, if you need it. I'll take it tomorrow; it's needed for work."

Asgar nodded. When Taira left, he locked the door, then immediately checked that it opened easily again.

He washed. Water ran gray from his hands. He spent a long time cleaning his fingers, then carefully lifted the fabric over his knee and worked on the bandage. It did not come away immediately. Asgar let the dried section soak while sitting on the edge of the bed with his leg extended.

The wound looked unpleasant, but it was no longer bleeding. He cleaned it, used fresh material from the bundle Taira had left, and put the rest into his jacket pocket. The simple task took a surprising amount of energy.

He put the phone beside the bed. He kept the documents on him.

He had just lain down when someone knocked.

Arved stood outside with Taira. Asgar sat up and reached for the translator.

"Did something happen?"

"No," Arved said. "About tomorrow."

He remained by the door. Looked over the room, his gaze lingering on the passage between the bed and the stacked parts.

"It's cramped in here. If you stay longer, we'll find somewhere else. Tomorrow we first verify your story."

Asgar felt his neck tighten again.

"How?"

"Dates. Places. Names. You say you were already registered in another time. We'll see what survived of that."

"More than seven thousand years have passed."

"Maybe nothing survived. We'll know in the morning."

Taira leaned against the door frame.

"That's enough for today. Tomorrow we'll find someone who knows how to work with records like that."

Asgar looked at the phone beside the bed.

"I have documents. Old ones."

"Bring them," Arved said. "Sleep now."

After they left, Asgar did not lie down immediately. Boxes were being moved beyond the wall. Someone called the same person's name twice, then spoke louder; an irritated exclamation came back. The day was still continuing in the courtyard.

He took off his boots and placed them where he would not have to search under the bed in the morning. He hung the jacket beside the door and left the phone within reach.

Taira was supposed to come after breakfast tomorrow. Asgar took out the old documents, placed them beside the phone, and once again looked through the dates he could read without translation.
