Chapter 17. A Place at the Table

A man came around the corner carrying a wet cloth.

When he saw Asgar, he stopped. Looked past his shoulder, stepped back, and shouted something.

Asgar raised his empty hands.

The man repeated himself more sharply and pointed to the ground in front of Asgar’s feet.

“I don’t understand. Russian. Do you understand Russian?”

The cloth dropped onto the stones.

The man touched a small object at his collar and spoke quickly without taking his eyes off Asgar.

Asgar turned toward the booth.

The red frame was still there.

Through the glass he could see the black telephone. He had just enough time to think he could pick up the receiver again and ask Mirena for help with the language—

then the wall began to show through behind the frame.

He recoiled.

The booth vanished, leaving smooth masonry and a narrow strip of damp earth.

The man opposite him gave a short cry.

Asgar stood sideways to him with one hand still raised, reaching for something that was no longer there.

Then slowly lowered it.

“That’s why I can’t just go back,” he said.

---

They did not let him approach the houses.

A woman who arrived pointed to a low stone ledge by a wall, placed a closed vessel nearby, and stepped back.

Asgar sat.

The vessel was a bottle: its upper section turned even though there was almost no visible seam.

He opened it, smelled the water, and took several swallows.

The woman watched.

When he set the bottle down, she asked something.

“Asgar,” he said, touching his chest.

She repeated the name with a different stress.

The man with the wet cloth remained by the passage. He now held it in both hands, twisting it, and addressed the woman several times.

She answered without turning.

From their voices, it was obvious they were arguing, but Asgar could not tell even what about: him, the vanished booth, or where the cloth belonged.

Soon another woman arrived.

She carried a narrow case and held a flat device beneath one arm.

She first went to the place where the booth had stood, examined the wall, listened to both of them, and only then turned toward Asgar.

She wore pale clothing with the sleeves rolled up.

A long dark stripe like a paint mark ran along one forearm.

The woman set the case on the ground, took out a small plate, and spoke.

Asgar shook his head.

“Russian. I speak Russian.”

The plate replied with several unfamiliar words.

The woman looked at it, changed something, and gestured for him to continue.

“My name is Asgar. I came out of a booth. The red one that was standing here. I need help.”

This time he caught a fragment.

“…repeat… designation…”

“Name?”

The woman touched the device again.

Asgar waited.

He had the urge to speak louder, as though volume could help.

He restrained himself and repeated everything slowly, separating the sentences.

The plate said:

“Now I… understand speech. Speak briefly, please. Old lexical correspondence.”

The woman listened to the translation of her own reply and grimaced.

Then she pointed to herself.

“Tyra.”

Asgar repeated it.

“Good,” the device said.

Tyra crouched beside the case.

“Are you injured?”

“Scraped my knee. Yesterday.”

The translator hesitated over the last word.

Tyra looked at the damaged fabric of his trousers, then at Asgar’s face.

“There, where you came from?”

“Yes.”

“And where did you come from?”

Asgar picked up the bottle but did not drink.

“If I tell you, you’ll think I’m insane.”

The device was silent for a long time, then produced something that made Tyra frown.

Asgar sighed.

“From another time. The last place was the year 2744.”

Tyra did not answer.

She called the other woman over and showed her the translation.

The woman read it, looked at the empty space by the wall, then stepped several paces away and touched her collar.

“I know how it sounds,” Asgar said.

Tyra raised a hand, asking him to wait, and returned to the device.

They had to clarify the numbers, the calendar, the meaning of *year.*

Asgar named 2014—the time he had first left.

While Tyra checked the translation, he sat with the bottle between his knees and felt sweat cooling beneath the jacket.

“This is Ar,” Tyra finally said. “Our settlement. We’ll give you water. You can sit in the shade. Arved decides the rest.”

“How long?”

Tyra looked toward the passage.

“He’s already coming.”

---

Arved was shorter than Tyra, with a thick neck and a quiet, even voice.

He did not sit.

He listened to the man with the cloth, asked him to show where the booth had appeared, then inspected the place himself.

Asgar had to tell his story again.

This time the translator stood between them on the ledge.

Several times Arved brought the conversation back to the same points: could the booth appear again, where exactly, what happened to people standing nearby?

“I don’t know where,” Asgar repeated. “It appears near me. But not always right next to me.”

“How near?”

“Last time I had to walk to it.”

“And before that?”

Asgar told him about the hall in 2191.

About the shutter whose mounting had torn out of the rail when it stopped.

About systems that kept operating around the booth.

They had to explain the word *hall* separately.

Arved listened to the end, occasionally asking Tyra to repeat the translation.

“Do you control its appearances?”

“If I did, I wouldn’t be here.”

Tyra’s hand paused above the plate, but the translation had already sounded.

Arved looked at Asgar.

“I asked seriously.”

“No. I don’t control it.”

The heat made him want to take off the jacket.

The conversation made him want to stand and leave.

Asgar understood why they were asking.

With every clarification, it became harder to answer calmly.

He was tired of being the man people had to measure distances around.

Arved stepped aside with Tyra.

They spoke long enough for Asgar to finish the water.

The woman took the empty bottle and came back with it filled.

At last, Tyra turned the translator toward him.

“There’s a room in the outer courtyard. You can stay there tonight. Don’t go into the residential courtyards without someone accompanying you for now.”

“Are you locking me in?”

“No,” Arved said. “But I’m not letting you into the living areas yet. You told us yourself that this booth damaged walls and equipment nearby. Tonight you sleep separately. Tomorrow we work out what to do.”

“And how long does that last?”

“I don’t know.”

Asgar looked at Tyra.

She did not look away, but she added no promises.

“All right,” Asgar said. “Show me.”

---

The outer courtyard smelled of sun-warmed leaves and something edible.

A light shade moved above the tables.

At first Asgar thought it was fabric, but when they passed beneath it, separate strips turned, blocking the sun.

The movement was nearly silent.

Beside one wall, a man was taking apart a long housing.

He removed a narrow module, set it on a stand, and answered Tyra irritably when she stopped nearby.

Open boxes stood on the ground, each holding identical components.

Asgar gave them a wide berth.

His room was at the end of a low building.

Tyra opened the door, and someone inside began complaining at once.

A woman with a dark stripe of hair pulled back at the nape stood among frames arranged across tables.

She held a broad transparent container.

While Tyra explained, the woman looked at Asgar, then at his dirty boots, then back at Tyra.

Asgar activated the translator only after she finished.

“They stored components here. Some have to be moved.”

The woman said something else.

Tyra exhaled and turned the plate.

“She asked this morning for the room to be cleared. Nobody came. Now you have.”

Asgar looked at the stacked frames by the wall.

“I’ll help.”

Tyra shook her head immediately.

“Not today. First we work out what to do with you.”

“I can at least carry the light ones.”

“You can. You won’t.”

The woman listened to the translation and said something short to Tyra.

Tyra answered, then pointed Asgar toward the tables outside.

“Sit in the shade. They’ll clear the room.”

Asgar wanted to argue, but Arved had just explained why he was not allowed into the residential courtyards.

Asking permission to sort other people’s things sounded even more ridiculous.

He removed the jacket, hung it over the back of a free chair, and sat beneath the shade.

A few minutes later, two people came into the courtyard.

The woman from the room showed them what to move first.

Boxes passed Asgar one after another.

Several times he stood when they needed the path, then sat again.

Tyra returned with food.

Only then did Asgar realize how hungry he was.

They sat outside under the shade.

The tray held warm flatbread, sliced vegetables, and a thick pale food whose flavor he could not identify.

He tried a little, then began eating faster.

Tyra watched but said nothing.

Work continued in the courtyard.

The man at the long housing replaced the module he had removed, closed the cover, and called to someone beyond the wall.

A voice answered from around the corner.

He waited, then turned a protruding handle.

A band of light traveled along the housing.

Asgar watched it.

“What’s that?”

“Water supply to this courtyard.”

“He shut it off?”

“Separated it from the neighboring section. They were looking for where the pressure was dropping.”

“And the whole courtyard had no water?”

“There was stored water. Now we refill it.”

Asgar broke off a piece of flatbread.

“I saw machines still working among ruins. In the time I came from. People avoid them. Hide from them.”

Tyra listened to the translation and looked at the installation.

“We find those too.”

“After a war?”

“Different reasons. Many were left after the Second Fall.”

Asgar stopped chewing.

“The second?”

Tyra moved her cup aside.

“That’s what we were taught.”

She spoke slowly, choosing expressions the translator would be able to carry across.

When the device produced *coordinated rescue of regions,* Tyra grimaced and pushed the plate away.

“Throw that out. I’ll tell you how they explained it to us.”

She arranged three empty cups in front of herself.

“Once, enormous areas depended on connected systems. This one managed energy. This one water. This one moved freight. Each did its own job well. Otherwise they could not have supported that many people.”

Tyra touched the first cup.

“Supplies began running short. The energy system started cutting off what it could function without. Pumps were among them.”

The second cup moved close to the first.

“Water changes distribution because it has been squeezed. Somewhere, cooling becomes insufficient.”

She turned the third cup sideways.

“Transport receives new restrictions and reroutes. The reroute goes through whichever nodes still work.

“After that, you can imagine.”

“Couldn’t they be stopped?” Asgar asked.

“One at a time, yes. That part isn’t even difficult: get there and switch it off.” Tyra spread the cups apart. “But you’re asking as though it were one enormous machine with one road leading to it. There were as many of them as there are courtyards in Ar.

“While people walked toward this one, that one over there began collapsing even though no one had been thinking about it that morning.

“They reached that one—something else had already happened here, and the person who had known this system since youth was four days away and could only listen while someone described what it was doing.”

She gathered the cups into one hand.

“None of them broke. None lied.

“Each protected its own function and let go of everything else.

“And each, doing that, cut off a neighbor’s arm, and the neighbor answered.

“Also honestly.

“People here still argue about who you would put on trial for that.”

Asgar looked at the man wiping his hands beside the water installation.

“Wait. He spent half the day separating your courtyard from the neighboring one. So you know how to cut connections. Why didn’t they do that then?”

“We learned afterward. Not because we got smarter.”

Tyra returned the cups to the tray one by one.

“Because now everyone knows how it ends. We can isolate water, power, and data locally. Slowly, expensively, inconveniently.

“But then somebody else’s repair system can’t leave us without water completely.”

“Do you isolate often?”

“Never without a reason. You saw today what it costs: someone spends half a day searching for a pressure loss while the courtyard lives on reserve.”

Someone nearby laughed.

The woman from the room emerged carrying an empty box, stopped beside them, and took a slice of vegetable from the tray.

She said something to Tyra.

Tyra answered; the woman shook her head and walked away.

“What?”

“She says the room is clear. They’ll bring a bed later.”

Asgar looked toward the door.

“And tomorrow?”

“First we try to verify your story. Then decide.”

“And if you find nothing?”

Tyra shrugged.

“Then we keep thinking.”

---

A bed appeared in the room.

Low, with a dense pale cover.

Some frames and sealed containers remained along the wall; they had cleared exactly enough room for him to walk to the door and the small washbasin in the corner.

Tyra showed him the water, light, and internal lock.

Beside the basin she left a small packet of clean dressing material.

She left the translator until morning.

“For talking, if you need it. I’ll take it tomorrow. We use it for work.”

Asgar nodded.

When Tyra left, he locked the door, then immediately checked that it opened easily from inside.

He washed.

Gray water ran from his hands.

He spent a long time cleaning his fingers, then carefully lifted the fabric over his knee and dealt with the dressing.

It did not come away immediately.

Asgar let the stuck section soak loose while sitting on the edge of the bed with his leg extended.

The wound looked unpleasant, but it was no longer bleeding.

He cleaned it, used the fresh material from Tyra’s packet, and put the remainder into the jacket pocket.

The simple task took a surprising amount out of him.

He set the phone beside the bed.

Kept his documents on him.

He had just lain down when someone knocked.

Arved stood outside with Tyra.

Asgar sat up and reached for the translator.

“Something happen?”

“No,” Arved said. “About tomorrow.”

He stayed in the doorway.

Looked over the room, lingering on the narrow path between the bed and the stacked components.

“It’s cramped. If you stay longer, we’ll find another place. Tomorrow, first we verify your account.”

Asgar felt the muscles in his neck tighten again.

“How?”

“Dates. Places. Names. You say you were already registered in another time. We’ll see what survived from that.”

“It’s been more than seven thousand years.”

“Maybe nothing survived. We’ll find out in the morning.”

Tyra leaned against the doorframe.

“That’s enough for today. Tomorrow we’ll find someone who knows how to work with records that old.”

Asgar looked at the phone beside the bed.

“I have documents. Old ones.”

“Bring them,” Arved said. “Now sleep.”

After they left, Asgar did not lie down immediately.

Boxes moved beyond the wall. Someone called the same person twice, then raised their voice; an irritated answer came back.

The day was still continuing in the courtyard.

He took off his shoes and placed them where he would not have to search beneath the bed in the morning.

Hung the jacket by the door.

Left the phone within reach.

Tyra was supposed to come after breakfast tomorrow.

Asgar took out his old documents, placed them beside the phone, and went over once more the dates he could read without translation.
