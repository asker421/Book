Chapter 2. While You Were Gone

The receiver was heavy. Asgar lifted it from the cradle, and the ringing cut off halfway through a stroke. Static rustled in his left ear; he pressed the receiver closer, still holding his mobile in front of his chest.

“Hello? I’m locked in here. The door won’t open.”

“Can you hear me?”

A woman’s voice. Even, with a slight delay before each reply, like a bad connection.

“I hear you. Open the door.”

“Can you stand? Are you injured?”

Asgar glanced at the shoulder he had slammed into the panel. The pain had already faded; he had managed to forget about it.

“I can stand. What happened? Where did everyone go?”

Someone spoke at the other end, but he could not make out the words. The woman answered away from the microphone, then came back to him.

“There was a malfunction. I have a link to the booth. I’m checking the lock now.”

“Where are you? At the stadium?”

“No.”

“Then who called you? There were people here, a full stadium. I stepped inside to make a call, something jolted me, and everyone disappeared. Do you understand me?”

“I can hear you. What is your name?”

“Asgar. Listen, I don’t need to file a repair ticket. I need to get out.”

Something clicked inside the door. Asgar shoved his mobile into his jeans pocket and pressed the handle with his free hand. It gave so easily he nearly punched the glass.

“Does the handle turn?” the woman asked.

“Yes.”

He pushed the door. It moved a short way and struck concrete. A cool draft slipped through the gap; Asgar turned his face into it. The corridor was there. He could reach an arm through, touch the wall, squeeze himself out.

“I need to understand what happened before the door closed,” the woman said. “How did you get inside?”

“Through the door. A man came out, then I went in. What is this—”

“Another person?”

It was the first time she interrupted him.

Asgar held the door open with his shoulder. It was awkward standing wedged between the panel and the telephone, while the empty corridor lay right in front of him, a place where he could still go looking for security.

“A man. Gray hair, beard. He came out just before I went in.”

“You saw him leave the booth?”

“Yes. I just said that.”

Static rustled on the line again. Asgar caught the beginning of someone else’s sentence, then the woman’s brief reply. He waited with the receiver pressed to his ear.

“Hello? Are you there?”

Her voice did not return.

He pressed the cradle switch twice with his thumb and let it spring back. Listened. A faint hiss remained in the receiver.

“For God’s sake.”

Asgar replaced it, turned sideways, and squeezed past the door. Once both feet were in the corridor, he let the panel go and stepped back. He wanted to get away from the red walls, but he was still waiting for the telephone to ring again and for the woman finally to say something that made sense.

The door closed.

The red edge beside the concrete wall shivered. Asgar jerked his head aside, thinking his vision had started swimming again. The lower part of the booth narrowed; the line of the floor passed straight through it. He staggered back, caught one heel on his other foot, and hit the opposite wall with his back.

There was concrete in front of him.

Asgar squeezed his eyes shut. Opened them. Slowly turned his head right, then left. Lamps. The far bend. The service entrance.

No red anywhere.

He stood with both palms against the wall behind him. Then he pushed away from it and walked to the spot where the door had been only seconds earlier. He held out one hand. His fingers touched rough concrete; a fleck of old paint caught beneath a nail.

Asgar ran his palm lower, down to where the handle had been. Knocked. A dull, heavy sound. He pressed both hands to the wall, then snatched them back at once, imagining the red body of the booth returning directly over his fingers.

“Hey! Anyone here?”

He headed toward the service entrance, walking fast at first, then breaking into a run. He shoved open the door to the concourse and stopped.

The kiosks were closed. Where he had recently been squeezing between people, a clean floor stretched away under broad streaks left by mopping. Daylight fell through the passage leading up to the stands.

Asgar went toward it. His own footsteps sounded on the stairs—clear, separate. He climbed to the opening and saw rows of empty seats. Sunlight lay across them. The field was clear. Far away, a man was dragging a hose across the grass.

Asgar gripped the railing.

He could see his section from here. Found the row, the seat by the aisle he had used on his way out. Yesterday—no, minutes ago—a man had been sitting there with a beer held against his chest. Now a long shadow from the edge of the canopy lay across the seats, and the sun was shining from the other side.

He took out his phone.

The network came back. Messages followed. The phone vibrated, stopped, vibrated again. Asgar opened the list and saw Katya’s name several times in a row, messages from people at work, then Katya again.

The date at the top of the screen was August twentieth.

He went back to the home screen. Opened the calendar.

Wednesday.

Asgar rubbed his thumb along the edge of the phone. The jolt could have reset something. The date could have synced wrong; maybe the network had done something strange. He closed the calendar and called Katya.

She answered immediately.

“Asgar?”

“Kat, listen, something’s—”

“Where are you?”

Her voice was loud and hoarse. Asgar pulled the phone away and checked whether speakerphone had somehow come on.

“At the stadium. I came out of that booth, there’s no one here. My date says—”

“Are you alone? Is anyone with you?”

“No. What happened?”

She drew in a noisy breath. He heard something fall near her, then footsteps.

“Kat?”

“Don’t go anywhere. Tell me which entrance you’re near.”

“Wait. What’s going on? We were just talking.”

“When?”

He did not answer. Beyond the field, the man with the hose stopped and bent toward the grass.

“Asgar, when did we talk?”

“Just now. You asked me to buy water.”

Katya went silent.

Asgar pressed the mobile so hard against his ear that the cartilage hurt. He wanted to hear something familiar beyond her silence: dishes, the television, his son. She was somewhere ordinary, surrounded by things that had continued to exist. He needed to hold on to her voice.

“What’s today’s date?” he asked.

“The twentieth.”

He looked at the empty stands.

“No. Kat, answer me properly.”

“August twentieth. Wednesday. We’ve been looking for you since Saturday.”

Asgar lowered himself into the nearest seat. His legs could still hold him, but standing had become difficult.

“I was here. I was here the whole time.”

“Okay. Just stay where you are. I’m coming now. And I’ll call Kerimov.”

“Who?”

“The investigator. He’s handling your disappearance.”

The word stayed in his ear after she finished the sentence. Asgar tried to imagine what disappearance she meant. Here was his hand on the railing. Here were his jeans, his phone. He had not intended to disappear anywhere. He had not even made it out of the stadium yet.

Footsteps sounded from the concourse. A man in a security uniform stopped at the bottom of the stairs and looked up.

“How did you get in here? The stadium’s closed.”

Asgar stood.

“I was at the match.”

The guard climbed several steps and stopped two short of him.

“What match? That was Saturday.”

He looked Asgar over from head to toe: jeans, phone in hand, empty section behind him. The hand holding his keys lowered.

“What’s your name?”

“Who’s there with you?” Katya asked.

“A security guard.”

“Give him the phone. Please, Asgar. Give it to him.”

He held the mobile out. The man took it carefully, without taking his eyes off Asgar.

“Yes? … Yes. Yes, he’s here.”

The guard straightened. Turned aside, asked Katya something in a lower voice, gave her the service-entrance location. Then he lowered the phone and held it for another second.

“So you’re the one they were looking for on Sunday. I walked through here with them myself. Down to that turn and back. Three times.”

He handed the phone over.

“So where were you?”

“Here,” Asgar said.

The guard was silent for a moment, then stepped aside to clear the way.

“Kat, I could have passed out. It shook me hard. Maybe I fell.”

“Then you need a doctor.”

“But four days… I couldn’t have been lying in a corridor for four days. People come through here.”

“I don’t know what happened. I’m coming to you.”

“Do you believe me?”

She exhaled into the phone.

“I can hear that you’re alive. You can tell me the rest when I get there.”

The guard suggested they go to the security room. Asgar followed him, glancing back at the stairs. He wanted to climb up one more time and check the section—maybe he had come through the wrong passage, maybe people were still sitting somewhere on the other side and the match was continuing. But the man with the hose was calmly working in the middle of the field.

Asgar had seen him with his own eyes.

A fan was running in the security room. Its grille rattled softly on every turn. Asgar sat at the side of the desk with his mobile in both hands, reading the messages in the order they had arrived.

At first Katya asked where he was. Then she begged him to answer. Farther down was a message sent during the night: she had already come to the stadium.

Asgar looked up at the guard. The man stood in the doorway talking to someone on his own phone. A logbook lay on the desk; across the top of the open page was written “20.08.2014.” Beside it were several entries about keys, arrival times, surnames. Asgar read them even though none of it helped.

He went back to the messages.

On Sunday, Katya wrote that the children were with relatives. On Monday, she asked him at least to let her know he was alive if he did not want to come home.

Asgar stopped at that line. Read it again. She had allowed herself to think he might have left them and switched off his phone. While he was trying to force open the booth door, she had had enough time to think that, reject it, think it again—how many times?

He opened the reply field even though he knew Katya was already on her way. Typed, *I didn’t leave,* and did not send it.

Voices sounded in the corridor. The guard stepped aside to let in a man carrying a folder. He entered, looked first at Asgar, then set the folder on the desk.

“Rauf Kerimov. I spoke to your wife on the phone. I’m handling your disappearance.”

Asgar stood.

“They told me. Listen, in that corridor—”

“We’ll go there in a moment. First tell me: do you need medical attention? Anything hurt? Any dizziness?”

“I hit my shoulder. It’s already fine. I want to know what today’s date is.”

Rauf did not reach for a calendar.

“What does your phone say?”

“The twentieth.”

“That’s correct.”

Asgar turned toward the fan. Warm air brushed the damp hair at his temple. He waited for the investigator to continue: a mistake, a misunderstanding, someone’s joke. Rauf pulled out a second chair and sat.

“Security says you came out of the service area. Tell me how you got in there.”

“On Saturday I came down from the stands. I was talking to my wife, looking for somewhere quieter. There was a booth there. Red. I went inside, the door closed, then something jolted me. I came out. That’s it.”

“Did you leave the stadium at any point after Saturday?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

Asgar looked at him.

“Because I was in the booth.”

“All four days?”

“There were no four days for me. I closed the door, something jolted me, then I opened it. That’s all.”

Rauf opened the folder.

“All right. That’s what we’ll write down. You remember entering and leaving one after the other. No four days in between.”

Asgar pressed his lips together.

“That’s exactly what I’m saying.”

“Now it’s clear.”

Rauf wrote something.

“Was the woman inside?”

“On the telephone. An old wall phone. She said there’d been some kind of malfunction.”

“Do you know her?”

“No.”

“Did she give her name?”

Asgar shook his head. He wanted to go back to the phone, force her to answer again. Ask her name, where she was, what she had done to the door. At the time, all he had been thinking about was getting out.

“Show me the corridor,” Rauf said.

The three of them went to the service door. The guard switched on the concourse lights and stayed by the entrance. Asgar stopped at the wall that, only a short while ago, he had been unable to touch because the red booth was in the way.

“Here.”

Rauf looked over the floor and wall, then raised his eyes to the ceiling.

“Show me how it was positioned.”

Asgar indicated the width with his hands. Took a step back, remembered the narrow gap between door and concrete, and moved closer to the wall.

“Like this. The door opened this way. You had to turn sideways to get through.”

“And where is the booth now?”

“It disappeared.”

Rauf looked from the concrete to him.

“You saw them remove it?”

“It disappeared right in front of me. Just now.”

Asgar heard his own voice and stopped talking. In the security room, the story had still been attached to familiar things: a conversation, a door, an impact. Here he had to point at an empty wall and demand that another adult understand him.

“I know how that sounds,” he said. “But it was here. I had my hands on it.”

Rauf did not touch the concrete. He noted the location, asked where the man Asgar had seen before entering had gone, and walked several steps toward the turn. Asgar stayed by the wall. He watched the investigator’s shoes, the way each sole came down on the floor, and desperately waited for one more thing to be wrong. If the line of concrete trembled, if even a flash of red appeared, he would no longer have to be the only one saying any of this.

Fast footsteps sounded from the concourse.

Katya appeared in the doorway and stopped.

Asgar went toward her. She started moving too, almost running, and wrapped her arms around him so hard his bruised shoulder flared.

He held her.

Katya gripped his back with both hands. Her face was buried against his neck; he could feel her breathing, rapid and hot. He wanted to tell her everything was all right, but over her shoulder he could still see the empty space by the wall.

“I’m here,” he said.

She pulled back only far enough to look at his face. Ran her fingers over his cheek, took his chin, turned his head toward the light.

“What happened to you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Was someone holding you somewhere? You can tell me.”

“Kat, I didn’t leave this place.”

She closed her eyes. The fingers on his cheek went still. Asgar covered them with his palm.

“I remember our conversation. You asked whether I was coming straight home. Then the connection dropped. For me that was a few minutes ago.”

“And I spent four nights not knowing where you were.”

He wanted to answer, to explain it again, but she pressed her forehead against his chest.

Asgar stroked her hair. Behind him, Rauf and the guard were speaking quietly. The corridor smelled of concrete and warm electrical wiring; everything was so ordinary that for a few seconds he managed to think only about Katya. She had come. He could hold her, take her hand, leave with her.

“Let’s go home,” he said.

Katya raised her head.

“A doctor needs to look at you first.”

“I feel fine.”

“You don’t remember four days. That is not fine.”

Asgar opened his mouth and closed it again. She was looking straight at him, and there was so much exhaustion in her face that for the first time he stopped searching for his next argument.

“Okay.”

Rauf came closer.

“We’ll arrange the doctor. But first I want to record your account now, exactly as you remember it. In an hour there’ll be ten people here, and every one of them will add their own version.”

“I want to talk to the kids,” Asgar said.

“You can.”

Katya took out her phone but did not dial immediately.

“I already told them you’d been found. Just don’t promise them anything yet about when you’ll be home.”

He nodded.

They went back to the security room. Katya sat beside him, close enough that their knees touched. While she made the call, Asgar looked at her hands. The skin beside one fingernail was torn. She caught him looking and tucked the finger beneath her palm.

Then she handed him the phone.

“Dad?”

Asgar put it to his ear.

“Yeah. It’s me. Hi.”

“Where were you?”

He looked at Katya. She sat very still, waiting with their son.

“I’m still figuring that out myself. Mom’s here with me now. It’s all right. They found me.”

Something rustled on the line; someone spoke in the background. Asgar waited. He wanted to ask about the birthday and stopped himself. For the child, that day was already over. Other days had followed it, days in which there had been no call from him.

“Are you coming?”

“I am. I just need to finish a few things here first.”

His son began telling him who was there with him. Asgar listened, asked questions, caught every word. Rauf turned pages at the desk. Katya held Asgar’s free hand.

When the call ended, he did not give the phone back right away.

“At first I thought you were late again,” Katya said. “I was angry. Even when you didn’t answer, I thought: fine, you’ll come home and we’ll talk.”

He stared at the screen, at the ended call.

“Kat…”

“Then I came here. They were already closing the place. I kept explaining that my husband was still in a telephone booth. They kept asking me which booth I meant.”

Asgar handed the mobile back.

Rauf placed a sheet of paper in front of him.

“This is the initial report. Look at the date. Your wife filed it after they failed to find you in the stadium.”

August seventeenth.

Asgar read the first line. Beneath it were his name, age, and a description of the last conversation. The words stood in neat lines with gaps where clarifications could be added. Katya’s signature was at the bottom.

He turned to her.

“You wrote this?”

“Yes.”

Asgar ran one finger along the edge of the page. A whole night lay between her request to buy water and this statement. She had come to the stadium, talked to people, waited, gone back to her phone.

Then morning had come.

And another.

He could not remember a single one of them.

Rauf drew a blank sheet toward himself.

“Let’s start with the match. Your wife calls. What happens next?”

Asgar pulled his chair closer to the desk. He did not let go of Katya’s hand.

“She called me during the match,” he said. “I went looking for somewhere quieter.”
