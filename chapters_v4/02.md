Chapter 2. While You Were Gone

The receiver was heavy. Asgar lifted it from the cradle, and the ringing cut off in the middle of a strike. Static rustled in his left ear; he pressed the receiver closer, still holding his mobile in front of his chest.

"Hello? I'm locked in here. The door won't open."

"Can you hear me?"

A woman's voice. Even; her replies came with a slight delay, like a bad connection.

"I can hear you. Open the door."

"Can you stand? Are you injured?"

Asgar glanced at the shoulder he had slammed into the panel. The pain was already fading; he had managed to forget about it.

"I can stand. What happened? Where did everyone go?"

Someone spoke at the other end, but he couldn't make out the words. The woman answered away from the microphone, then came back to him.

"There was a malfunction. I'm maintaining contact with the cabin. I'll check the lock now."

"Where are you? At the stadium?"

"No."

"Then who called you? There were people here, a full stadium. I stepped in to make a call, something jolted me, and everything disappeared. Do you understand me?"

"I can hear you. What's your name?"

"Asgar. Listen, I don't need a repair ticket. I need to get out."

Something clicked inside the door. Asgar shoved his mobile into the pocket of his jeans and pressed the handle with his free hand. It gave so easily that he nearly punched the glass.

"Does the handle turn?" the woman asked.

"Yes."

He pushed the door. It moved a little and hit the concrete. A cool draft slipped through the gap; Asgar turned his face toward it. The corridor was accessible. He could put an arm through, touch the wall, climb out.

"I need to understand what happened before the door closed," the woman said. "How did you get inside?"

"Through the door. A man came out, I went in. What is this..."

"Another person?"

For the first time, she interrupted him.

Asgar held the door open with his shoulder. It was awkward standing between the door and the telephone, while the empty corridor lay in front of him, a place where he could still look for security.

"A man. Gray-haired, with a beard. He came out before me."

"You saw him come out of the cabin?"

"Yes. I just said that."

Static rustled on the line again. Asgar heard the beginning of someone else's sentence, then the woman's brief reply. He waited, pressing the receiver to his ear.

"Hello? Are you there?"

Her voice did not return.

He pressed the phone's cradle switch twice with his thumb and let go. Listened. A faint rustle remained in the receiver.

"Damn it."

Asgar put it back, turned sideways, and squeezed past the door. Once both feet were in the corridor, he let the door go and stepped back. He wanted to get farther away from the red walls, but he was still waiting for the telephone to ring again and for the woman to finally say something that made sense.

The door closed.

The red edge beside the concrete wall trembled. Asgar moved his head back, thinking his vision was swimming again. The lower part of the booth narrowed; the line of the floor passed straight through it. He recoiled, caught one heel on his other foot, and hit the opposite wall with his back.

There was concrete in front of him.

Asgar squeezed his eyes shut. Opened them, slowly turned his head right and left. Lights, the far turn, the service entrance. There was no red anywhere.

He stood with both palms braced against the wall behind him. Then he pushed away from it and walked to the place where the door had been a moment ago. He held a hand out in front of him. His fingers touched rough concrete; a grain of old paint caught beneath his fingernail.

Asgar ran his palm lower, to the level of the handle. Knocked. Dull and heavy. He pressed both hands to the wall and immediately pulled them away, imagining the red body returning directly over his fingers.

"Hey! Is anyone here?"

He headed toward the service entrance, first quickly, then broke into a run. He pushed open the door to the concourse and stopped.

The kiosks were closed. Where he had recently been squeezing between people, there was a clean floor streaked broadly from mopping. Daylight fell through the passage leading to the stands.

Asgar headed toward it. His own footsteps sounded on the stairs—clear, separate. He climbed to the exit and saw empty rows. The seats stood in the sun, the field was empty. In the distance, a man was dragging a hose across the grass.

He grabbed the railing.

From here he could see his section. He found the row, the aisle seat he had worked his way out from. Yesterday—no, a few minutes ago—a man had been sitting there with a beer held to his chest. Now a long shadow from the edge of the canopy lay across the seats, and the sun was shining from the other side.

He took out his mobile.

The network came back. Messages followed it. The phone vibrated, stopped, then vibrated again. Asgar opened the list and saw Katya several times in a row, messages from people at work, then Katya again. At the top of the screen it said August twentieth.

He returned to the home screen. Opened the calendar.

Wednesday.

Asgar rubbed the edge of the phone with his thumb. The jolt could have thrown the phone off. The date had synced incorrectly, something had happened with the network. He closed the calendar and called Katya.

She answered immediately.

"Asgar?"

"Katya, listen, something's..."

"Where are you?"

Her voice was loud and hoarse. Asgar pulled the phone away from his ear to check whether speakerphone had somehow turned on.

"At the stadium. I came out of that booth, there's no one here. My date says..."

"Are you alone? Is anyone with you?"

"No. What happened?"

She drew in a noisy breath. He heard something fall near her, then footsteps.

"Katya?"

"Don't go anywhere. Tell me which entrance you're standing near."

"Wait. What's going on? We were just talking."

"When?"

He didn't answer. Across the field, the man with the hose stopped and bent toward the grass.

"Asgar, when were we talking?"

"Just now. You asked me to buy water."

Katya fell silent.

Asgar pressed the mobile so hard against his ear that the cartilage hurt. He wanted to hear something familiar behind her silence: dishes, the television, his son. She was somewhere ordinary, among things that had kept existing. He needed to hold on to her voice.

"What's today's date?" he asked.

"The twentieth."

He looked at the empty stands.

"No. Katya, tell me properly."

"August twentieth. Wednesday. We've been looking for you since Saturday."

Asgar sat down on the nearest seat. His legs could still hold him, but standing had become difficult.

"I was here. Here the whole time."

"Okay. Just stay where you are. I'm coming now. And I'll call Kerimov."

"Who?"

"The investigator. He's handling your disappearance."

The word stayed in his ear after she finished the sentence. Asgar tried to imagine what disappearance she was talking about. Here was his hand on the railing, here were his jeans, his phone. He had never meant to disappear. He hadn't even managed to leave the stadium yet.

Footsteps came from the concourse. A man in a security uniform stopped at the bottom of the stairs and looked up.

"How did you get in here? The stadium is closed."

Asgar stood up.

"I was at the match."

The guard climbed a few steps and stopped two short of him.

"What match? That was Saturday."

He looked Asgar up and down: jeans, the mobile in his hand, the empty section behind him. The hand holding his keys dropped.

"What's your name?"

"Who's there with you?"

"A guard."

"Give him the phone. Please, Asgar, give it to him."

He held the mobile out to the man. The guard took it carefully without taking his eyes off Asgar.

"Hello... Yes. Yes, he's here."

The guard straightened. Turned away, quietly clarified something with Katya, named the service entrance. Then he lowered the phone and held on to it for another second.

"So you're the one they were looking for on Sunday. I walked around here with them myself. From here to that turn and back, three times."

He handed the mobile back.

"And where were you?"

"Here," Asgar said.

The guard was silent, then stepped aside to clear the way.

"Katya, I could have lost consciousness. Something jolted me hard. Maybe I fell."

"Then you need a doctor."

"But four days... I couldn't have been lying in a corridor for four days. People walk through here."

"I don't know what happened. I'm coming to you."

"Do you believe me?"

She exhaled into the phone.

"I can hear that you're alive. Tell me the rest when I get there."

The guard suggested they go to the security room. Asgar followed him, looking back at the stairs. He wanted to climb up one more time and check the section: maybe he had taken the wrong passage, maybe people were sitting somewhere on the other side and the match was still going. But the man with the hose was calmly working in the middle of the field. Asgar could see him with his own eyes.

A fan was running in the security room. The grille rattled softly with every rotation. Asgar sat beside the desk, holding his mobile in both hands, and read the messages in the order they had arrived.

First Katya had asked where he was, then asked him to answer. After that came a message written at night: she had already come to the stadium.

Asgar looked up at the guard. The man was standing in the doorway, talking to someone on his own phone. A logbook lay on the desk; at the top of the open page was written "20.08.2014." Beside it were several entries about keys, arrival times, surnames. Asgar read them, even though none of it helped.

He went back to the messages.

On Sunday, Katya had written that the children were with relatives. On Monday, she asked him at least to let her know he was alive if he didn't want to come home.

Asgar stopped at that sentence. Read it again. She had allowed for the possibility that he might have left them and turned off his phone. While he had been trying to force open the door, she had had time to think it, reject it, think it again—how many times?

He opened the reply field even though he knew Katya was already on her way. Typed "I didn't leave" and did not send it.

Voices sounded in the corridor. The guard stepped away from the door to let in a man carrying a folder. The man entered, looked first at Asgar, then placed the folder on the desk.

"Rauf Kerimov. Your wife and I spoke on the phone. I'm handling your disappearance."

Asgar stood up.

"They told me. Listen, out there in the corridor..."

"We'll go there now. First tell me: do you need medical attention? Does anything hurt, are you dizzy?"

"I hit my shoulder. It's already gone. I want to understand what today's date is."

Rauf did not reach for a calendar.

"What does your phone show?"

"The twentieth."

"The date is correct."

Asgar turned toward the fan. The warm air touched the damp hair at his temple. He waited for the investigator to continue: mistake, misunderstanding, someone's joke. Rauf pulled out the second chair and sat down.

"Security says you came out of the service area. Tell me how you got in there."

"On Saturday I came down from the stands. I was talking to my wife, looking for somewhere quieter. There was a booth there. Red. I went in, the door closed, then something jolted me. I came out. That's all."

"Did you leave this place at any point after Saturday?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

Asgar looked at him.

"Because I was in the booth."

"All four days?"

"For me, there were no four days. I closed the door, something jolted me, then I opened it. That's all."

Rauf opened the folder.

"All right. That's how we'll write it down. You remember entering and leaving one after the other. With no four days in between."

Asgar pressed his lips together.

"That's exactly what I'm saying."

"Now it's clear."

Rauf wrote something down.

"Did you speak to anyone in there?"

"A woman. On an old wall phone. She said there had been a malfunction."

"Do you know her?"

"No."

"Did she give her name?"

Asgar shook his head. He wanted to go back to the phone, make her answer again. Ask her name, where she was, what she had done to the door. Back then, all he had been thinking about was getting out.

"Show me the corridor," Rauf said.

The three of them walked to the service door. The guard turned on the light in the concourse and stayed near the entrance. Asgar stopped by the wall he had been unable to touch only a short while ago because the red body had been in the way.

"Here."

Rauf looked over the floor and wall, then raised his eyes to the ceiling.

"Show me how it was positioned."

Asgar indicated the width with his hands. He took a step back, remembered the gap between the door and the concrete, and moved closer to the wall.

"Like this. The door opened this way. You had to go through sideways."

"And where's the booth now?"

"It disappeared."

Rauf shifted his gaze from the concrete to him.

"You saw it being removed?"

"It disappeared right in front of me. Just now."

Asgar heard his own voice and stopped. In the security room, the story had still been anchored to familiar things: a conversation, a door, an impact. Here, he had to point at an empty wall in front of another adult and demand that the man understand.

"I know how it sounds," he said. "But it was here. I was holding on to it with my hands."

Rauf did not touch the concrete. He wrote down the location, asked which way the man Asgar had seen before entering had gone, and walked a few steps toward the turn. Asgar stayed by the wall. He watched the soles of the investigator's shoes, watched them come down on the floor, and desperately waited for one more thing to be wrong. If the line of the concrete trembled, if red appeared for just a second, he would not have to be the only one saying all of this.

Quick footsteps came from the concourse.

Katya appeared in the doorway and stopped. Asgar walked toward her. She moved toward him too, almost running, and hugged him so tightly that his bruised shoulder flared.

He held her against him.

Katya had both arms around his back. Her face was buried in his neck; he could feel her breathing, fast and hot. He wanted to say everything was all right, but over her shoulder he could see the empty place by the wall.

"I'm here," he said.

She pulled back just far enough to look at his face. Ran her fingers over his cheek, took him by the chin, and turned his head toward the light.

"What happened to you?"

"I don't know."

"Was someone holding you? You can tell me."

"Katya, I didn't leave this place."

She closed her eyes. The fingers on his cheek went still. Asgar covered them with his palm.

"I remember our conversation. You asked if I was coming straight home. Then the connection dropped. For me, that was a few minutes ago."

"And I spent four nights not knowing where you were."

He wanted to answer, to explain it again, but she pressed her forehead to his chest.

Asgar stroked her hair. Behind him, Rauf and the guard were speaking quietly. The corridor smelled of concrete and warm wiring; everything was so ordinary that for a few seconds he managed to think only about Katya. She had come. He could hold her, take her hand, leave with her.

"Let's go home," he said.

Katya raised her head.

"A doctor needs to look at you first."

"I feel fine."

"You don't remember four days. That's not fine."

Asgar opened his mouth, then closed it. She was looking directly at him, and there was so much exhaustion in her face that for the first time he stopped searching for the next argument.

"Okay."

Rauf came closer.

"We'll arrange the doctor. But first I want to write down your account now, exactly as you remember it. In an hour there'll be ten people here, and each of them will add their own version."

"I want to talk to the children," Asgar said.

"You will."

Katya took out her phone but did not dial immediately.

"I already told them you'd been found. Just don't promise them anything yet about when you'll be home."

He nodded.

They went back to the security room. Katya sat beside him, so close that their knees touched. While she made the call, Asgar watched her hands. The skin beside one fingernail had been torn. She caught him looking and tucked the finger beneath her palm.

Then she handed him the phone.

"Dad?"

Asgar held it to his ear.

"Yes, it's me. Hi."

"Where were you?"

He looked at Katya. She sat motionless, waiting together with their son.

"I'm still figuring that out myself. Mom's here with me now. That's it, I've been found."

Something rustled in the receiver; someone spoke in the background. Asgar waited. He wanted to ask about the birthday and stopped himself: for the child, that day was already over. Other days had passed after it, days in which there had been no call from him.

"Are you coming?"

"I am. I just need to finish a few things here first."

His son started telling him who was there with him. Asgar listened, asked questions, caught every word. Rauf turned pages at the desk. Katya held Asgar's free hand.

When the call ended, he kept the phone for a while before giving it back.

"At first I thought you'd stayed out late again," Katya said. "I got angry. Even when you weren't answering, I thought: fine, you'll come home and we'll talk."

He stared at the screen, at the ended call.

"Katya..."

"Then I came here. They were already closing everything. I was explaining that my husband was still inside a telephone booth. They kept asking me to repeat which booth."

Asgar gave the mobile back to her.

Rauf placed a sheet of paper in front of him.

"This is the initial report. Look at the date. Your wife filed it after they couldn't find you at the stadium."

August seventeenth.

Asgar read the first line. Below were his name, age, and a description of the last conversation. The words were set out neatly, with spaces where clarifications could be added. At the bottom, Katya had signed it.

He turned to her.

"You wrote this?"

"Yes."

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the page. A night had passed between her asking him to buy water and this report. She had come to the stadium, spoken to people, waited, gone back to the phone. Then morning came. And the next one.

He could not remember a single one of them.

Rauf pulled a blank sheet toward himself.

"Let's start with the match. Your wife calls. What happens next?"

Asgar pulled his chair closer to the desk. He did not let go of Katya's hand.

"She called me during the match," he said. "I went to look for somewhere quieter."
