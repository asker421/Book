Chapter 19. It’ll Be Fixed by Evening

Bertram brought the latch just before lunch.

Asgar was sitting at a table in the outer courtyard, trying to work out where he had lost track of one frame. By his count there was one more than the woman checking the work had recorded. They counted twice, then she pointed to the piece beside his elbow. He had set it aside for another look and forgotten to put it back.

Once the numbers matched, Bertram placed a small gray mechanism in front of him.

“Take a look?”

The translator stood beside the box. Asgar turned it toward the craftsman.

“What’s wrong with it?”

Bertram pressed a raised piece. The tongue slid out of the housing but did not retract. He had to push it back with a finger.

“Works on an empty door. Under load it stops holding. I have to go to the pump now, and this needs to be back by evening.”

“I don’t know how it works.”

“That’s why I brought it out of the door. Open it and you’ll see. Mark this side first or you’ll put it back together backward.”

He showed Asgar where, left a short tool with a rotating head, and was already turning to go.

“What if I can’t fix it?”

“Then I’ll look at it myself this evening.”

Bertram left without waiting for a promise.

Asgar pressed the tongue again. The housing was warm from someone else’s hands. He turned it over, found the fasteners, and tried the tool. The head fit after the second adjustment.

There were more pieces inside than he had expected, but the motion itself was easy enough to follow: pressing one part released another, which returned the tongue. A small torn piece of seal had jammed beside the guide.

He removed it, put the mechanism back together, and tested it several times.

The latch clicked easily.

Bertram returned when people were already clearing dishes from the tables. He watched Asgar press the raised piece, took the mechanism, and braced the housing against the edge of the table.

The tongue jammed.

“Under load,” Bertram said.

Asgar reached for the tool.

Bertram did not take the work away from him. He only turned the latch and showed where the housing twisted slightly.

They missed lunch.

A woman brought them two covered bowls, set them on the free edge of the table, and covered the open box so crumbs would not fall inside.

Asgar ate while looking at the dismantled mechanism.

By then, he wanted to finish it himself.

Toward evening, Bertram took him to a tall cabinet in the next room. They installed the latch, closed the heavy door, and pulled.

It held.

Bertram asked someone working nearby to test it again.

The man closed the door, opened it, and went back to what he had been doing.

On the way back, Asgar held out the tool.

“Leave it on the worktable for now,” Bertram said. “There are two more like that. Just don’t take both apart at once.”

By then Asgar had been moved to another room.

It was just as cramped, but at least there were no longer somebody else’s frames stacked along the walls.

That was where he realized he had gone the whole day without looking toward the place where the booth had disappeared.

He remembered it only after he was already lying down and turning out the light.

---

A house was freed for him at the end of the third week.

It was small, at the edge of the outer courtyard, with a door that could be locked from inside by an ordinary turn of a handle.

Someone who had gone to work in another community had lived there before. Tyra explained that the house was only being assigned to Asgar temporarily. Some of the owner’s things would remain in a locked cabinet; he should not open it.

Asgar agreed without even asking how long the owner would be gone.

Tyra helped carry over the bedding and dishes that had been issued to him.

Asgar carried the jacket, his documents, and the phone himself.

Everything else fit into one small bundle: two changes of local clothing, given to him after he had finally asked for something he could work in.

His old trousers had been washed, and the worn patch over the knee had been covered with a soft repair.

The abrasion itself had already healed. Sometimes he still favored the leg from habit until he noticed it did not hurt.

At the door, Tyra stopped.

“Bertram will come by this evening. The window panel facing the passage needs checking. Sometimes it doesn’t close all the way.”

“I’ll look at it myself.”

“Look. Bertram’s still coming. I already arranged it.”

Asgar nodded and set his bundle on the table.

The house was unnaturally quiet.

No one was sorting parts through the wall anymore.

He could lie down in the middle of the day without hearing someone trying to move a box quietly because a guest was sleeping on the other side.

He went to the sink, turned on the water, and watched it disappear down the drain for a long time.

Then he went back for his things.

The phone went beside the bed.

He hung the jacket from 2744 near the door.

He folded one of his old T-shirts separately from the local clothes and put it on an empty shelf.

Bertram came that evening with Tobin.

The boy noticed the phone immediately but did not touch it.

He bent over it, looked at Asgar, and asked something.

Tyra had left the translator with them while Bertram worked on the window panel. Asgar moved it closer.

“Is it broken?”

“Dead battery.”

“Charge it.”

Asgar smiled.

“Haven’t managed yet.”

Tobin looked at Bertram, then back at the phone.

The craftsman was standing by the window and did not even turn around.

“I haven’t taken it apart. And I’m not connecting power until I know what it needs.”

The boy sat on the edge of a chair.

In his hands was a wooden playing piece with an unevenly carved head. He ran a thumbnail along its back, blew the shavings onto the floor, and earned a short remark from Bertram.

Asgar found a cloth.

Tobin took it, gathered the shavings, and asked whether he knew how to play.

“Play what?”

The translator handled the answer badly.

The boy tried to explain again, then brought in a flat board and several pieces from the courtyard.

One was missing.

They used the lid of an empty container instead.

While Bertram worked on the window, Asgar lost twice.

On the third game he noticed Tobin’s piece had moved through a place where Asgar’s had just been forbidden to go.

“Wait. Why can you do that?”

The boy launched into an explanation.

The translator fell behind.

Asgar raised a hand.

“Slowly. I wasn’t allowed to go through here.”

Bertram turned from the window, listened, and said something to Tobin.

The boy reluctantly put the piece back.

“He forgot one rule,” the device translated.

“Very convenient timing.”

Tobin smiled without looking up and made a different move.

---

A month later, Asgar was late for work.

He had only meant to pick up water, but he met Saima by the shade structure, said hello, and lingered while trying to tell her in the local language that the window in his house now closed properly.

What came out had something to do with a wall.

Saima corrected him patiently.

Then he went back for the translator he had begun receiving for the day, separately from Tyra’s device, and discovered he had left it somewhere other than where he thought.

When he finally reached the workshop, Bertram was working alone.

A drive assembly lay dismantled on the table.

The second section—the one Asgar was supposed to hold while it was removed—had been fixed in place with straps.

“I thought we were starting later.”

Bertram kept tightening a fastener.

“We agreed yesterday. After the first signal.”

Asgar knew the signal.

He had heard it in the courtyard and decided he still had time.

“I was only held up for a little while.”

“I already took it off.”

He said it evenly and did not slide a part or tool toward Asgar.

Asgar stood beside him for several minutes, then began clearing what looked like unnecessary fasteners from the table.

Bertram stopped him.

They were laid out in the order they had been removed.

Asgar put them back.

He wanted to say he was not some salaried worker here and did not owe anyone minute-by-minute obedience to a schedule.

The thought was satisfying exactly until he looked at the straps Bertram had used in place of Asgar’s hands.

“You’re right,” he said. “I heard the signal. I decided later would be fine.”

Bertram checked the fastener.

“Next time tell me beforehand. I’ll start with something else.”

They assembled the drive together, but did not talk much that day.

In the evening Asgar tried to tell Tyra about it.

At first his version came out as if he simply had not understood how much of a hurry Bertram was in.

She listened while setting dishes on the table, then asked:

“Did you think the two of you had agreed on a time?”

He went quiet.

Tyra put a cup in front of him.

“He doesn’t expect you to know how to do everything. He needs to know when he can leave the second side in your hands.”

Asgar looked at those hands.

A small callused patch had formed on his thumb from the tool.

“I understand.”

“Then eat. Everything went cold while you were explaining.”

He picked up the spoon.

He did not want her to smile.

Tyra did not smile.

---

During the hot days, the shade over the common courtyard stopped being enough.

By midday people moved work indoors, but the air stood still there too.

Asgar woke with a damp pillow, opened the door, and waited for the house to cool a little.

One morning he saw Tyra before breakfast.

She sat beside the water node checking readings, drinking from a cup she had set directly on the ground.

A few days later, it became easier.

Air moved beneath the shade. Leaves along the wall stopped hanging limp.

In the evening people could sit outside without fanning heat away from their faces.

Tyra explained that several neighboring sections had coordinated the operation of their climate systems.

Shared regulation reduced water consumption.

Bertram listened with his hands on his knees.

“Isolation points checked?”

“Yes. Separately for every section.”

“Good.”

That ended the conversation.

One such evening, Tobin brought the board to the table after dinner.

By then the pieces were almost the same size, though the one Asgar usually played with had one flat side: the boy had cut too deeply into the wood.

Asgar tried the local word for defeat.

Tobin corrected his pronunciation and immediately repeated it back to him with the sound exaggerated.

Tyra laughed.

“He says it’s too early for you to learn that word. Win first.”

“I’m about to.”

He could already play without the translator.

He did not understand rapid jokes, mixed up the names of the pieces, but argued confidently enough that Tobin had begun demanding the translator whenever the rules stopped favoring him.

That evening, Asgar won.

The boy inspected the board, moved one piece, put it back, and announced that the game was not actually over yet.

Bertram, sitting nearby, told him to accept the result.

Tobin protested in his own language now, fast and at length.

Asgar laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

Someone at the table asked for a bowl to be passed.

Tyra tore off a piece of bread.

Bertram moved his chair to let someone carrying dishes through.

Tobin swept up the pieces so sharply that one fell to the ground and he ducked under the table after it.

Asgar bent down, picked it up first, and held out his open palm.

The boy took it.

“Tomorrow,” he said in the Russian he had learned.

---

They noticed the malfunction through the water.

There was still enough in the work courtyard, but farther down the slope the supply had become intermittent.

Tyra returned from there, walked past the breakfast table without stopping, and called Bertram.

Asgar heard his own name among unfamiliar words and stood.

“We need to move the reserve containers,” she said through the translator. “To the lower courtyard. While we work out what’s happening.”

He went with them.

People were already lining empty vessels along the wall.

Asgar helped pass full ones and set them where he was told.

At one point an irrigation device continued running while a woman nearby could not get water into her own container.

Tyra stopped the irrigation manually.

A little later it started again.

She looked at Bertram.

He crouched beside the local controls, checked them, then opened a protective cover.

Asgar set down the container he was carrying.

“Why did it come back on?”

“It received a shared command,” Tyra said. “The neighboring section needs cooling. Our use is already accounted together with theirs.”

“But you shut it off.”

“Local irrigation. Now we’ll break the coordination link.”

She spoke in short sentences while watching the readings.

Bertram called another worker.

While the man was on his way, Asgar went back to moving water.

By noon they discovered that some sections could be isolated and others could not.

Some accepted local restrictions.

Others returned to shared operation.

People argued beneath the shade.

Asgar understood individual words and waited until Tyra had time to translate.

Arved arrived with two people from the lower courtyard.

He listened for a long time, then ordered them to prepare a complete physical disconnection.

Tyra did not object to the decision itself.

She argued about sequence: first they needed to move supply onto local reserves or several rooms would lose water immediately.

Arved wanted it done quickly.

At one point Bertram interrupted both of them and pointed into the open housing.

Asgar could not make out the words, but everyone stopped and looked.

A light was blinking inside.

Tyra came over to him.

“Move the remaining containers. Then stay by the passage. Don’t let anyone come onto this side while Bertram works.”

That, at least, he understood.

They isolated the sections one by one.

After every break, Tyra waited for confirmation from the far end, checked the local supply, and only then moved on.

The courtyard grew hotter.

Air movement beneath the shade stopped.

At the third node, the confirmation did not agree.

Tyra waited for the response from below and checked the local supply.

Her instrument still showed that the node remained in shared mode.

Bertram lifted the severed end and turned it toward her.

The metal pieces were physically separated.

Air passed between them.

He checked with his own instrument.

His value did not match hers.

They swapped instruments and checked again.

The readings diverged once more, but in a different way.

Tyra did not average them.

She recorded both separately and moved on to the next node.

At one of the nodes they had to remove a long protective panel.

Asgar helped Bertram carry it toward the wall.

As they lowered the bottom edge, a small stone slipped from beneath Bertram’s foot.

The panel tilted.

Asgar held his side, but the upper corner struck him above the left eyebrow.

He squeezed his eyes shut and stepped back.

First came the impact.

Then warmth over his eyelid.

He touched his forehead.

His fingers came away bloody.

“Stop,” Bertram said.

The panel was already on the ground.

The craftsman took clean material from the work kit and pressed it against the cut.

Asgar tried to take it himself, but the blood made it difficult to open one eye.

Tyra led him into the shade.

“Can you see normally?”

“Yes. It’s just bleeding.”

“Hold this here. We’ll clean it now.”

“What about this?”

She looked toward the node.

Another worker had already taken Asgar’s place beside Bertram.

“They’ll continue.”

The cut was shallow.

They cleaned it, closed the edges, explained when to remove the covering and what to do if the pain worsened.

Asgar sat with the jacket across his knees.

By the time he returned to the courtyard, water was already flowing from the local supply.

The air remained heavy and hot.

---

Arved brought up Asgar that evening when people gathered to discuss the next day’s work.

The translator lay in the middle of the table.

Tyra had put it there deliberately: Asgar still could not follow rapid conversation when several people were speaking.

Arved listed the sections that still needed checking, then reminded everyone of the damage around the red booth in the old records.

Asgar looked up.

“There was no booth today.”

“I saw,” Arved said. “But today we spent half a day carrying water by hand because one connected system failed. If one day your booth appears beside the pumps, I’ll be the one explaining why I decided everything would probably be fine.”

“This would have happened without me.”

“Maybe. Tyra still doesn’t know what the instruments were showing at the third node. I’m not putting that on you. But I can’t say what follows you has nothing to do with us either.”

Several people nodded.

Asgar felt the skin above his eyebrow tighten.

Tyra moved closer to the translator.

“I have two readings that don’t agree. That means I haven’t explained them. It does not mean he explains them.

“So talk about the next thing.

“Don’t put today’s fault beside his booth as if they’re the same event.”

Arved looked at her.

“They’re not. But if it appears beside shared supply, all of us deal with what follows.”

One of the women asked where Asgar was working now.

Bertram explained.

Then the man who had been dismantling the long housing in the outer courtyard on Asgar’s first day spoke.

Asgar had seen him almost every day since. He worked on supply and was responsible for the nodes they had physically isolated today.

“Equipment should be moved away from his house,” he said.

Tyra began to object, but he went on without raising his voice.

“I’ve been carrying water in buckets since dawn. Not because he caused it—I didn’t say that.

“But when something breaks, I go fix it.

“Arved gets to explain why we have a man living here when walls break around him.”

He looked across the table at Asgar.

“You get told interesting stories about yourself. I get told which panel I may have to remove tomorrow.”

Asgar reached toward the translator to answer and discovered there was nothing to say.

All of it was true.

“I’ll move whatever you tell me to.”

“You will.

“And I’ll be the one putting it back.”

The man did not address him again.

Asgar looked toward Bertram.

The craftsman sat with his fingers interlocked and made no promise that everything would remain as it had been.

No decision about Asgar was made that evening.

They agreed to return to the question separately.

Asgar walked home alone.

He found the board and pieces at his door.

Tobin was waiting on the step, but when he saw the covering above Asgar’s eyebrow, he stopped arranging them.

“Hurts?”

Asgar understood without the device.

“A little.”

The boy shifted the board to make room.

They played one game.

Asgar lost quickly, and this time Tobin did not celebrate out loud.

“Play properly,” Asgar said.

He had to explain through the translator.

Tobin protested: he was playing properly.

They began the second game already arguing.

---

The skin above Asgar’s eyebrow healed before the old coolness returned to the courtyards.

A thin pale line remained where the cut had been.

Asgar noticed it one morning while washing.

He leaned toward the mirror and turned his head.

From one step back, the line nearly disappeared.

He lingered another second.

He remembered the paused stadium frame:

gray hair, a gaunt face breaking into blotches when enlarged.

Asgar ran a finger beside the scar, turned the water back on, and washed his face.

Then he shut it off and stepped away from the mirror.

Later Bertram checked whether the scar made him squint in bright light.

Asgar said no.

The craftsman nodded and asked him to hold a removed drive.

By then there were several jobs Asgar could do without having each step explained.

Bertram still took the complicated work for himself.

Sometimes Asgar hurried.

Sometimes he forgot to return a tool.

The craftsman complained and pointed toward the empty place in the kit.

Tyra did not come by every day anymore.

Sometimes they saw each other only at dinner.

She would be talking with other people while Asgar sat beside them, understanding more and more but still losing the thread once people began interrupting one another.

He learned how to ask for repetition without the translator.

He learned to recognize when a joke was about him before he understood every word.

One evening Tyra asked him to clear the table by the window tomorrow so they could inspect parts there.

“Tobin’s board is on it.”

“Move it.”

“He’ll say I changed the pieces.”

“Then finish the game tonight.”

Asgar looked at the abandoned position.

He no longer remembered whose move it was.

---

On the hundred and third morning, Asgar woke before the signal.

The sky beyond the window was overcast.

He dressed, put away the bedding, and moved the game board onto a low stand, careful not to disturb the pieces.

He had to steady one with a finger.

Tobin had refused to finish the night before.

Said he wanted to think.

Tyra came after breakfast.

She checked the cleared table, placed fasteners for a new section of shade on it, and asked him to help carry them down tomorrow.

“After the first signal?”

“Yes. Bertram will be waiting at the lower passage.”

Asgar smiled.

“I won’t be late.”

“I’ll tell him you said that.”

She went across the courtyard and stopped to speak with a woman by the water.

Asgar returned home for his work tools.

Someone knocked when he was already about to leave.

Bertram stood on the threshold.

No bag.

No removed part in his hands.

He looked at the tool Asgar was holding.

“Leave it for now.”

Asgar did not put it down immediately.

“What?”

“There’s a red booth by the river. People moved away. I called Tyra.”

He spoke slowly so Asgar could understand without translation.

Asgar looked toward the table by the window.

The fasteners they were supposed to carry down tomorrow lay there.

He set the tool beside them, returned to the door, then stopped again and took the jacket from its hook.

The phone was beside the bed.

Asgar took that too.

And his documents.

They walked to the river along a familiar path.

At the turn where rainwater collected, Bertram went around the edge.

Asgar automatically stepped onto the flat stone.

He had put it there himself several weeks ago after getting tired of coming home with one wet shoe.

Tyra was waiting on the bank.

Two other people stood nearby.

Farther up the path, Arved was approaching.

No one went down into the water.

The booth stood in the middle of the shallow river.

The current moved around the red wall and gathered in ripples before it.

The telephone was visible through the glass.

Asgar stared at the lower part of the door and could not reconcile what he saw:

outside, the water stood higher.

Inside, its surface was visibly lower.

Between the two levels ran a clean, impossible boundary.

He took one step toward the slope.

Tyra touched his arm.

“Wait. Look from here first.”

Asgar stopped beside her.

Behind him, farther up the path, stood a house with an unfinished game inside.

Here, in the water, stood the door he had spent so many days waiting for and then stopped expecting every morning.

He watched it without going down to the bank.
