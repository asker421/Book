Chapter 19. It'll Be Fixed by Evening

Bertram brought the latch just before lunch.

Asgar was sitting at a table in the outer courtyard trying to understand where he had missed one frame. By his count there was one more than in the count of the woman accepting the work. They counted twice, then she pointed to the part by his elbow. He had set it aside for rechecking and forgotten to return it.

When the numbers matched, Bertram put a small gray mechanism in front of him.

"Will you look at it?"

The translator stood beside the box. Asgar turned it toward the craftsman.

"What's wrong with it?"

Bertram pressed a protrusion. The tongue came out of the housing but did not return. He had to push it with a finger.

"It works on an empty door. Under load it stops holding. I'm going to the pump now, and this needs to be back by evening."

"I don't know how it works."

"That's why I brought it removed. Open it—you'll see. Just mark it here first, otherwise you'll assemble it backward."

He showed the spot, left a short tool with a rotating head, and was already preparing to leave.

"What if I can't do it?"

"Then I'll look at it myself this evening."

Bertram left without waiting for a promise.

Asgar pressed the tongue again. The housing was warm from someone else's hands. He turned it over, found the fasteners, and tried the tool. The head fit after the second turn. There were more parts inside than he expected, but the movement itself was understandable: pressing one part released another, and that one returned the tongue. A small piece of seal was stuck by the guide.

He removed it, reassembled the mechanism, and tested it several times. The latch clicked easily.

Bertram returned when dishes were already being cleared from the tables. He watched Asgar press the protrusion, took the mechanism, and pressed the housing against the edge of the table.

The tongue stuck.

"Under load," Bertram said.

Asgar reached for the tool. The craftsman did not take the job away, only turned the latch and showed where the housing twisted slightly.

They missed lunch. The woman brought them two covered bowls, set them on a free edge, and covered the box so crumbs would not get inside. Asgar ate while looking at the disassembled mechanism. By then he already wanted to finish it himself.

Toward evening, Bertram took him to a long cabinet in the neighboring room. They installed the latch, closed the heavy door, and pulled. It held. Bertram asked the person working nearby to check it once more.

He closed it, opened it, and went back to his work.

On the way back, Asgar held out the tool.

"Leave it on the worktable for now," Bertram said. "There are two more like that. Just don't take both apart at once."

By then they had changed Asgar's room: just as cramped, but no longer lined with someone else's frames.

There he discovered he had not looked all day at the place where the booth had disappeared.

He remembered it only after lying down, when he turned off the light.

---

They freed up a house for him at the end of the third week.

Small, at the edge of the outer courtyard, with a door that could be locked from inside by an ordinary turn of a handle. A man who had gone to work in another community had lived there before. Taira explained that the house was being provided temporarily. Some belongings would remain in a locked cupboard; he was not to open it.

Asgar agreed without even asking how long the owner would be gone.

Taira helped carry over the issued bedding and dishes. Asgar himself carried the jacket, documents, and phone. Everything else fit into a small bundle: he had been given two changes of local clothes after he finally asked for something suitable for work. His old trousers had been washed, and the worn spot at the knee covered with a soft patch. The abrasion had already healed; sometimes he still protected the leg out of habit until he noticed it no longer hurt.

At the door Taira stopped.

"Bertram will come by this evening. The shutter needs to be checked from the passage side. Sometimes it doesn't close all the way."

"I'll look at it myself."

"Look. But Bertram is coming anyway, I arranged it with him."

Asgar nodded and put the bundle on the table.

The house was unusually quiet. No one was sorting parts behind the wall anymore. He could lie down in the daytime without hearing someone try to move a box quietly because a guest was sleeping here.

He went to the washbasin, turned on the water, and watched it disappear into the drain for a long time.

Then he went back for his things.

The phone went beside the bed. He hung the jacket from 2744 by the door. He folded the T-shirt from his old things separately from the local clothes and put it on an empty shelf.

In the evening Bertram came with Tobin.

The boy immediately noticed the phone but did not pick it up. He bent over it, looked at Asgar, and asked something. Taira had left them the translator while they worked on the shutter; Asgar pulled it closer.

"Is it broken?"

"Discharged."

"Charge it."

Asgar smiled.

"Hasn't worked yet."

Tobin looked at Bertram, then at the phone again. The craftsman, standing by the window, did not even turn around.

"I haven't taken it apart. And I won't connect it without checking the power."

The boy sat on the edge of the chair. He held a wooden figure with an unevenly carved head. He scraped a fingernail along its back, blew the shaving onto the floor, and received a brief remark from Bertram.

Asgar found a cloth. Tobin took it, collected the shaving, and asked whether Asgar knew how to play.

"Play what?"

The translator handled the answer badly. The boy tried to explain again, then brought a flat board and several figures from the courtyard. One was missing; they used a lid from an empty container instead.

While Bertram worked on the shutter, Asgar lost twice.

On the third game, he noticed that Tobin's piece had moved where his own had just been forbidden to go.

"Wait. Why are you allowed?"

The boy began to explain. The translator could not keep up; Asgar raised a hand.

"Slowly. Right here, I wasn't allowed."

Bertram turned from the window, listened, and said something to Tobin. The boy reluctantly moved the piece back.

"He forgot one rule," the device translated.

"Very convenient timing."

Tobin smiled without raising his eyes and made a different move.

---

A month later, Asgar was late for work.

He had meant only to fetch water, but met Saima beneath the canopy, greeted her, and lingered trying to explain in the local language that the window at his house now closed properly. What came out was something about a wall. Saima patiently corrected him. Then he went back for the translator, which by then they issued to him for the day separately from Taira's device, and discovered he had left it somewhere other than where he thought.

When he reached the workshop, Bertram was working alone.

A disassembled drive lay on the table. The second part, which Asgar was supposed to hold during removal, the craftsman had secured with straps.

"I thought we were starting later."

Bertram kept tightening the fastening.

"Yesterday we agreed after the first signal."

Asgar knew that signal. He had heard it in the courtyard and decided he would make it.

"I got held up for a little while."

"I already took it off."

He said it evenly and did not move either the part or a tool toward Asgar.

For several minutes Asgar stood nearby, then started clearing unnecessary fasteners from the table. Bertram stopped him: they had been laid out in removal order.

Asgar put everything back.

He wanted to say he was not a salaried employee here and did not have to adjust to someone else's schedule by the minute. The thought was pleasant only until he looked at the straps with which the craftsman had replaced his hands.

"You're right," he said. "I heard the signal. Decided later was fine."

Bertram checked the fastening.

"Next time say so in advance. Then I'll start with something else."

They assembled the drive together, but spoke little that day.

That evening Asgar tried to tell Taira about it. At first his version made it sound as though he simply had not understood how much of a hurry Bertram was in. She listened while arranging dishes on the table, then asked:

"Did you yourself think you had an agreement?"

He fell silent.

Taira set a cup in front of him.

"He doesn't require you to know everything. He needs to know when he can leave the second side to you."

Asgar looked at his hands. A small callused patch had appeared on his thumb from the tool.

"I get it."

"Then eat. While you were explaining, everything got cold."

He picked up the spoon. He did not want her to smile, and Taira did not.

---

On hot days, the canopy over the common courtyard stopped being enough.

By noon people moved their work indoors, but the air stood still there too. Asgar woke with a damp pillow, opened the door, and waited for the house to cool a little. One morning he saw Taira even before breakfast: she was sitting by the water node checking readings and drinking from a cup she had set directly on the ground.

A few days later it became easier.

Air moved beneath the canopy, leaves by the wall stopped hanging like rags. In the evening they could sit outside without fanning the heat away from their faces. Taira explained that several neighboring sections had coordinated their climate installations. Shared regulation allowed them to use less water.

Bertram listened with his hands on his knees.

"Did you check the disconnects?"

"Yes. Separately in every section."

"Good."

That ended the conversation.

One evening like that, Tobin brought the board to the table after dinner. The pieces were now almost the same size, though the one Asgar usually played with still had one flat side: the boy had cut the wood too deep.

Asgar tried the local word for defeat. Tobin corrected his pronunciation and immediately repeated it back to him, exaggerating the sound.

Taira laughed.

"He says it's too early for you to learn that word. Win first."

"I'll win right now."

He could already play without the translator. He did not understand fast jokes, confused the names of the pieces, but argued confidently enough that Tobin began demanding the translator whenever it was to his advantage.

That evening Asgar won.

The boy checked the arrangement of the pieces, moved one, moved it back, and declared that they were not finished yet. Bertram, sitting nearby, told him to accept the result. Tobin protested in his own language now, quickly and at length.

Asgar laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

Someone at the table asked for a bowl to be passed. Taira broke off a piece of bread, Bertram moved his chair to let someone carrying dishes through. Tobin swept the pieces together so sharply that one fell and he crawled under the table after it.

Asgar bent down, picked it up first, and held out his open palm.

The boy took the piece.

"Tomorrow," he said in learned Russian.

---

They noticed the malfunction by the water.

There was still enough in the work courtyard, but supply to the section lower down the slope became intermittent. Taira came back from there, walked past the breakfast table, and called Bertram. Asgar heard his own name among the unfamiliar words and stood up.

"We need to move the reserve containers," she said through the translator. "To the lower courtyard. While we figure it out."

He went with them.

People were already setting empty vessels along the wall. Asgar helped pass the filled ones and put them where he was shown. In one place, water kept running from an irrigation unit although a woman nearby was unsuccessfully trying to fill her container.

Taira stopped the irrigation manually.

A little later, the unit started again.

She looked at Bertram. He crouched beside the local controls, checked them, then opened the protective cover.

Asgar set the container on the ground.

"Why did it turn back on?"

"It received a shared task," Taira answered. "The neighboring section requires cooling. Our consumption is already counted together with theirs."

"But you turned it off."

"Local irrigation. Now we'll break the coordination."

She spoke briefly while watching the readings. Bertram called another worker over. While he was coming, Asgar returned to moving water.

By noon it became clear they could not separate everywhere. Some sections accepted local restrictions; others returned to shared mode. People under the canopy argued; Asgar understood individual words and waited for Taira to be able to translate.

Arved arrived with two residents of the lower courtyard.

He listened for a long time, then ordered them to prepare a full physical separation.

Taira did not object to the decision itself. She argued about the order: first they had to switch supply to local reserves, otherwise several rooms would lose water immediately. Arved demanded that they not delay. At some point Bertram interrupted both of them, pointing to the open cover.

Asgar did not understand the words, but everyone fell silent and looked.

A light was blinking inside.

Taira came over to Asgar.

"Move the remaining containers. Then stay at the passage. Don't let people come to this side while Bertram works."

At least here he understood what to do.

They separated the sections one by one. After each break, Taira waited for confirmation from people at the other end, checked local supply, and only then moved on. The courtyard grew hotter. Air stopped moving beneath the canopy.

At the third node, the confirmation did not match.

Taira waited for the response from below and checked local supply. The device showed the node was still holding the shared mode. Bertram lifted the cut-off end and turned it toward her: the metal ends were separated, air passing between their edges.

He checked with his own instrument. The value did not match hers.

They exchanged instruments and checked again. The values diverged again, but differently.

Taira did not average them together. She recorded both separately and moved to the next node.

At one node, a long protective panel had to be removed. Asgar helped Bertram carry it to the wall. As they lowered the bottom edge, a small stone slipped from beneath the craftsman's foot. The panel swung; Asgar held his side, but the upper corner caught him above the left eyebrow.

He squeezed his eyes shut and stepped back.

At first he felt the impact, then warmth on his eyelid. He ran a hand over his forehead; his fingers came away bloody.

"Stop," Bertram said.

The panel was already on the ground. The craftsman took clean material from the work kit and pressed it to the cut. Asgar wanted to take it himself, but the blood made it hard to open his eye.

Taira led him into the shade.

"Can you see normally?"

"Yes. It's just bleeding."

"Hold it here. We'll go clean it now."

"What about this?"

She looked toward the node. Another worker had already taken his place beside Bertram.

"They'll continue here."

The cut turned out to be shallow. They cleaned it, closed the edges, explained when to remove the covering and what to do if it began hurting more. Asgar sat with the jacket on his knees.

When he returned to the courtyard, water was already flowing from local supply.

The air remained heavy and hot.

---

Arved brought up Asgar that evening when people gathered to discuss the next day's work.

The translator lay in the middle of the table. Taira had put it there deliberately: Asgar still understood a fast conversation between several people too poorly.

Arved listed the sections that still needed checking, then reminded them of the damage near the red cabin in old records.

Asgar raised his head.

"There was no booth today."

"I saw that," Arved answered. "But today we spent half the day carrying water by hand because of one linked failure. If one day your cabin appears beside the pumps, I'll be the one explaining to people why I decided everything would be fine."

"This would have happened without me."

"Maybe. Taira still doesn't know what the instruments showed at the third node. I'm not putting that on you. But I can't say there's nothing of yours here either."

Several people nodded to Arved. Asgar felt the skin tighten above his eyebrow.

Taira moved closer to the translator.

"I have two readings that don't match. That means I haven't explained them, not that he explained them. Talk about what comes next. Don't place today's failure beside his cabin as if they're the same thing."

Arved looked at her.

"They're not the same. But if it appears by shared supply, all of us will deal with the consequences."

One of the women asked where Asgar worked now. Bertram explained.

Then the man who on the first day had been taking apart the long housing in the outer courtyard spoke. Asgar had seen him almost every day since: he worked on supply and was responsible for the nodes they had physically separated today.

"The equipment should be moved away from his house," he said.

Taira started to object, but he continued without raising his voice.

"I've been carrying water in buckets since dawn. Not because he's guilty—I didn't say that. It's just that when something breaks, I go fix it, and Arved goes explain why a man who has walls breaking around him lives here." He looked across the table at Asgar. "They tell you interesting things about you. They tell me where I'll be removing a panel tomorrow."

Asgar reached toward the translator to answer and discovered he had nothing to say. All of it was true.

"I'll move whatever you say," he said.

"You'll move it. I'll be the one putting it back."

He did not address Asgar again.

Asgar looked at the craftsman. Bertram sat with his fingers interlaced and did not promise that everything would remain as before.

No decision about Asgar was made that evening. They agreed to return to the question separately.

Asgar walked home alone.

At the door he found the board and pieces. Tobin was waiting on the step, but when he saw the covering above Asgar's eyebrow, he stopped arranging them.

"Does it hurt?"

Asgar understood without the device.

"A little."

The boy moved the board, making room.

They played one game. Asgar lost quickly, and this time Tobin did not celebrate aloud.

"Play normally," Asgar said.

He had to explain through the translator. Tobin objected: he was playing normally.

They began the second game already arguing.

---

The skin above his eyebrow healed before the courtyards regained their former coolness.

A thin pale line remained where the cut had been. Asgar noticed it one morning while washing. He leaned toward the mirror and turned his head. The line almost disappeared if he stepped back. He lingered another second.

He remembered the frozen frame from the stadium: gray hair, a thin face, breaking into blotches under enlargement. Asgar ran a finger beside the scar, turned the water on again, and washed his face.

Then he shut off the water and stepped away from the mirror.

Later Bertram checked whether the scar made him squint in the light. Asgar said no. The craftsman nodded and asked him to hold a removed drive.

By then they already had several tasks Asgar could do without having every step explained. Bertram still took the complicated work himself. Sometimes Asgar rushed, sometimes forgot to put back a tool; the craftsman grumbled and pointed to the empty place in the kit.

Taira did not come by every day. Sometimes they saw each other only at dinner, and she talked to other people while Asgar sat beside them, understanding more and more but still losing the thread when everyone began interrupting one another. He learned to ask for repetition without the translator. Learned to recognize when they were making fun of him before he understood the words.

One evening Taira asked him to clear the table by the window tomorrow for a parts inspection.

"Tobin's board is there now."

"Move it."

"He'll say I moved the pieces."

"Then finish the game tonight."

Asgar looked at the unfinished position. He no longer remembered whose turn it was.

---

On the one hundred third morning, Asgar woke before the signal.

It was cloudy outside. He dressed, made the bed, and moved the game board to a low cabinet, careful not to disturb the pieces. He had to steady one with a finger. Tobin had refused to finish the night before: he said he wanted to think.

Taira came after breakfast. She checked the cleared table, put fasteners for a new section of canopy on it, and asked him to help carry them tomorrow.

"After the first signal?"

"Yes. Bertram will be waiting at the lower passage."

Asgar smiled.

"I won't be late."

"That's what I'll tell him."

She crossed the courtyard, stopped to talk to the woman by the water. Asgar went back into the house for the work tool.

Someone knocked when he was already about to leave.

Bertram stood in the doorway. No bag, no removed part in his hands. He looked at the tool Asgar was holding and said:

"Leave it for now."

Asgar did not put it down immediately.

"What?"

"There's a red cabin by the river. People moved away. I called Taira."

He spoke slowly so Asgar could understand without translation.

Asgar looked at the table by the window. The fasteners prepared for tomorrow lay there, waiting to be carried down. He put the tool beside them, returned to the door, then stopped again and took the jacket off the hook.

The phone remained beside the bed.

Asgar took it and the documents.

They walked to the river along a familiar path. At the turn where water collected after rain, Bertram went around the edge while Asgar, out of habit, stepped on the flat stone. He had put it there himself several weeks ago after getting tired of returning with a wet boot.

Taira was waiting on the bank. Two more people stood nearby; farther along the path Arved appeared. No one went down into the water.

The booth stood in the middle of the shallow river.

The current bent around the red wall and gathered into ripples in front of it. The telephone was visible through the glass. Asgar looked at the lower part of the door and could not reconcile what he saw: outside, the water rose higher; inside, its surface lay noticeably lower. A straight impossible boundary ran between the two levels.

He took a step toward the descent.

Taira touched his arm.

"Wait. Look from here first."

Asgar stopped beside her.

Behind him, higher up the path, was the house with the unfinished game. Here in the water stood the door he had waited for so many days and then stopped waiting for each morning.

He looked at it without going down from the bank.
