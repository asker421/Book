Chapter 16. What Should We Call You

"The transition is complete. They're finishing the check. The previous operator has gone off shift; I'm with you now."

Asgar took his palm off the frame. Dust remained on his skin, damp in the folds of his fingers. The voice was different: the woman spoke a little more slowly and did not cut off the ends of her sentences even when the receiver crackled.

"Is she all right?"

"Yes. My name is Mirena. I handle emergency accompaniment."

"And where are you?"

"On Earth. In two thousand seven hundred forty-four. At the service's ground facility. Not where you just were. We're far from that settlement."

Asgar looked at the receiver.

"Then how are you talking to me now?"

"We don't move with you. The service connected to the device's own service channel. When it transitions, we remain here, and the line continues through its next states."

"So you're calling me in the future?"

"No. We can't call a chosen time at all. We only have this device and its service circuit. As long as the circuit maintains the connection, your telephone remains the same channel for us wherever the device ends up."

"And if the connection breaks?"

"Then we stay here. And we won't know where you are until the circuit gives us a signal again."

He leaned a shoulder against the wall beside the telephone. His legs ached after the walk, and the jacket made it hard to bend the arm holding the receiver. Through the glass, nothing was visible yet except his own faint reflection.

"Do you know where I was?"

"The last exit was the year 2744. Something outside was interfering with the transition. Then you passed a check by the local system, the influence decreased, you went inside and closed the door. Did I miss anything important?"

"Damir stayed by the booth. Radmila and Nazar are back at the settlement."

"You want to know what happened to them?"

"Yes."

"I can't. After the transition, that location disappears for me."

He was silent. In the dark glass hung his hand holding the black receiver. Damir had raised a thumb in farewell, then tapped his own knee twice. Radmila and Nazar had stayed in the settlement, beyond a route Asgar could not have crossed alone. Now all he could do was hope Damir made it back.

"For you, everything ends when the door closes," he said.

"There—yes. With you, the line remains for now."

Light appeared along the lower edge of the window. Gray at first, then warm, with a yellow cast. Asgar straightened. Flat stones emerged beyond the glass, a narrow strip of soil, and grass stems beside a wall. Higher up was a roof; its edge blocked part of the sky.

He turned as far as the cord allowed. On the other side, there was a passage between low buildings and a staircase with broad steps. A piece of folded cloth lay on the bottom step, held down by a round object. Nothing ruined. Nothing flying over the roofs.

"There are houses here."

"Tell me what's in front of the door."

"A stone platform. Flat. Wall on the left, passage on the right. I don't see any people yet."

Mirena asked him to wait. The line went quiet, but she did not disappear: he heard her take one shallow breath before speaking again.

"Air, pressure, temperature—normal. You can open the door."

Asgar put his hand on the handle but did not press it. Something moved in the passage beyond the booth. Low, pale; it flashed between the walls and disappeared.

"There's a machine."

"Far?"

"I don't know. At the end of the passage. I only saw the edge of it."

"Is it approaching?"

"I can't see it at all now."

He leaned toward the glass. From here, the corner of the building blocked almost the entire passage. To look farther, he would have to go outside.

"Can we check again?"

"We can. There's enough time for one more check. But the instruments still won't show me what the machine is doing behind the wall."

"What do you think?"

"I'd wait a minute. You barely saw it."

Asgar released the handle.

"Let's wait."

"All right."

He remained by the door. Before, he could be angry at an instruction, hurry because he was told to, stand because he was forbidden to move. Now he had chosen himself to spend time he could not see on any clock.

The pale edge appeared again. This time Asgar made out a long flat body, several dark recesses along the side, and a thin stream of water running down to the ground. The device moved along the wall. It stopped beside the plants, waited, and slowly continued on.

He watched until it disappeared around the next corner.

"Looks like it's watering."

"What do you see?"

"Water is running. It stops by the plantings. It never turned toward me."

"The readings are the same. The machine isn't coming toward you. Shall we exit?"

He wanted to wait until the machine came back. See it a second time. Make sure there was no other device behind it. Asgar caught himself wanting that and looked over the empty platform beyond the threshold.

"We exit."

"All right. Better not stretch the window any further now."

He put his palm on the handle again. It was strange to stand in a red booth with a telephone to his ear waiting for a machine from an unknown year to finish watering plants.

"You really can't see it at all?"

"No. Only through what you tell me."

Asgar looked at the device.

"And me?"

"Not you either."

He involuntarily looked up at the ceiling corners even though he had examined this cramped cabin many times. Red frames, glass, the wall with the telephone. Nothing new.

"Then what do you have?"

"I can see that someone inside is moving. I receive some indicators of your state and data from the circuit. I'll notice a change. Why it happened—I can't always tell."

He shifted his weight to relieve his right leg. The bandage pulled at the skin.

"Right now?"

"You moved. I can't see what you did."

"Shifted onto the other leg."

"Does it hurt?"

Asgar looked down at his trousers. Dirt had dried on the knee; the fabric around the abrasion was stiff.

"After the last exit I fell on a slope. Scraped my knee. They cleaned it and bandaged it. I walked a lot today."

"Is it bleeding now?"

"I can't tell. I haven't removed the bandage."

"I can't assess the wound from these data. Want me to bring in a medic?"

"Not yet."

"Then check the dressing after you get out. If it's bleeding again, don't play hero—find help."

Asgar relaxed his hand slightly. His fingers were tired from holding the heavy receiver; he moved it to his other ear, drawing the cord across his chest.

"And the person checking all of this? The field, the transition..."

"The engineer watches the technical side. I talk to you."

"So when you say 'you can,' he decided?"

"He confirms the technical part. I combine that with what I hear from you. But you're still the one who presses the handle."

"And if you say I can't?"

"I'll tell you why. If I don't understand myself, I'll say that too."

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the door handle. There was a small depression in it he had not noticed before. He started feeling for it with his fingernail, then stopped.

"Where have you brought me?"

This time Mirena did not answer immediately.

"We have an external date. By your calendar—June seventh, nine thousand three hundred eight."

He kept looking at the handle.

"Repeat that."

She repeated it.

Beyond the glass lay the folded cloth on the step. Its edge moved slightly. Creases had formed around the object weighing it down—ordinary creases, like those left in a towel thrown over the back of a chair.

Asgar could not connect them to the number she had given.

"Nine thousand?"

"Yes."

He tried to subtract two thousand fourteen, lost track, and stopped. Even a thousand years would not fit in his head. There might not be a single familiar word left here, not one street, not one country name he could pronounce in a way anyone understood.

He pulled at the jacket collar. It felt stuffy.

"Why here?"

"I didn't choose the date."

"I understand you didn't personally. Who did?"

"No one. We don't have a button like that."

He turned away from the window. He wanted to put the receiver back on the cradle and end the conversation for at least a minute. But then he would be alone with the number.

"Do you know at all how to get me back?"

"To the date you need—no."

"What do you have?"

The line was quiet. Mirena did not hurry to fill the pause, and because of that, when the answer came, it sounded clearer.

"We can accompany the transitions while they keep appearing. We're looking for a way to break your connection to the system so it stops pulling you onward."

Asgar pressed the receiver closer.

"So the booth stops appearing around me?"

"If we find a way—yes."

"And then I can stay?"

"Then you won't have to enter again just because the cabin returned."

"Here?"

"We can't do that here right now."

He slowly exhaled. Not an offer, not a ready-made way out of everything that had happened. And still, for the first time the conversation concerned the possibility that one day he might stop looking for the red door.

He remembered Katya.

He had not agreed to anything, yet he was already imagining waking up and not waiting for the booth. Leaving the phone on the table, going out for bread, and returning the same day. In that imagined day there was no home he was trying to reach.

"I want to go back," he said.

"I know."

"That doesn't mean I've agreed to stay."

"I didn't hear agreement. You asked—I answered."

Asgar closed his eyes and for a moment rested his forehead against the cool glass. His breath fogged it. He leaned back, wiped the mark away with the sleeve of the jacket Nazar had given him.

"Tell me in advance if that option appears."

"I'll tell you when there's something to offer."

"And how much time I'll have to decide."

"Absolutely."

He still disliked almost everything he had learned. But when Mirena again paused before answering, Asgar caught himself waiting not for an order. For an answer. Or an honest "I don't know."

Asgar pressed the handle.

"Opening."

The heavy door yielded. Warm air carrying the smell of damp earth entered the cabin. Water dripped somewhere, and farther away, beyond the buildings, voices could be heard. He could not make out the words.

He held the door with his free hand, remaining inside. The cord allowed him to stand right at the threshold; the receiver tugged slightly at his wrist.

The platform was dry except for a narrow dark strip beside the wall. There were no machines or people in front of the door. Beyond the passage, another roof was visible, leaves above a fence, and pale sky.

"The door is open," he said. "Breathing feels normal."

"Once you step out, the telephone stays in the cabin. As long as it's here, you'll be able to return to the connection."

"I know."

He said it more sharply than he intended. Mirena did not answer.

Asgar looked at the receiver. Simply holding it to his ear and hearing understandable speech was now the easiest action in the world. Beyond the threshold, he would again have to explain who he was, search for words, ask for water, and hope people allowed him near.

"Mirena."

"Yes?"

"If your shift changes, pass on about the knee. So I don't have to tell it from the beginning again."

"I will."

He nodded, then remembered she could not see it.

"All right. I'm going out."

Asgar returned the receiver to the cradle. Holding the door, he turned toward the light and first placed his left foot on the platform. Shifted his weight and carefully stepped with the right.

Warm air touched his sweaty back beneath the jacket.

He let go of the handle. The door remained open behind him. Asgar stood beside it, listening to voices beyond the houses.
