Chapter 16. What Should We Call You

“The transition completed. They’re finishing the checks. The previous operator has gone off shift; I’m with you now.”

Asgar took his palm off the frame. Dust remained on his skin, damp in the folds of his fingers.

The voice was different.

This woman spoke a little more slowly and did not clip the ends of her sentences even when the line crackled.

“Is she all right?”

“Yes. My name is Mirena. I handle emergency support.”

“And where are you?”

“On Earth. In 2744. At the service’s ground facility. Not where you just were. We’re a long way from that settlement.”

Asgar looked at the receiver.

“Then how are you talking to me now?”

“We don’t travel with you. The service connected to the apparatus’s own service channel. When it transitions, we remain here, and the line continues through its subsequent states.”

“So you’re calling me into the future?”

“No. We don’t know how to call a chosen time at all. We only have this apparatus and its service circuit. As long as the circuit maintains the link, your telephone remains the same channel for us, wherever the apparatus appears.”

“And if the connection breaks?”

“Then we stay here. And we won’t know where you are until the circuit gives us another signal.”

He leaned a shoulder against the wall beside the telephone.

His legs throbbed after the walk. The jacket made it awkward to bend the arm holding the receiver.

Beyond the glass, nothing could yet be distinguished except his own faint reflection.

“You know where I was?”

“Your last exit was 2744. Something outside was interfering with the transition. Then you passed an access review by a local system, the interaction decreased, you entered, and closed the door. Did I miss anything important?”

“Damir stayed by the booth. Radmila and Nazar are at the settlement.”

“You want to know what happened to them?”

“Yes.”

“I can’t. Once the transition happens, that location disappears from my access.”

He was quiet.

His hand holding the black receiver floated in the dark glass.

Damir had given him a thumbs-up goodbye, then tapped his own knee twice.

Radmila and Nazar had stayed at the settlement beyond a route Asgar could never have made alone.

Now all he could do was hope Damir got back.

“For you everything ends when the door closes,” he said.

“At that location, yes. The line to you is still here.”

Light appeared at the lower edge of the window.

Gray at first, then warm, tinged yellow.

Asgar straightened.

Flat stones emerged beyond the glass, a narrow strip of soil, blades of grass beside a wall.

Above them was a roof; its edge cut off part of the sky.

He turned as far as the cord allowed.

On the other side he could see a passage between low buildings and a staircase with broad steps.

A folded piece of cloth lay on the bottom step, held down by a round object.

Nothing ruined.

Nothing flying above the roofs.

“There are houses here.”

“Tell me what’s in front of the door.”

“A stone surface. Level. Wall on the left, passage on the right. I don’t see anyone yet.”

Mirena asked him to wait.

The line went quiet, but she was still there; he heard her take one shallow breath before speaking again.

“Air, pressure, temperature all normal. You can open the door.”

Asgar took hold of the handle but did not press it.

Something was moving in the passage beyond the booth.

Low and pale.

It flashed between sections of wall and disappeared.

“There’s a machine.”

“How far?”

“I don’t know. At the end of the passage. I only saw part of it.”

“Is it coming closer?”

“I can’t see it now at all.”

He leaned toward the glass.

From here, the corner of the building blocked almost the entire passage.

To see farther, he would have to step outside.

“Can we check again?”

“Yes. There’s time for one more check. But the instruments still won’t tell me what the machine is doing behind that wall.”

“What do you think?”

“I’d wait a minute. You barely saw it.”

Asgar released the handle.

“Let’s wait.”

“All right.”

He stayed beside the door.

Before, he could be angry at an instruction, rush because someone ordered him to, remain still because he was forbidden to move.

Now he had chosen to spend time he could see on no clock.

The pale edge appeared again.

This time Asgar made out a long flat body, several dark recesses along the side, and a thin stream of water running down to the ground.

The device moved beside the wall.

Stopped near the plants.

Waited.

Then slowly continued.

He watched until it disappeared around the next corner.

“Looks like it’s watering.”

“What do you see?”

“Water coming out. It stops by the plantings. It never turned toward me.”

“The readings are unchanged. The machine isn’t approaching you. Shall we exit?”

He wanted to wait for the machine to come back.

Watch a second time.

Make sure there was no other device behind it.

Asgar caught himself wanting that and looked over the empty surface outside the threshold.

“Let’s go.”

“All right. Better not extend the window any longer now.”

He put his hand on the handle again.

It was strange to stand inside a red telephone booth with the receiver at his ear, waiting for a machine from an unknown year to finish watering plants.

“You couldn’t see it at all?”

“No. Only through your description.”

Asgar looked at the telephone.

“What about me?”

“Not you either.”

He involuntarily glanced up at the ceiling corners, though he had examined the cramped interior many times already.

Red frames.

Glass.

The wall with the telephone.

Nothing new.

“Then what do you have?”

“I can see that someone inside is moving. I receive some indicators of your condition and data from the circuit. I’ll notice a change. I won’t always know why it happened.”

He shifted his weight off his right leg.

The dressing pulled at the skin.

“What about now?”

“You moved. I can’t see what you did.”

“Shifted onto the other leg.”

“Does it hurt?”

Asgar looked down at his trousers.

Dried dirt covered the knee. Around the scuffed area, the fabric had gone stiff.

“After the last exit I fell on a slope. Scraped my knee. They cleaned it and bandaged it. I walked a lot today.”

“Is it bleeding now?”

“I don’t think so. I haven’t removed the dressing.”

“I can’t assess the wound from these readings. Do you want me to bring a medic onto the line?”

“Not yet.”

“Then check the dressing after you exit. If it’s bleeding again, don’t play hero. Find help.”

Asgar relaxed his grip slightly.

His fingers were tired from holding the heavy receiver. He shifted it to the other ear, drawing the cord across his chest.

“And whoever checks all this? The field, the transition…”

“The engineer handles the technical side. I talk to you.”

“So when you say ‘you can,’ that’s his decision?”

“He confirms the technical part. I combine that with what I hear from you. You still press the handle.”

“And if you tell me I can’t?”

“I’ll tell you why. If I don’t understand myself, I’ll say that too.”

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the door handle.

There was a small indentation he had never noticed before.

He started feeling for it with his thumbnail and stopped.

“Where did you bring me?”

This time Mirena did not answer immediately.

“We have an external date. By your calendar: June seventh, 9308.”

He kept looking at the handle.

“Repeat that.”

She did.

Beyond the glass, the folded cloth lay on the step.

Its edge moved slightly.

The round object holding it down created ordinary folds around it—the same kind a towel made when thrown over the back of a chair.

Asgar could not connect those folds with the number he had just heard.

“Nine thousand?”

“Yes.”

He tried subtracting 2014 and lost track.

Stopped trying.

Even a thousand years would not fit into his head.

There might not be a single familiar word left here, not one street, not one country name he could pronounce in a way anyone understood.

He tugged at the jacket collar.

It felt stuffy.

“Why here?”

“I didn’t choose the date.”

“I understand you didn’t personally. Who did?”

“No one. There is no button for that.”

He turned away from the window.

He wanted to put the receiver down and end the conversation for just one minute.

But then he would be alone with the number.

“Do you know how to send me back at all?”

“To the date you want? No.”

“What do you have?”

The line was quiet.

Mirena did not rush to fill the pause, and because of that, when the answer came it was clearer.

“We can support the transitions as long as they keep happening. We’re looking for a way to break your connection to the system so it stops pulling you onward.”

Asgar pressed the receiver closer.

“So the booth would stop appearing after me?”

“If we find a way, yes.”

“And then I could stay?”

“Then you wouldn’t have to enter again just because the booth came back.”

“Here?”

“We can’t do it here right now.”

He exhaled slowly.

Not an offer.

Not a finished way out of everything that had happened.

And still, for the first time, the conversation touched on the possibility that one day he might stop looking for the red door.

He thought about Katya.

He had not agreed to anything, and yet he was already imagining waking up without waiting for the booth.

Leaving the phone on the table.

Going out for bread and returning on the same day.

In that imagined day, there was no home he was still trying to reach.

“I want to go back,” he said.

“I know.”

“That doesn’t mean I’ve agreed to stay.”

“I didn’t hear an agreement. You asked. I answered.”

Asgar closed his eyes and rested his forehead against the cool glass for a moment.

His breath fogged it.

He drew back and wiped the mark with the sleeve of the jacket Nazar had given him.

“Tell me in advance if that option appears.”

“I’ll tell you when there’s something real to offer.”

“And how much time I have to decide.”

“Absolutely.”

He still disliked almost everything he had learned.

But when Mirena fell silent again before answering, Asgar caught himself waiting not for an order.

For an answer.

Or an honest *I don’t know.*

Asgar pressed the handle.

“Opening.”

The heavy door gave.

Warm air smelling of damp earth entered the booth.

Somewhere water was dripping, and farther away beyond the buildings came voices.

He could not make out the words.

He held the door with his free hand and remained inside.

The cord allowed him to stand directly at the threshold; the receiver tugged slightly at his wrist.

The surface outside was dry except for a narrow dark strip beside the wall.

No machines or people stood in front of the door.

Beyond the passage he could see another roof, leaves above a fence, pale sky.

“Door’s open,” he said. “Breathing feels normal.”

“Once you step outside, the telephone remains in the booth. As long as it stays here, you can come back to the line.”

“I know.”

It came out sharper than he meant.

Mirena did not answer.

Asgar looked at the receiver.

Simply holding it to his ear and hearing comprehensible speech was the easiest thing in the world right now.

Across the threshold, he would once again have to explain who he was, search for words, ask for water, hope people allowed him to approach.

“Mirena.”

“Yes?”

“If you go off shift, pass on the thing about my knee. So I don’t have to explain it from the beginning again.”

“I will.”

He nodded, then remembered she could not see it.

“All right. I’m going out.”

Asgar replaced the receiver.

Holding the door, he turned toward the light and put his left foot onto the stone first.

Transferred his weight.

Carefully brought out the right.

Warm air touched the sweaty skin beneath his jacket.

He released the handle.

The door remained open behind him.

Asgar stood beside it, listening to voices beyond the houses.
