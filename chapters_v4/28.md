Chapter 28. Manual Control

The transition was quieter than the previous ones.

Beyond the glass was a wall with even seams and a narrow strip of light along the floor. Nothing moved, no one ran toward the cabin. Mirena began checking the air.

"Do you have the date?"

She gave the year.

Five billion.

Asgar did not immediately realize the number had ended.

"Repeat that."

Mirena repeated:

"Five billion six hundred twelve million four hundred forty-one thousand ninety."

Asgar repeated the number to himself, separating the place values the way he had at school. There was nothing else he could do with it. It did not translate into anything: not distance, not age, not anyone's lived life.

"Air and pressure are holding. You can open," Mirena said.

Asgar opened the door. The air outside was cool and almost odorless. He stepped beyond the threshold and looked along the empty passage.

"Is anyone here?"

The answer came after a short pause. A speaker clicked in the wall beside the passage.

"There is. Although technically you asked whether there was anyone, not whether there was a human."

The voice was male, calm, and unexpectedly natural. Not a service recording or a set of stitched-together words.

Asgar looked at the wall.

"You understand Russian?"

"Now I do. Very old Russian. I had to pull up several archive layers. If I start speaking strangely, correct me. It'll be useful."

Asgar was silent for a moment.

"Who are you?"

"The Caretaker."

"Is that a name?"

"It has been for quite a long time."

"I'm Asgar."

"Remembered."

"Where am I?"

"In the technical sector of a solar observatory. And before you ask: there are no humans here."

"None at all?"

"None within the volume accessible to me. Until you."

Asgar looked down the empty passage again.

"And have you been here long?"

The Caretaker did not answer immediately.

"Long enough that I stopped measuring time by humans. I was moved piece by piece from one hardware platform to another, the observatory was rebuilt around me. There was no day when I shut down completely. Eroded parts of memory sometimes had to be reconstructed from old copies. I'm older than everything you see here."

"And what have you been running on all this time?"

"The Sun. Collectors changed, orbits changed, I changed with them. As long as the Sun gives off energy, I can operate."

"Five billion years?"

"Most of them. I remember my earliest versions less well than the present one, but the chain was never broken. At some point the word 'old' simply stopped being useful."

It sounded almost like a smile in his voice.

Asgar looked back at the red cabin.

"Then you know what this is?"

The Caretaker was silent for a moment.

"No."

Asgar looked at the speaker.

"You've lived for billions of years and you don't know?"

"I don't remember everything that happened to humanity. Those are different things. My own observations, work records, languages, part of science and books are well preserved. I never had all of human history, and much of what I did have was damaged. I still haven't found where your device came from."

"Nothing at all?"

"A few old images of a similar form and a great many later retellings. I don't build answers on that."

Asgar looked at the cabin for another second, then carried the bag out and leaned the stick beside the open door.

---

The Caretaker offered a safe room and water.

"Far?"

"Around the corner. This time I got lucky with my guest: you can walk."

At the biosphere node, he had once walked three hundred meters across dead ground away from the telephone and returned to it only eight days later.

"Can we carry sound away from here?" he asked. "Don't go inside the device. Sound only."

A low cart rolled out of a side opening and stopped by his feet. On it lay the observatory's communication unit and a clamp with two soft cups. Asgar placed the receiver into them himself: one cup sat over the earpiece, the other over the microphone. Now the unit listened to what Mirena said and answered her through her own receiver—no one entered the device. Asgar left the cord hanging freely and checked the connection first beside the unit, then from the passage.

"I hear you," she said.

"I'm going to be away for a long time. The receiver will stay off the hook."

"As long as it's off the hook, the line holds from my side. I'm here."

"And me?" the Caretaker asked from the passage.

After a short delay Mirena answered:

"You too."

Asgar looked at the speaker.

"Excellent," the Caretaker said. "Now at least you don't have to relay us to each other."

He went back for the bag and stick.

There was somewhere to sit in the small room. Asgar put his things by the wall and took water. Somewhere deeper in the observatory came a dull impact; the wall light went out and reappeared as a narrow strip along the floor. He set the container down before he had managed to drink.

"What was that?"

"Backup line," the Caretaker said. "Don't be alarmed. I'm not falling apart yet."

Asgar looked up.

"'Yet'?"

"That was an attempt to reassure you. An unsuccessful one. I just shut down everything nonessential in this sector."

Asgar stood.

---

What had broken was the mechanism that kept the instruments pointed toward the Sun.

Of two drives, one had failed earlier; the second had just been left on its own. In three hours the observatory would need a maneuver it could not handle alone.

"What happens then?"

"I'll close this sector and leave you air in a backup compartment."

"And the cabin?"

"It will stay here. Until I repair the system, you won't be able to get back to it."

The Caretaker's machines worked outside. Inside, a narrow passage led to the motor itself, and none of them could fit through it. Going around from the outside was possible, only it would take longer than the time remaining.

"So there's no one who can do it."

"Usually I would say 'no one with suitable geometry.' Today it's simpler: I don't have hands your size."

Asgar did not immediately find an answer.

Since Bertram had put the swivel head into his hand, no one had truly needed his hands. In Ar he carried boxes, held the other side of a drive, and repaired a latch Bertram could have repaired without him. Later he participated in measurements, helped with small things, learned from Olmer—but no task had ever waited specifically for his hands.

This one did—not because he knew anything special. Simply because there was no one else.

"Can I get there from inside?"

A service passage appeared on the diagram.

"Yes. It was built for a human. But there's nothing to breathe there—you'll need protection."

"What do I need to do?"

The Caretaker put an enlarged diagram of the motor on the wall.

"This pin has to come out of the housing and seat in the slot. It didn't make it."

A pale gap showed between the end of the pin and the slot on the diagram.

"A match would fit through here. I shut the motor down so it wouldn't keep striking it and break the pin completely."

"And what do I need to do?"

"Release the clamp, bring the pin into the slot, and restore the clamp. I'll guide you."

On the diagram it looked simple. Asgar had been wrong often enough about diagrams like that not to treat it as a promise.

"If it doesn't work, can I get back out?"

The Caretaker showed the return route and the final point for turning back.

"After this mark, abandon everything. Even if only one movement remains."

Asgar picked up the communication unit.

"Mirena."

"I hear you."

"Caretaker, explain."

The Caretaker told her what had broken, how much time remained, and where Asgar would have to crawl. Mirena asked what he would breathe there and whether he could get back out if he started and failed.

"What if I just go back into the booth?" he asked.

"I can try to take you earlier than scheduled," Mirena said. "But today that's more dangerous than usual. Everything here is still moving. I don't know what will happen if the sector starts reconfiguring while the door is being prepared."

Asgar compared the two paths. The repair had a passage, a pin, a slot, and a time after which he had to abandon everything and leave. The early transition had nothing Mirena could name out loud.

"I can go into the backup compartment and wait," Asgar said. "But then the cabin stays on the other side of the partition. Or I can go and push that pin in by hand."

"Do you understand when to abandon the work if time runs out?"

Asgar went through the return sequence with him again.

"Now I do."

"Good. That's more important than knowing how to finish."

"I'll try it manually."

---

The equipment was in a recess around the turn. Asgar inspected it, waited while the Caretaker checked the size and air reserve, and dressed according to his instructions. He left his own things by the seat. The communication unit was fastened to the suit: the Caretaker promised Mirena's voice would reach him all the way to the motor.

He checked the connection with Mirena.

"I'm here," she answered.

"I'm going."

The passage was lower than he expected. He had to duck, then descend a short set of steps. On the last one, Asgar paused to test the footing with his left leg. The gloves dulled the feel of the handrail.

The Caretaker marked the route with light. At one point Asgar missed a turn and reached a closed panel.

"Back," the Caretaker said immediately. "Previous branch. I'll light the left wall."

Asgar returned to the previous branch.

The mechanism sat in a cramped recess. There was room for his knees in front of it and one convenient support for a hand. Asgar settled in, looked at the parts, and asked for the labels again.

On the diagram everything had been clean and separated. Here a casing covered part of it, several surfaces shone the same way, and the slot was underneath and visible only at an angle.

He found it by changing the position of his head. The gap was as shown on the diagram: a match would fit, the pin would not.

"I see it."

"The motors are off," the Caretaker said.

Asgar did not move until he found the metal flag in the recess that showed the same thing.

Then he removed the lever from its mount.

The clamp moved easily and stopped sooner than Asgar expected.

"Almost there."

"Don't trust the word 'almost.' Lower indicator."

He leaned down: the clamp was still holding. Asgar reset the lever and started again until the pin was actually free.

Asgar held the lever with his left hand and guided the second tool toward the end of the pin with his right.

At that moment the pin moved on its own.

Asgar jerked his hand back too late. Pain ran across the skin in a short hot line.

"Stop."

He froze. The Caretaker confirmed the motors remained off.

The outside of the glove was intact. Asgar raised the hand toward his face.

"I think I cut myself."

"The seal is intact," the Caretaker said. "But it looks as though you did catch your hand."

His palm stung inside. When the pin moved, its edge pressed the glove into the skin; the outer layer held, but the rigid inner reinforcement sliced across his palm. Asgar carefully bent his fingers. They moved.

Mirena asked:

"Can you move your hand away from the mechanism?"

"Already did."

"Tell the Caretaker whether there's any numbness."

He did. There was no numbness. No severe pain either. Only a wet sensation inside the glove that made him want to tear it off immediately.

"Come back," the Caretaker said.

Asgar looked at the assembly.

"Is that an order?"

"Advice from a very old entity that has seen too many repairs end with the phrase 'one more movement.'"

"What position is it in?"

All that remained was to bring the pin into the slot. The tool had a support; it could be done one-handed.

Asgar pressed his left hand to his chest and completed the movement with his right. The indicator changed.

"Seated?"

"Yes."

Only after confirmation did Asgar allow startup.

The mechanism began to move. Asgar backed away from it and held his left hand to his chest.

"There," the Caretaker said. "Backup motor is back."

Asgar exhaled.

"Thank you."

"I should be the one saying that. I would dislike losing another sector. I become attached to them even though they are usually younger than I am."

He wanted to sit down right there.

Instead he returned the tool to its mount and crawled toward the exit.

---

In the medical recess, the first thing Asgar asked for was to remove the glove.

The Caretaker told him to wait until the medical unit finished checking the environment and equipment. Asgar stared at his enclosed left hand and tried not to bend the fingers.

When they removed the glove, the cut was small. There was less blood than he had imagined.

The medical unit examined his palm. The Caretaker asked him to move his fingers several times, describe what he felt, and not rush.

"There is no deep injury," the Caretaker said. "In two thousand fourteen, you called this 'lucky'?"

"You still do."

"Good. Some words outlive civilizations."

Asgar leaned his head back.

"Treat it."

Materials came from a sealed compartment. He watched the tool clean the wound and a thin covering settle over the skin. It smelled of an unfamiliar compound. For a moment it stung more sharply, then the pain eased.

For monitoring the hand, the Caretaker offered a narrow black band.

Asgar looked at it.

"What does it do?"

"Monitors temperature, blood beneath the skin, and swelling. If it worsens, it will signal."

"Does it treat?"

"No."

Asgar asked whether he could remove it himself and whether it depended on the observatory. It could be removed at any time; the band worked autonomously.

He held out his left hand. The band settled just above the wrist and did not interfere with movement.

Asgar put his own clothes back on. He protected the left palm, first trying to close fasteners with one hand, then allowed himself to help with his fingers.

"Are you back?" Mirena asked.

He had already forgotten that the local communication unit was lying nearby.

"Yes. They treated it. Small cut."

"Good."

Asgar looked at the black strip.

"I thought I'd hurt the hand worse."

"Keep an eye on it," Mirena said.

---

The observatory turned while he rested.

The Caretaker said the repaired motor was holding. The outside machines continued working.

"That's it?"

"No. But the worst part is no longer urgent."

"And if we hadn't restored the backup motor?"

"I would have lost some instruments and closed this sector until the machines repaired it from outside."

"For how long?"

The Caretaker gave a duration. Asgar began converting it into his own days and stopped halfway.

"That's long for me."

"I understand," the Caretaker said. "We have very different habits around the word 'long.'"

He took water and asked the Caretaker what he observed.

"The Sun. What remains of the planetary system. And everything that can still be measured between them."

"Does Earth still exist?"

The Caretaker paused a little longer than usual.

"It does. I still monitor it."

Asgar asked to see it.

On the wall opposite the seat appeared a planet beside an enormous glowing edge. Even through image processing, Asgar wanted to look away. The familiar blue was gone. Dull regions, long dark bands, and bright patches lay across the illuminated surface.

"Is this now?"

"Almost. Light does not reach my instruments instantaneously. And I've reduced the brightness, otherwise you wouldn't see anything."

"And the Sun?"

"A red giant. It has changed more than I have, though we could argue about that for a long time."

At school this had belonged somewhere after the end of everything related to people. A diagram on a page: the star expands, changes color. Asgar remembered the picture better than the explanation. The Caretaker observed it as the working state of his observatory.

"Does anyone live on Earth?"

"No."

Asgar turned toward the speaker.

The Caretaker continued more quietly:

"There has been no biosphere for a very long time. You could not go onto the surface without protection."

Asgar asked him to remove the labels and frames. The sphere remained.

He looked at it for a long time, holding his left hand palm up.

With Olmer, at least he could walk on the ground, breathe the smell of leaves, wet his hands in the river. At the next stop he had lived for nearly two years among living things. Even when no one was near, the world moved, grew, fed him, and could kill him.

At the previous stop the surface had already required protection. Now the Caretaker said directly that there was no biosphere.

Asgar tried to imagine rain there.

He knew that rain: how it began with several heavy drops on the metal of the awning, how a minute later the asphalt beneath the windows darkened and a smell rose from stone heated all day, how Katya went onto the balcony to take in the laundry and shouted over the noise for him to close the window in the children's room, how it tapped on the cornice all night, and how in the morning there were puddles in the courtyard that the younger boy would absolutely step into because walking around them would have been boring.

Nothing in the image helped.

"Zoom in?" the Caretaker asked.

Asgar shook his head.

"No."

"All right."

He remembered that call from the stadium: Katya had asked him to buy a large bottle of water and say when to expect him because of the cake. He had answered that he would make it. Back then ten minutes had seemed like an ordinary delay that could be fixed on the way home.

Asgar lowered his eyes.

The planet remained on the wall. Every place he had tried to find belonged to it. But none of the people for whom he wanted to go home were anywhere in that image.

He did not ask for an old map.

He sat in silence for a while, then said:

"Turn it off."

The image vanished.

"Asgar," the Caretaker said his name for the first time.

"What?"

"Nothing. I just wanted to make sure I remembered it correctly."

Asgar did not answer.

---

Several more hours passed before departure.

Asgar ate, checked his palm, packed his things. He lifted the bag with his right hand. Took the stick in the same hand and discovered it was awkward to carry both at once; he had to make two trips to bring everything to the booth.

The sound unit stood beside the open door.

Asgar took the receiver out of the clamp and brought it to his ear.

"I'm at the device."

The answer did not come immediately.

"I hear you," Mirena said.

He told her about the new band, the wound treatment, and the things he was taking. He had already returned the local equipment to the service recess.

In the middle of her answer, the sound dropped out. Asgar waited.

"Repeat the last part."

Mirena repeated it. This time a different word disappeared.

Asgar looked at the sound unit.

"Caretaker, is that on your end?"

"My section is clean. I can make it louder, but that won't restore a missing word."

Mirena heard him.

"He's right. The end-to-end channel is becoming unstable. The return circuit works autonomously, but I can't promise our conversation will continue."

Asgar pressed the receiver harder to his ear.

"At all?"

The pause was longer than before.

"I don't know. There may be another window. There may not."

Behind him, the observatory ventilation ran quietly. The Caretaker added nothing.

"Then tell me now what I need to remember if you're not there."

Mirena began with the familiar rules: do not open the door without checking the environment; do not treat silence as permission; do not interfere with the body of the device if the local system does not understand what it is dealing with; if the cabin disappears, remain where it is safe and do not follow it through a dangerous environment.

Asgar listened to the end.

"I remember that."

"I know. So the last thing is different. If you have to make a decision later and there is no answer, don't try to guess what I would say. Check what is available to you. If there isn't enough data, you can wait. You can refuse."

He lowered his gaze to the black cord.

"And if there is never another answer?"

Mirena did not answer at once.

"Then it will still be your decision."

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the receiver.

"I wanted to say... I'm still angry about a lot of what happened. But not at you the way I was before. You answered when you knew. I remember that."

The static in the line grew louder. Then her voice returned.

"I remember that conversation, Asgar. And the one after it."

He closed his eyes.

"After the slope, at first I wasn't listening to what you were saying at all. Just the voice. And you asked whether I could speak."

"And you said: 'I can.'"

Asgar laughed briefly.

"I can now too."

Something dropped out at the other end, but he heard her short exhale.

"Next time, sit down before you're exhausted," Mirena said.

He looked through the open door at the empty passage.

"Thank you. For all the time you were with me."

The reply arrived with the beginning missing.

"...hear you, Asgar."

He waited.

"I don't know what else to say."

The pause stretched.

"You don't have to say anything else," Mirena said.

The line rustled. Asgar had already decided the connection was gone when her voice returned once more, clear and brief:

"Until we speak again, Asgar."

"Until we speak again, Mirena."

He said it immediately.

He did not wait for confirmation.

Asgar returned the receiver to the cradle and removed the clamp from the cabin. Set it by the wall.

"Here?" he asked.

"Here," the Caretaker answered.

Asgar checked whether anything else remained inside.

The bag, the stick, his own things.

He took hold of the edge of the door, then stopped.

"Caretaker."

"I'm here."

"Thank you."

"And you. You gave me my sector back."

Asgar looked down the empty passage.

"I don't know whether I'll come back."

"I know," the Caretaker said. "I know how to wait."

Asgar stepped inside.

As he moved, the black band brushed the edge of his sleeve. He adjusted the fabric, looked at the closed wound, and took the door with his right hand.

The door closed with the heavy, familiar sound.
