Chapter 28. Manual Control

The transition was quieter than the others.

Beyond the glass stood a wall with even seams and a narrow line of light along the floor.

Nothing moved.

No one ran toward the booth.

Mirena began checking the air.

“Do we have a date?”

She gave him the year.

Five billion.

Asgar did not immediately understand that the number had ended.

“Repeat that.”

Mirena did.

“Five billion six hundred twelve million four hundred forty-one thousand ninety.”

Asgar repeated it in his head, separating the orders of magnitude the way he had learned in school.

There was nothing else to do with the number.

It did not translate into distance.

Age.

Any human life.

“Air and pressure are stable. You can open the door,” Mirena said.

Asgar opened it.

The air outside was cool and almost odorless.

He stepped across the threshold and looked along the empty passage.

“Anyone here?”

The reply came after a short pause.

A speaker clicked somewhere in the wall.

“Yes.

“Although technically you asked whether there was anyone, not whether there was a human.”

The male voice was calm and unexpectedly natural.

Not a service recording.

Not a chain of stitched words.

Asgar looked at the wall.

“You understand Russian?”

“Now I do.

“Very old Russian.

“I had to retrieve several archive layers.

“If I start sounding strange, correct me.

“It’s useful.”

Asgar was quiet for a moment.

“Who are you?”

“The Keeper.”

“That a name?”

“It has been for long enough.”

“I’m Asgar.”

“Remembered.”

“Where am I?”

“In a technical sector of the solar observatory.

“And before you ask: there are no people here.”

“None?”

“Not within the volume accessible to me.

“Not before you.”

Asgar looked again at the empty passage.

“And you’ve been here long?”

The Keeper did not answer immediately.

“Long enough that counting time by human lives stopped being useful.

“I was transferred piece by piece from one hardware substrate to another.

“The observatory was rebuilt around me.

“There has never been a day when I was completely shut down.

“Corrupted pieces of memory occasionally had to be reconstructed from old copies.

“I am older than everything you can see here.”

“And what have you been running on all this time?”

“The Sun.

“Collectors changed.

“Orbits changed.

“I changed with them.

“As long as the Sun gives energy, I can work.”

“Five billion years?”

“Most of them.

“I remember my earliest versions less clearly than the current one, but the chain was not broken.

“At some point, the word ‘old’ simply stopped being useful.”

It sounded almost like a smile.

Asgar looked back at the red booth.

“Then you know what that is.”

The Keeper paused.

“No.”

Asgar looked toward the speaker.

“You’ve lived for billions of years and you don’t know?”

“I do not remember everything humanity did.

“Those are different statements.

“My own observations survived well.

“Operational records.

“Languages.

“Some science and literature.

“I never possessed all of human history, and much of what I did possess was damaged.

“I never found the origin of your device.”

“Nothing?”

“Several old images of similar shape.

“A great many later retellings.

“I don’t build answers from that.”

Asgar looked at the booth for another second, then carried out his bag and leaned the walking stick beside the open door.

---

The Keeper offered a safe room and water.

“Far?”

“Past the turn.

“I’m fortunate with today’s guest.

“You can walk.”

At the biosphere node he had once left the telephone and crossed three hundred meters of dead ground.

He had returned eight days later.

“Can we carry the audio out?” he asked.

“Don’t touch anything inside the apparatus.

“Just audio.”

A low cart emerged from a side opening and stopped at his feet.

On it lay an observatory communication unit and a bracket with two soft cups.

Asgar mounted the receiver himself:

one cup over the earpiece.

one over the microphone.

No one entered the booth.

The cord remained loose.

He tested the connection beside the unit, then again from the passage.

“I hear you,” Mirena said.

“I may be away from the booth for a while.

“The receiver stays off the hook.”

“As long as it remains off, the channel stays open on my side.

“I’m here.”

“And me?” the Keeper asked through the passage speaker.

After a short delay, Mirena answered.

“You too.”

Asgar looked toward the speaker.

“Excellent,” said the Keeper.

“At least now you don’t have to paraphrase us to each other.”

He went back for the bag and walking stick.

The room beyond the turn had somewhere to sit.

Asgar left his things by the wall and took water.

Somewhere deep in the observatory came a dull impact.

The light on the wall went out.

Then returned as a narrow strip along the floor.

Asgar put down the water before he had taken more than a mouthful.

“What was that?”

“Backup line,” the Keeper said.

“Don’t worry.

“I’m not falling apart yet.”

Asgar raised his head.

“Yet?”

“That was an attempt at reassurance.

“Apparently not a successful one.

“I’ve just shut down everything nonessential in this sector.”

Asgar stood.

---

What failed was the system keeping the instruments oriented toward the Sun.

Of two drives, one had failed earlier.

The second had just been left working alone.

In three hours the observatory would need a maneuver it could not handle by itself.

“What happens then?”

“I seal this sector and leave you air in the reserve compartment.”

“And the booth?”

“Stays here.

“Until I repair the drive, you won’t get back to it.”

The Keeper’s machines worked outside.

The motor itself was accessible from inside through a narrow passage.

None of the machines could fit.

The outside route existed but would take longer than the time remaining.

“So there’s no one who can do it.”

“Normally I’d say no one with suitable geometry.

“Today the simpler version is: I don’t have hands your size.”

Asgar did not immediately know what to say.

Since Bertram had put the rotating tool into his hand, no one had needed Asgar’s hands.

In Ar, he had carried boxes, held the second side of a drive, repaired a latch Bertram could have repaired without him.

After that, everywhere he went, people received him.

Fed him.

Treated him.

Explained.

No one asked him for anything.

Here they were not asking because he was skilled.

They were asking because there was simply no one else.

“Can I reach it from inside?”

A service route appeared on the wall.

“Yes.

“The passage was built for a human.

“There’s no breathable air there.

“You’ll need protection.”

“What do I have to do?”

The Keeper enlarged a diagram of the motor.

“This pin must leave the housing and seat in that slot.

“It stopped short.”

On the diagram, a narrow gap remained between the end of the pin and the slot.

“You could fit a match through that gap.

“I shut the motor down so it wouldn’t strike the obstruction again and break the pin completely.”

“And what do I do?”

“Release the clamp.

“Move the pin into the slot.

“Restore the clamp.

“I’ll guide you.”

On the diagram it looked simple.

Asgar had already been wrong often enough about things that looked simple on diagrams.

“If I fail, can I get back out?”

The Keeper showed him the route back and the final safe return point.

“After this mark, drop everything.

“Even if you think you have one movement left.”

Asgar picked up the communication unit.

“Mirena.”

“I hear you.”

“Keeper, explain.”

The Keeper described the failure, the remaining time, and where Asgar would have to go.

Mirena asked what he would breathe there and whether he could return if he started and could not finish.

“What if I just go back into the booth?” Asgar asked.

“I can try to take you before the normal window,” Mirena said.

“But today that is more dangerous than usual.

“This place is still moving.

“I don’t know what happens if the sector begins reconfiguring while the door is preparing.”

Asgar compared the two options.

The repair had a passage.

A pin.

A slot.

And a time after which he had to stop and leave.

The early transition had nothing Mirena could put into words.

“I can go to the reserve compartment and wait,” Asgar said.

“But then the booth stays behind the sealed section.

“Or I can go push this pin into place by hand.”

“You understand exactly where to stop if time runs out?”

Asgar went through the return sequence one more time.

“Now I do.”

“Good.

“That matters more than knowing how to finish.”

“I’ll try it manually.”

---

The equipment waited in a niche past the turn.

Asgar inspected it, waited while the Keeper checked fit and air reserve, and put it on with his guidance.

He left his own belongings beside the seat.

The communication unit clipped to the suit.

The Keeper promised Mirena’s voice would reach him all the way to the motor.

Asgar checked the line.

“I’m here,” Mirena said.

“I’m going.”

The passage was lower than he expected.

He had to crouch, then descend a short flight of steps.

At the last one he paused and tested the footing with his left leg.

The gloves dulled the feel of the handrail.

The Keeper marked the route with light.

At one point Asgar missed a turn and reached a sealed panel.

“Back,” the Keeper said immediately.

“Previous branch.

“I’ll light the left wall.”

Asgar returned.

The mechanism sat in a cramped recess.

There was room for his knees and one good handhold.

He settled in, studied the parts, and asked the Keeper to repeat the labels.

The diagram had been clean and separated.

Here a housing covered part of the assembly.

Several surfaces reflected light in the same way.

The slot was beneath the pin and visible only from one angle.

Asgar found it by moving his head.

The gap matched the diagram.

A match could have passed through.

The pin could not.

“I see it.”

“Motors are isolated,” the Keeper said.

Asgar did not move until he found the mechanical flag inside the recess confirming the same thing.

Then he removed the lever from its mount.

The clamp moved easily and stopped sooner than he expected.

“Almost there.”

“Do not trust the word ‘almost.’

“Lower indicator.”

Asgar leaned down.

The clamp was still holding.

He reset the lever and began again until the pin was truly free.

He kept the lever with his left hand and guided a second tool toward the pin with his right.

At that moment, the pin shifted on its own.

Asgar pulled his hand back too late.

Pain crossed his palm in a short hot line.

“Stop.”

He froze.

The Keeper confirmed both motors remained isolated.

The glove was intact on the outside.

Asgar raised the hand toward his face.

“I think I cut myself.”

“Seal integrity is intact,” the Keeper said.

“But yes, you appear to have injured the hand.”

The palm burned.

When the pin shifted, its edge had trapped the glove against his skin.

The external layer held.

A rigid inner reinforcement had sliced across the palm.

Asgar flexed his fingers carefully.

They moved.

Mirena asked:

“Can you get the hand away from the mechanism?”

“Already did.”

“Tell the Keeper whether there’s numbness.”

He did.

No numbness.

No severe pain.

Only a wet sensation inside the glove that made him want to remove it immediately.

“Come back,” the Keeper said.

Asgar looked at the mechanism.

“Is that an order?”

“Advice from a very old being that has seen too many repairs end with the phrase ‘one more movement.’”

Asgar looked again.

The only remaining step was to move the pin into the slot.

There was a brace for the tool.

It could be done one-handed.

Asgar held the left hand against his chest and completed the movement with the right.

The indicator changed.

“Seated?”

“Yes.”

Only after confirmation did he permit startup.

The mechanism began moving.

Asgar pulled back and held the injured hand against his chest.

“There,” said the Keeper.

“The backup drive is back.”

Asgar exhaled.

“Thank you.”

“I believe that is my line.

“I would have disliked losing another sector.

“I get attached to them, even though most are younger than I am.”

Asgar wanted to sit down right there.

Instead he returned the tool to its mount and crawled toward the exit.

---

In the medical niche, the first thing Asgar asked was for the glove to be removed.

The Keeper made him wait while the medical unit completed checks of the environment and the suit.

Asgar stared at the enclosed left hand and tried not to flex his fingers.

When the glove came off, the cut was small.

There was less blood than he had imagined.

The medical unit examined the palm.

The Keeper asked him to move his fingers several times, describe sensation, and not hurry.

“No deep injury,” the Keeper said.

“In 2014, would you have called that ‘lucky’?”

“We still do.”

“Good.

“Some words survive longer than civilizations.”

Asgar leaned his head back.

“Treat it.”

Materials arrived from a sealed compartment.

He watched the instrument clean the wound and apply a thin covering.

The unfamiliar compound had a smell.

For a moment it stung more sharply.

Then the pain eased.

For monitoring, the Keeper offered a narrow black band.

Asgar looked at it.

“What does it do?”

“Tracks temperature, subdermal blood flow, and swelling.

“If the condition worsens, it alerts you.”

“Does it treat anything?”

“No.”

Asgar asked whether he could remove it himself and whether it depended on the observatory.

He could remove it at any time.

It operated independently.

He held out his left arm.

The black band settled just above the wrist and did not restrict movement.

Asgar put his own clothes back on.

He favored the left palm.

At first he tried closing fastenings one-handed.

Then allowed himself to use his fingers.

“Are you back?” Mirena asked.

He had forgotten the local communication unit was beside him.

“Yes.

“They treated it.

“Small cut.”

“Good.”

Asgar looked at the black strip.

“I thought it was worse.”

“Keep watching it,” Mirena said.

---

The observatory completed its turn while he rested.

The Keeper said the repaired drive was holding.

External machines continued their work.

“That’s everything?”

“No.

“But the worst part is no longer urgent.”

“What if the backup motor hadn’t been restored?”

“I would have lost part of the instrumentation and sealed this sector until external machines repaired it.”

“For how long?”

The Keeper gave him a duration.

Asgar began converting it into his own days and stopped halfway through.

“That’s long for me.”

“I understand,” the Keeper said.

“We have very different habits around the word ‘long.’”

Asgar took water and asked what the Keeper observed.

“The Sun.

“What remains of the planetary system.

“And anything between them that can still be measured.”

“Earth still exists?”

The Keeper paused a little longer than usual.

“Yes.

“I still watch it.”

Asgar asked to see.

The wall opposite the seat showed a planet beside an enormous luminous edge.

Even with the image processed, Asgar wanted to turn his eyes away.

No familiar blue remained.

Across the lit surface lay dull regions, long dark bands, bright patches.

“Is that now?”

“Nearly.

“Light does not reach my instruments instantaneously.

“And I reduced the brightness.

“Otherwise you wouldn’t see anything.”

“And the Sun?”

“Red giant.

“It has changed more than I have, although we could argue about that for a long time.”

At school, this had belonged somewhere beyond the end of everything that had anything to do with people.

A diagram on a page:

star expands.

changes color.

Asgar remembered the picture better than the explanation.

The Keeper observed it as an operating condition of his observatory.

“Anyone alive on Earth?”

“No.”

Asgar turned toward the speaker.

The Keeper continued more quietly.

“The biosphere has been gone a very long time.

“You cannot go onto the surface without protection.”

Asgar asked for the labels and frames to be removed.

Only the sphere remained.

He watched it for a long time with his left hand resting palm-up.

With Olmer, at least he had been able to walk on ground.

Smell leaves.

Put his hands into a river.

At the next stop, he had lived almost two years among living things.

Even alone, the world moved.

Grew.

Fed him.

Could kill him.

At the previous stop, the surface had already required protection.

Now the Keeper had simply said the biosphere no longer existed.

Asgar tried to imagine rain there.

He knew that rain.

A few heavy drops striking the metal awning.

Then the asphalt beneath the windows darkening.

The smell rising from stone heated all day.

Katya stepping onto the balcony to bring in laundry and shouting over the noise that he should close the nursery window.

Rain knocking against the sill all night.

Puddles in the courtyard the next morning that the younger boy would step into because walking around them would be boring.

Nothing in the image helped.

“Zoom?” the Keeper asked.

Asgar shook his head.

“No.”

“All right.”

He remembered the stadium call.

Katya asking him to buy a large bottle of water and tell her when to expect him because of the cake.

He had said he would make it.

Back then, ten minutes had been an ordinary delay that could be fixed on the way home.

Asgar lowered his eyes.

The planet remained on the wall.

Every place he had tried to find belonged to it.

But none of the people he wanted to go home to were anywhere on that image.

He did not ask for the old map.

After a while he said:

“Turn it off.”

The image vanished.

“Asgar.”

For the first time, the Keeper used his name.

“What?”

“Nothing.

“I only wanted to make sure I remembered it correctly.”

Asgar did not answer.

---

Several hours passed before he left.

Asgar ate.

Checked the palm.

Packed.

He lifted the bag with his right hand.

Took the walking stick in the same hand and discovered it was awkward to carry both.

He had to make two trips to the booth.

The audio unit stood beside the open door.

Asgar removed the receiver from its bracket and held it to his ear.

“I’m at the apparatus.”

The answer took longer than usual.

“I hear you,” Mirena said.

He reported the new band, the treated wound, and the things he was taking.

Local equipment had already been returned to the service niche.

Halfway through her reply, the sound dropped out.

Asgar waited.

“Repeat the last part.”

Mirena did.

This time a different word vanished.

Asgar looked at the audio unit.

“Keeper, is that you?”

“My section is clean.

“I can make it louder.

“That will not restore a missing word.”

Mirena heard him.

“He’s right.

“The through-channel is becoming unstable.

“The return circuit is autonomous, but I can’t promise the conversation will remain possible.”

Asgar pressed the receiver harder to his ear.

“Completely?”

The pause was longer.

“I don’t know.

“There may be another window.

“There may not.”

Behind him, the observatory ventilation worked quietly.

The Keeper said nothing.

“Then tell me now what I need to remember if you’re gone.”

Mirena began with the familiar things:

do not open the door without checking the environment.

do not interpret silence as permission.

do not interfere with the shell if the local system does not understand the object.

if the booth disappears, remain where it is safe and do not pursue it through a hostile environment.

Asgar listened to the end.

“I remember all of that.”

“I know.

“So the last one is different.

“If you need to decide something later and there is no answer, do not try to guess what I would have said.

“Check what is available to you.

“If there isn’t enough information, you can wait.

“You can refuse.”

Asgar looked down at the black cord.

“And if there’s never another answer?”

Mirena did not reply immediately.

“Then it will still be your decision.”

Asgar ran a thumb along the edge of the receiver.

“I wanted to say…

“I’m still angry about a lot of what happened.

“But not at you the way I was before.

“You answered when you knew.

“I remember that.”

The line noise grew louder.

Then her voice returned.

“I remember that conversation too, Asgar.

“And the one after it.”

He closed his eyes.

“After the slope…

“At first I wasn’t listening to what you were saying at all.

“Just the voice.

“And you were asking whether I could stand.”

“You weren’t answering the question.”

Asgar laughed briefly.

“I’m standing now.”

Something disappeared at the other end, but he heard her short exhale.

“Next time, sit down before you get tired,” Mirena said.

He looked through the open door at the empty passage.

“Thank you.

“For all the time you were with me.”

The beginning of her reply dropped out.

“…hear you, Asgar.”

He waited.

“I don’t know what else to say.”

The pause stretched.

“You don’t have to say anything else,” Mirena said.

The line hissed.

Asgar thought it had ended.

Then her voice returned once more.

Clear.

Brief.

“Until we speak again, Asgar.”

“Until we speak again, Mirena.”

He answered immediately.

No confirmation came back.

Asgar removed the receiver from the cups, returned it to the cradle, and carried the bracket out of the booth.

He set it by the wall.

“Here?” he asked.

“Here,” the Keeper said.

Asgar checked that nothing else remained inside.

Bag.

Walking stick.

His own things.

He took the edge of the door.

Then stopped.

“Keeper.”

“I’m here.”

“Thank you.”

“And thank you.

“You gave me my sector back.”

Asgar looked into the empty passage.

“I don’t know whether I’ll return.”

“I know,” the Keeper said.

“I know how to wait.”

Asgar stepped inside.

The black band caught the edge of his sleeve.

He adjusted the fabric, looked at the sealed cut, and took the door with his right hand.

The panel closed with the familiar heavy sound.
