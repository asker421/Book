Chapter 8. On Both Sides of the Glass

The employee raised the radio to his face.

"Stop the descent. No one enters the hall for now."

Asgar backed toward the ramp, looking at the far wall. The red was already gone. The yellow stripe lay straight, the concrete behind it remained gray, but he kept looking there until his heel caught the leg of a chair.

The caseworker took the screen off the table.

"Asgar, over here."

To the right of the ramp was a door into the control room. The employee opened it, let the woman through, then held it for Asgar. He entered last.

The room smelled of plastic and long-cold coffee. A table with several monitors stood along the transparent partition; one had not yet been switched on. From here almost the entire hall could be seen except for the area right beside the ramp. Asgar went to the glass.

"It can appear again," he said.

The employee stopped by the door.

"You said the tripod had a leg cut off. Where was it standing?"

"Where it appeared afterward. I can't give you the exact spot."

The employee looked into the hall, toward the yellow stripe by the far wall.

"Then I can't tell you exactly where it'll appear here either. So far the changes have only happened in the hall. We'll keep it empty. You stay here; we'll move everyone else into the corridor."

"Has Katya arrived?"

"I'll find out."

He went out, leaving the door open. The caseworker set the screen on the table and moved to the opposite wall. Several times Asgar caught her looking at him; each time she shifted her gaze into the hall.

He took out his mobile. Pressed the button, already knowing nothing would happen, and set the phone beside the inactive monitor.

A few minutes later, footsteps sounded beyond the door. Asgar turned.

Katya stopped in the doorway.

On the screen he had seen only her face and shoulders. Now she stood there in full, a bag hooked over one bent arm, wearing a pale blouse he had never seen. She looked at him, and Asgar could not understand why he was not walking toward her. There were only a few steps between them.

Katya set the bag on the floor. He managed to say:

"Wait, in here—"

She came over and took his face in her hands. Her fingers were cool. Katya turned his head slightly, ran her thumb over his cheek. Asgar stood still while she examined him. Then her hands slipped to his shoulders and she pressed herself against him.

He hugged her too hard. Katya let out a short breath against his neck but did not pull away. Asgar loosened his arms, felt the fabric of her blouse beneath his palm, the fastening, the movement of her back. Her hair smelled different. It made him want to pull her even closer until everything became familiar again.

"You're warm," she said.

He tried to answer and only nodded. Katya held him tighter.

The employee quietly said something by the door. The caseworker went out with him. Asgar heard them stop in the corridor. The door remained open.

At last Katya stepped back just far enough to look at him.

"Give me your hand."

He held out his right one. She took it in both hands and smoothed his fingers straight.

"All the way here I kept thinking I'd arrive and it would turn out to be some mistake."

"Me too."

"No. At least you knew you were here."

Asgar lowered his eyes to her hands. Fine veins stood out on one of them. He remembered that palm without them. Katya noticed where he was looking.

"You'll get used to it."

"Katya..."

"I didn't get used to it right away either."

She did not let go of his hand.

Asgar wanted to kiss her. He leaned closer, paused halfway. Katya moved in herself and kissed him on the lips. Briefly, carefully. Then she rested her forehead against his chin.

"Please don't look at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you're trying to remember."

He hugged her again.

---

The first red edges did not reappear. The duty group came down into the hall, set up equipment by the entrance, ran a cable along the wall. Asgar and Katya remained in the control room until the employee asked him to go out into the hall for observation.

Katya stood up with him immediately.

"Stay here," the employee said. "So far the changes have only been in the hall, and I don't want to test the distance on you."

Asgar took his mobile off the table.

"I'll be right opposite."

He went out, closed the door behind him, and approached the partition from the other side. Katya stood by the glass. Her lips moved, but he could not hear the words.

Asgar pointed at his ear. The employee leaned over the table in the control room. The speaker by the door clicked.

"Can you hear now?" Katya asked.

"Yes."

She looked at the small microphone in front of her, then back at him.

"Sit down at least. You're standing all the time."

One of the chairs remained by the partition. Asgar moved it and sat. Katya settled opposite him behind the glass, her bag on her knees.

For a while they talked like that.

She said she had contacted the sons. The younger one wanted to come immediately, but Katya asked him to wait for her call.

"And the older one?"

"He's out of town. He's already on the road." Katya looked at her device. "He'll be here in the morning."

Asgar nodded.

Morning was only a few hours away—in a person who was now driving toward the city along a darkening road.

"Show me to them," Asgar said.

"I will. I don't want them to see you for one second, then something happens here and the connection cuts off. I barely made it here myself."

He looked into the depth of the hall.

"All right."

Katya opened her bag, found a tissue, and wiped beneath her eyes. Then took out water.

"Have you eaten today?"

Asgar remembered the cold coffee in the yard.

"Not really."

"I see."

She called the employee over. After a while, food was brought to him in paper packaging. Asgar ate while sitting by the glass and listened to Katya tell him about the drive there. The navigation system had brought her to a closed gate. She drove around the building, ended up at the same gate again, called the caseworker, and only then found the entrance.

"I tell them: everything's closed here. They say: no, it's open. Well, I can see the gate," she said. "Turned out we were talking about different sides of the building."

Asgar smiled with his mouth full. Katya smiled too and immediately turned away, screwing the cap back onto the bottle.

He finished eating. Folded the paper so the crumbs stayed inside.

Upstairs, it was gradually getting dark; he could see it on one of the monitors showing the camera by the entrance. Down here, the light did not change. Asgar stood several times, walked along the partition, then returned. Katya watched him. When he sat down, she continued the conversation from where she had stopped.

The whole evening could pass like this.

The caseworker came over to the table in the control room and showed the employee her screen. Through the open intercom, Asgar heard the word "journalists" and looked up.

"What's going on?"

The woman turned the screen toward the glass. In the image, Asgar stood in the courtyard of the center with a police officer beside him. Then she opened the old missing-person notice with his photograph.

"A newsroom sent this. They're asking whether this is you. They found the old notice after footage from outside the center was posted. I asked them to route inquiries through the unit's reception office."

Katya moved closer to the screen.

"Don't give anyone the boys' numbers. I'll call them and warn them."

"We haven't," the caseworker said and stepped away to answer a call.

Asgar looked at the image until the screen went dark. Katya called his name; he sat opposite her again.

He started asking about her apartment. Which floor. Whether there was a balcony. Katya said the balcony was small, but at least the morning sun did not hit it. Asgar pictured a chair, a mug, her bare feet on the cool floor.

"And where would I..." he began, then stopped.

Katya looked up.

"We'll figure it out at home."

---

Her device made a sound while she was putting the bottle back into the bag.

Katya looked at the screen and did not pick it up immediately.

"It's the younger one."

Asgar said nothing. After what she had explained, he could not ask.

She turned partly away and answered. Through the partition Asgar could hear only her: that she was here, that it was all true, that no, he should not come today. Then she fell silent and listened for a long time.

"Wait," she said into the device. Then, without turning her head: "Voice only. He won't look."

She set the device on the table closer to the partition.

At first there was silence.

Asgar moved closer. He prepared to hear the boy who breathed into the microphone and asked whether there was a crane at the depot. The voice was adult, even, a little lower than Katya's, and completely unfamiliar.

"Dad?"

Asgar swallowed.

"Yes."

The other side went quiet again.

"I don't know what to say."

"Neither do I."

Asgar looked at Katya. She sat with her eyes lowered to the table.

"I watched that stadium recording so many times," his son said. "There's no sound. What could you hear there?"

"The stands were roaring so loud I couldn't hear your mother," Asgar said. "That's why I went into that corridor. So I could hear her."

His son did not answer.

Asgar waited. He wanted to ask everything at once: what he did for work, where he lived, whether he had children, whether he remembered his tenth birthday. The moment he imagined saying each question aloud, it became a stranger's question.

"I spent twenty years trying to understand why you went in there," his son said at last. "It wasn't written anywhere."

"Now it is."

His son exhaled briefly—not laughter, but close.

Katya raised a hand toward the device and stopped without touching it.

"I have to go," he said.

"Yes."

The pause was short and noticeable.

"Goodbye," his son said.

And, almost as the connection ended:

"Dad."

Katya took the device and put it in her bag without looking at the screen.

Asgar sat with his palms on his knees.

Nothing had changed beyond the partition. Someone in the control room moved a chair.

---

The booth appeared while Asgar was carrying the empty food package toward the door.

First, the reflection of a column broke on the smooth metal panel near the far wall. The vertical line shifted as though someone had moved the lower half of the image sideways. Asgar stopped.

The red body appeared about thirty meters from him.

The lower section went into the concrete. A beam crossed above the door; from where Asgar stood, it looked as though the door would not have enough space to open. For several seconds, the edge of the roof doubled. The doubling ran along the beam in both directions and ended about ten meters from the body; beyond that, the metal looked normal. Then the nearer section became still too.

Something clicked at the base.

A small gray fragment rolled across the concrete. It stopped by the yellow stripe.

Asgar set the packaging on the nearest chair.

Behind the glass, the employee was speaking quickly into the radio. Katya stood up. He saw her approach the partition but kept looking at the fragment.

A ring came from the booth.

"Asgar, stay where you are," came through the speaker.

He nodded. The ringing repeated.

The employee turned one of the monitors toward a colleague. They looked between the image and the hall. Asgar could not make out their conversation: the intercom was silent.

At last the speaker came on.

"Everything is stable right now. But I wouldn't approach yet."

"I'll pick up the receiver. Like before, from outside," Asgar said, walking toward the glass.

Katya looked at him.

"Then you'll come back here?"

"Yes."

She held her gaze on the booth.

"And tell me everything they say."

Asgar nodded and walked across the hall.

The closer he got, the stronger the urge to turn back. A fresh pale chip was visible in the concrete floor beside the body. The red wall crossed its edge. Asgar went around the spot on the right and stopped in front of the door.

The handle was warm.

He pulled the door toward himself. It passed beneath the beam even though a second earlier it had looked as though it should hit it. Asgar looked up, then at his own hand, and forced himself to focus on the telephone.

He lifted the receiver and remained in front of the threshold.

"I came out in two thousand thirty-nine."

"Asgar, I can hear you."

The same voice. Asgar tightened his grip on the receiver.

"Twenty-five years. Do you understand?"

"Yes. Minutes passed for you. Here—twenty-five years."

Asgar adjusted the receiver against his ear. Across the hall he could see Katya—a small figure behind the transparent partition.

"I found my wife," he said. "She's here. I want to stay."

The woman did not answer immediately.

"You can stay. I won't force you to enter."

"And what happens with this? With the appearances?"

"The connection will remain. The cabin may appear again. Where and when—I don't know."

"Concrete chipped off here. Right by the body."

"Don't approach the boundary with the floor."

Asgar moved one foot slightly farther away.

"I have nowhere to get away from you. I can't spend my whole life living on an empty site."

"We didn't close the connection without you entering."

"And where will you close it?"

He waited for the familiar "I don't know." The woman said:

"To work with an incomplete transition, we need the source."

Asgar frowned.

"What source?"

"The point the module was sent from and where it can be received."

"The module is this cabin?"

"Yes."

He looked at the telephone, at the round metal dial beneath the set.

"So the place where it was built?"

"Not necessarily. It was sent from there, and it can be received back there."

"Are there people there who can send me home?"

"I don't know whether they'll send you home."

"But I can ask them?"

"If we reach the source, at least they'll be able to handle your return separately. I don't have a solution right now."

Asgar was silent. His fingers on the door handle were beginning to go numb.

"I need the year two thousand fourteen," he said. "Not some other good year. Mine."

"That date can't be selected."

"But you can select the source?"

"No. We don't select the next stop at all. I don't know when the source will come up."

Behind the glass, Katya shifted position. Asgar could not make out what she was doing and craned his neck as though that might help.

"If I stay, will you keep looking for a way?"

"Yes. But without continuing the transition today, the connection has not closed."

He stood listening to the faint noise on the line.

Then he said he was going to step away to talk to his wife, returned the receiver to the cradle, and carefully let go of the door.

---

On the way back to the partition, Asgar was already choosing his words.

He wanted to say that at least there was a possibility there. That here they would only wait for the next appearance. That they could not leave things as they were after what had happened to the tripod leg and the concrete.

Katya was standing by the glass waiting for him.

"What did she say?"

He told her. About the source, about the point of origin, about the impossibility of choosing a year. Katya listened without interrupting. The employee of the building stood beside her.

"What if you stay?" she asked.

"They'll keep working. But they can't close the connection yet."

Katya turned to the employee.

"What can you do?"

He moved the microphone closer so Asgar could hear too.

"Keep him here. Close the hall, provide separate housing, arrange meetings with family. If anything changes, we'll adjust the measures. I can't say everything will stop."

"How long here?" Asgar asked.

"You're here today. Tomorrow we'll assess the situation and decide together."

"And his documents?" Katya looked at the caseworker standing behind them.

"We'll sort those out. His identity has already been confirmed."

Asgar listened and looked at Katya's bag beside the table.

She could bring him clothes tomorrow. The day after tomorrow, the sons could come. He would watch them enter this room, hear their adult voices. He could wake in the morning and learn that nothing had happened overnight.

"I can come tomorrow," Katya said. "And in the evening. As often as necessary. You don't have to come home today."

"Katya, I haven't decided anything yet."

She looked at him more closely.

"Then why are you answering as though I'm asking you to do something impossible?"

"I'm only explaining that this won't end by itself."

"I heard that. And the people beside me heard it. They're telling us what we can do next."

Asgar looked back at the booth. Katya fell silent. When he turned toward her again, she was standing closer to the glass.

"You already want to go."

"I want this to end."

"So do I. Answer what I said."

He ran a hand over the back of his head.

"If there's a chance to reach the place where they can deal with it..."

"Then you want to."

Asgar looked at the employee beside her. The man stepped back toward the table but did not leave the room.

"I'm still here," Asgar said. "We're talking."

"You're listing the reasons why you have to leave. And when I suggest staying at least for the night, you say you haven't decided anything."

He felt heat rise into his face.

"Because we have no proof it'll be safe here tomorrow."

"And you have no proof they'll send you back there."

"The concrete already chipped here."

"I saw it."

"If that happens beside you—"

Katya stepped away from the glass. Asgar stopped. She ran a hand over her mouth and waited until she could speak.

"Don't do this again, where I have to be the one who sends you. I can't say there's no danger. But I can't say 'go' either. I want you to stay. I just hugged you."

He looked at her.

"I know."

"No. For you, we were talking this morning. And I..."

She did not finish. She took the bottle from the table, put it back without opening it.

Asgar lowered his head. In the reflection near the bottom edge of the partition he could see his shoes and the chair legs. He could sit. Ask for more food. Say he would not go anywhere until morning. For several seconds, there was only the chair, food, Katya behind the glass, and a night without another ringing phone.

Then he imagined the morning when the telephone would ring again and himself explaining to Katya again why he needed to approach.

"I was already leaning toward going," he said. "When I came back from the phone."

Katya nodded once.

"That's what you should have said."

"I was afraid."

"I'm afraid too, Asgar. But I'm not telling you I don't care whether you stay or not."

Asgar looked up at her.

"I do care."

"I know," Katya said.

---

They were silent for a long time.

The employees in the control room spoke quietly among themselves; the intercom carried only indistinct background noise. Katya sat with her hands on the bag. Asgar stood by the glass. He wanted to ask her to come closer, but he was not sure he had the right to ask her for anything now.

At last she stood on her own.

"What will you take?"

He patted his pocket.

"Phone. Documents."

"The phone is dead."

"I know."

Katya opened her mouth, but instead of answering, nodded.

"There are photographs on it."

"Yes."

Asgar took out the mobile. Her face reflected in the black glass on the other side of the partition.

"I won't lose them."

Katya closed her eyes. He put the phone away.

"That wasn't what you should have said."

She did not answer. Asgar placed his palm against the partition. Katya looked at it, then raised hers. The thickness of the glass lay between their fingers. He could not feel her warmth.

"I love you," he said.

Her mouth trembled.

"I love you too. That's why I'm asking you to stay."

Asgar remained there without lowering his hand. Katya dropped hers first.

The employee at the table waited without suggesting anything. Behind him was the door to the corridor, beyond that the exit from the building, the city, Katya's car. Asgar looked that way, then back at her.

"I'm going."

Katya tightened her grip on the strap of the bag. Asgar waited, but she did not answer. Then he turned toward the hall.

---

At the booth, Asgar stopped and looked back.

Katya remained by the partition. He could barely make out her face now. Only the pale blouse, the dark line of hair, the hand holding the edge of the table.

He opened the door and lifted the receiver.

"I'm here."

"I hear you."

Asgar wanted to ask how long the next transition would last. Instead, he looked at the threshold.

"There are people outside. They're staying."

"Understood. Step fully inside before closing the door."

He crossed the red edge, brought the second foot in. Turned inside, shifted the receiver so the cord would not lie in the doorway. With his free hand, he took hold of the inner handle.

Katya was visible through the open gap. Asgar paused. Behind the partition she raised her head. He did not know whether she could see his face or only the figure in the doorway.

He wanted to go back if only to say it differently. Without explanations, without trying to get her agreement.

He pulled the door. It moved heavily. Asgar kept his gaze on the pale blouse through two layers of glass until the door settled into the frame.

"Closed," he said.

He released the inner handle.

Something clicked in the receiver.

Katya remained in place.

Then the line of the partition shifted. The edge of the table stretched and passed through her figure; Asgar jerked forward and struck his palm against the booth glass.

The hall disappeared.

He stood with his hand pressed to the cold surface, looking at daylight outside.

---

Katya gripped the edge of the table. Where the red door had just been, only concrete remained. The employee beside her called someone on the radio and reached for the intercom; the light by the microphone went out.

She was still looking into the depth of the hall when a straight line on the floor bent.

At first Katya thought the glass had trembled. Then the yellow stripe on the concrete shifted relative to the wall, and the section of hall behind it seemed to become shorter. The table by the far wall stretched sideways and immediately snapped back. The employee beside her fell silent.

In the empty place where there had been no one a second before, a human figure began to appear.

He did not step out of a booth—there was no booth there anymore. The man appeared directly in the hall, as if several mismatched pieces of space had finally fitted together around him. He stood sideways, one arm extended as though he had just tried to brace himself against a wall. Then he spun around.

Katya recognized the face before she understood anything else.

The cheeks had hollowed. The hair was longer. The clothes were different.

"Asgar?"

He saw her.

Katya knew it from the way his face changed instantly. Asgar shouted something and ran toward the control room. She turned to the door and pressed the device by the opening.

"Open it. He's back."

The employee was already at the table. The door began to slide aside.

Asgar nearly reached the pale line on the floor. His right foot crossed it, and he swayed as though a step had not been where he expected it. He stopped and looked down. Katya pressed her palm to the glass.

He raised his hand toward hers.

His mouth moved again. The speaker was silent.

Katya pulled the door toward herself. Asgar stepped along the pale line, and the floor between them stretched. She did not see it at first: he was moving toward her, but the distance was not getting shorter. Then it grew.

"Here, Asgar. I'm opening it, wait."

He stopped and reached out. Katya held the edge of the door and stretched her free hand toward him even though there were still several meters between them.

Asgar moved again.

The pale line split in two. A different pale floor showed through the concrete near his feet, then the edge of a table. Asgar turned sideways, trying to go around it.

The table shifted with him.

Katya struck the glass with her palm.

Asgar lunged toward her and fell forward with his arm outstretched. For an instant his figure stretched with the changing hall, then a white wall from another room showed through his shoulder.

"Asgar!"

He was still looking at her.

Then he disappeared.

The floor became flat again. The yellow stripe returned to its place. Beyond the partition lay the same empty technical hall, and Katya kept her arm stretched toward the opening for several more seconds.
