Chapter 8. On Both Sides of the Glass

The employee raised the radio to his face.

“Stop anyone coming down. No one enters the hall for now.”

Asgar backed toward the ramp, watching the far wall. There was no red anymore. The yellow stripe lay straight, the concrete beyond it remained gray, but he kept staring until his heel struck a chair leg.

The caseworker took the display off the table.

“Asgar. In here.”

There was a door to the control room on the right side of the ramp. The employee opened it, let the woman through, then held it for Asgar. He entered last.

The room smelled of plastic and long-cold coffee. A desk with several monitors ran along the transparent partition; one screen had not been switched on yet. From here they could see almost the entire hall except the area immediately beside the ramp.

Asgar went to the glass.

“It can appear again,” he said.

The employee stopped by the door.

“You said a tripod leg was severed. Where was the tripod standing?”

“Where the booth appeared afterward. I can’t be more precise than that.”

The employee looked into the hall, toward the yellow stripe by the far wall.

“Then I can’t tell you where it will appear in here either. So far the changes have only been in the hall. We’ll keep it empty. You stay here and we’ll move everyone else into the corridor.”

“Has Katya arrived?”

“I’ll find out.”

He left the door open as he went out. The caseworker set the display on the desk and moved to the opposite wall. Several times Asgar caught her looking at him; each time she shifted her gaze back into the hall.

He took out his mobile. Pressed the button, already knowing nothing would happen, and set the phone beside the dark monitor.

A few minutes later footsteps sounded beyond the door.

Asgar turned.

Katya stopped on the threshold.

On the video he had seen only her face and shoulders. Now she stood there in full, a bag hooked over one bent arm, wearing a pale blouse he had never seen. She looked at him, and Asgar could not understand why he was not moving toward her.

There were only a few steps between them.

Katya put the bag on the floor.

He managed to say:

“Wait, in here—”

She crossed the room and took his face in both hands.

Her fingers were cool. Katya turned his head slightly to one side and ran a thumb over his cheek. Asgar stood motionless while she examined him.

Then her hands slid to his shoulders and she pressed herself against him.

He held her too tightly.

Katya breathed out sharply against his neck but did not pull away. Asgar loosened his arms, felt the fabric of her blouse beneath his palm, a fastening, the movement of her back.

Her hair smelled different.

It made him want to pull her closer until everything became familiar again.

“You’re warm,” she said.

He tried to answer and only nodded.

Katya held him tighter.

The employee said something quietly from the doorway. The caseworker went out with him. Asgar heard them stop in the corridor. The door remained open.

At last Katya moved back just far enough to look at him.

“Give me your hand.”

He held out his right hand. She took it in both of hers and smoothed his fingers open.

“All the way here I kept thinking I’d arrive and it would turn out to be some kind of mistake.”

“Me too.”

“No. At least you knew you were here.”

Asgar looked down at her hands. Fine veins showed beneath the skin of one. He remembered that hand without them.

Katya saw where he was looking.

“You’ll get used to it.”

“Kat…”

“I didn’t get used to it immediately either.”

She did not let go of his hand.

Asgar wanted to kiss her. He leaned forward, hesitated halfway.

Katya moved in herself and kissed him on the lips.

Briefly. Carefully.

Then she rested her forehead against his chin.

“Please don’t look at me like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you’re trying to remember me.”

He held her again.

---

The red edges did not reappear.

The duty team came down into the hall, placed equipment near the entrance, and ran a cable along the wall. Asgar and Katya remained in the control room until an employee asked him to step into the hall for observation.

Katya stood with him immediately.

“Please stay here,” the employee told her. “So far the changes have only occurred in the hall, and I don’t want to test the distance on you.”

Asgar picked up his mobile from the desk.

“I’ll be right in front of you.”

He went out, closed the door behind him, and approached the partition from the other side.

Katya stood at the glass. Her lips were moving, but he could not hear her.

Asgar pointed to his ear.

Inside the control room, the employee leaned over the desk. A speaker near the door clicked.

“Can you hear me now?” Katya asked.

“Yes.”

She looked at the small microphone in front of her, then back at him.

“Sit down at least. You’ve been standing the whole time.”

One chair remained by the partition. Asgar moved it and sat. Katya settled opposite him behind the glass, her bag on her lap.

For a while they talked that way.

She said she had contacted their sons. The younger one had wanted to come immediately, but Katya had asked him to wait for her call.

“And the older one?”

“He’s out of town. He’s already on his way back.” Katya checked her device. “He’ll be here in the morning.”

Asgar nodded.

Morning was only a few hours away—in the person driving toward the city now along a darkening road.

“Show me to them,” Asgar said.

“I will. I don’t want them to see you for one second, then have something happen here and lose the connection. I barely made it here myself.”

He looked deeper into the hall.

“Okay.”

Katya opened her bag, found a tissue, and wiped beneath her eyes. Then she took out a bottle of water.

“Have you eaten today?”

Asgar remembered the cold coffee at the maintenance yard.

“Not really.”

“Of course.”

She called the employee over. After a while someone brought Asgar food in paper packaging. He ate sitting beside the glass while Katya told him about the drive there. The navigation system had led her to a locked gate. She drove around the building, came back to the same gate, called the caseworker, and only then found the entrance.

“I kept telling them, everything’s locked. They kept telling me, no, it’s open. I can see the gate,” she said. “Turns out we were talking about different sides of the building.”

Asgar smiled with his mouth full. Katya smiled too, then immediately looked away and tightened the cap on her bottle.

He finished eating. Folded the paper so the crumbs stayed inside.

It gradually grew dark aboveground; he could tell from the entrance camera on one of the monitors. The light below never changed. Asgar stood several times, paced along the partition, then returned. Katya watched him. Whenever he sat, she resumed the conversation from exactly where she had left it.

The entire evening could have passed that way.

The caseworker approached the control-room desk and showed the employee something on her display. Through the active intercom Asgar heard the word “journalists” and looked up.

“What is it?”

The woman turned the display toward the glass. In the photograph, Asgar stood in the courtyard of the citizen center beside a police officer. Then she opened the old missing-person notice with his photograph.

“A newsroom sent this. They’re asking whether that’s you. Someone found the old notice after footage from outside the center was posted. I’ve asked that all inquiries go through the division’s reception desk.”

Katya leaned toward the display.

“Don’t give anyone the boys’ numbers. I’ll call them and warn them.”

“We haven’t,” the caseworker said, then stepped away to answer another call.

Asgar stared at the photograph until the screen went dark.

Katya called his name. He sat opposite her again.

He began asking about her apartment. Which floor. Whether there was a balcony. Katya said the balcony was small, but at least the morning sun did not hit it directly.

Asgar pictured a chair, a mug, her bare feet on cool flooring.

“And where would I…” he began, then stopped.

Katya looked up.

“We’ll work that out at home.”

---

Her device signaled while she was putting the bottle back into her bag.

Katya looked at the screen and did not answer at once.

“It’s the younger one.”

Asgar said nothing. After what she had explained, he could not ask.

She turned half away and answered. Through the partition Asgar heard only her side: that she was here, that it was true, that no, he should not come tonight.

Then she fell silent and listened for a long time.

“Wait,” she said into the device. Then, without turning her head: “Voice only. He won’t look.”

She put the device on the desk closer to the partition.

At first there was silence.

Asgar moved nearer.

He prepared himself to hear the boy who had breathed into the microphone and asked whether there was a crane at the yard.

The voice was adult. Even. A little lower than Katya’s.

Completely unfamiliar.

“Was there really no sound?”

Asgar did not understand.

“On the stadium recording,” his son said. “There’s no audio. I always thought it was because the camera was old.”

“The camera simply didn’t record sound.”

“What could you hear?”

Asgar looked at Katya. She sat with her eyes lowered to the table.

“The stands were so loud I couldn’t hear your mother,” he said. “That’s why I went into that corridor. So I could hear what she was saying.”

The other side went quiet.

Asgar waited.

He wanted to ask everything at once: what he did for work, where he lived, whether he had children, whether he remembered his tenth birthday.

Every question, once spoken, would sound like a stranger asking it.

“I’ve been reading about that corridor for twenty years,” his son said at last. “And nowhere did it ever say why you went in there.”

“Now it does.”

His son exhaled shortly—not quite a laugh, but close.

Katya raised one hand toward the device and stopped before touching it.

“I have to go,” he said.

“Yeah.”

The pause was brief and unmistakable.

“Goodbye,” his son said.

And then, almost as the call was ending:

“Dad.”

Katya picked up the device and put it in her bag without looking at the screen.

Asgar sat with his palms on his knees.

Nothing beyond the partition had changed. Someone in the control room shifted a chair.

---

The booth appeared while Asgar was carrying the empty food wrapper toward the door.

First, the reflection of a column broke on a smooth metal panel at the far wall. The vertical line shifted as though someone had moved the lower half of the image sideways.

Asgar stopped.

The red structure emerged about thirty meters from him.

Its lower section sank into the concrete. A beam crossed above the door; from where Asgar stood, there seemed to be too little room for the panel to open. For several seconds the roof edge doubled. The doubling extended along the beam in both directions and ended about ten meters from the booth; beyond that, the metal looked normal.

Then the near section became still too.

Something snapped near the base.

A small gray fragment rolled across the concrete and stopped beside the yellow line.

Asgar set the wrapper on the nearest chair.

Behind the glass, the employee was speaking quickly into his radio. Katya stood. Asgar saw her come to the partition, but he kept looking at the fragment.

The telephone rang inside the booth.

“Asgar, stay where you are,” the speaker said.

He nodded.

The bell rang again.

The employee turned one monitor toward a colleague. They looked from the image to the hall and back. Asgar could not make out what they were saying; the intercom was muted.

At last the speaker came alive.

“Everything is stable at the moment. I still wouldn’t approach yet.”

“I’ll pick up the receiver. From outside, like before,” Asgar said, walking toward the glass.

Katya looked at him.

“And then you’ll come back here?”

“Yes.”

Her eyes lingered on the booth.

“And tell me everything they say.”

Asgar nodded and crossed the hall.

The closer he got, the stronger the urge to turn around. Near the booth, a fresh pale gouge showed in the concrete floor. The red wall cut through its edge. Asgar went around it on the right and stopped before the door.

The handle was warm.

He pulled the panel toward himself. It passed beneath the beam even though a second earlier it had looked as though it should strike it.

Asgar looked up, then at his own hand, and forced himself to focus on the phone.

He lifted the receiver and remained outside the threshold.

“I came out in 2039.”

“Asgar, I hear you.”

The same voice.

Asgar tightened his grip.

“Twenty-five years. Do you understand?”

“Yes. Minutes passed for you. Here, twenty-five years passed.”

Asgar adjusted the receiver against his ear. Across the hall he could see Katya, a small figure behind the transparent partition.

“I found my wife,” he said. “She’s here. I want to stay.”

The woman did not answer immediately.

“You can stay. I will not force you to enter.”

“And what happens to this? The appearances?”

“The connection remains. The booth may appear again. Where and when, I don’t know.”

“The concrete broke here. Right beside it.”

“Stay away from the boundary with the floor.”

Asgar moved one foot farther back.

“I have nowhere else to keep running from you. I can’t live on an empty lot for the rest of my life.”

“Without your entry, we didn’t close it.”

“Where can you close it?”

He expected the usual *I don’t know.*

The woman said:

“To work with an incomplete transition, we need the source.”

Asgar frowned.

“What source?”

“The point from which the module was sent and where it can be received.”

“The module is this booth?”

“Yes.”

He looked at the telephone, at the circular metal dial beneath it.

“So the place where it was built?”

“Not necessarily. The place from which it was sent and where it can be received back.”

“Are there people there who can send me home?”

“I don’t know whether they can send you home.”

“But we can ask them?”

“If we reach the source, they can at least treat your return as a separate problem. I don’t have a solution right now.”

Asgar said nothing. The fingers gripping the door handle were beginning to go numb.

“I need 2014,” he said. “Not another good year. Mine.”

“That date cannot be selected.”

“But you can select the source?”

“No. No one selects the next stop at all. I don’t know when the source will come up.”

Behind the glass, Katya shifted. Asgar could not make out what she was doing and stretched his neck as though that might help.

“If I stay, will you keep looking for a way?”

“Yes. But without continuing the transition, the connection did not close today.”

He stood listening to the faint noise on the line.

Then he said he was going back to speak to his wife, replaced the receiver, and carefully released the door.

---

On the way to the partition, Asgar was already choosing his words.

He wanted to say that there was at least a possibility there. That here they would only wait for the next appearance. That after what had happened to the tripod leg and the concrete, they could not simply leave things as they were.

Katya was standing at the glass when he arrived.

“What did she say?”

He told her. About the source, the sending point, the impossibility of choosing a year.

Katya listened without interrupting. The archive employee stood beside her.

“What if you stay?” she asked.

“They’ll keep working. But they can’t close the connection yet.”

Katya turned to the employee.

“What can you do?”

He drew the microphone closer so Asgar could hear him too.

“Keep him here. Close the hall, arrange separate accommodation, organize visits with his family. If something changes, we change the precautions. I cannot promise the appearances will stop.”

“For how long would he stay here?” Katya asked.

“Tonight, here. Tomorrow we reassess and decide together.”

“And his documents?” Katya looked toward the caseworker behind him.

“We’ll handle the documents. His identity has already been confirmed.”

Asgar listened and looked at Katya’s bag beside the table.

She could bring him clothes tomorrow. The day after that, his sons could come. He could watch them enter this room and hear their adult voices. He could wake in the morning and learn that nothing had happened overnight.

“I can come tomorrow,” Katya said. “And in the evening. As often as necessary. You don’t have to come home tonight.”

“Kat, I haven’t decided anything yet.”

She looked at him more closely.

“Then why are you answering as though I’m trying to talk you into something impossible?”

“I’m explaining that this isn’t going to end by itself.”

“I heard you. So did everyone standing here. They’re telling us what they can do next.”

Asgar looked back at the booth.

Katya fell silent. When he turned to her again, she was closer to the glass.

“You already want to go.”

“I want this to end.”

“So do I. Answer what I actually said.”

He ran a hand over the back of his neck.

“If there’s a chance of reaching the people who can deal with it—”

“So you do want to.”

Asgar looked at the employee beside her. The man stepped back toward the desk but did not leave the room.

“I’m still here,” Asgar said. “We’re talking.”

“You’re listing reasons why you need to leave. Then when I ask you to stay at least for one night, you say you haven’t decided.”

Heat rose into his face.

“Because we have no proof this place will be safe tomorrow.”

“And you have no proof they can send you back.”

“The concrete already broke.”

“I saw it.”

“If that happens beside you—”

Katya stepped back from the glass.

Asgar stopped.

She ran a hand over her mouth and waited until she could speak.

“Don’t do this again. Don’t make it so I’m the one who has to send you away. I can’t tell you there’s no danger. But I can’t say ‘go’ either. I want you to stay. I only just held you.”

He looked at her.

“I know.”

“No. For you, we were talking this morning. But I…”

She did not finish. Picked up the bottle from the desk and put it down again without opening it.

Asgar lowered his head. Near the bottom edge of the partition, the glass reflected his shoes and the legs of the chair.

He could sit down.

Ask for more food.

Say he would not go anywhere until morning.

For several seconds there was nothing in his mind except the chair, food, Katya behind the glass, and one night without another ringing telephone.

Then he imagined the morning when it rang again, and himself explaining once more why he needed to approach it.

“I was already leaning toward going,” he said. “When I came back from the phone.”

Katya nodded once.

“That’s what you should have said.”

“I was afraid.”

“I’m afraid too, Asgar. But I’m not pretending I don’t care whether you stay or go.”

Asgar looked up at her.

“I care.”

“I know,” Katya said.

---

They were silent for a long time.

The staff inside the control room spoke quietly among themselves; the intercom carried only an indistinct background murmur. Katya sat with her hands resting on her bag. Asgar stood by the glass.

He wanted to ask her to come closer, but he was not sure he had the right to ask anything from her now.

At last she stood on her own.

“What will you take?”

He patted his pocket.

“Phone. Documents.”

“Your phone is dead.”

“I know.”

Katya opened her mouth, then only nodded.

“The photographs are on it.”

“Yes.”

Asgar took out the mobile. Her face reflected in the black glass from the other side of the partition.

“I won’t lose them.”

Katya closed her eyes.

He put the phone away.

“That wasn’t the right thing to say.”

She did not answer.

Asgar placed his palm against the partition. Katya looked at it, then raised her own.

The thickness of the glass lay between their fingers.

He could not feel her warmth.

“I love you,” he said.

Her mouth trembled.

“I love you too. That’s why I’m asking you to stay.”

Asgar kept his hand against the glass.

Katya lowered hers first.

The employee by the desk waited without suggesting anything. Behind him was the door to the corridor, and beyond it the way out of the building, the city, Katya’s car.

Asgar looked that way, then back at her.

“I’m going.”

Katya tightened her grip on the strap of her bag.

Asgar waited, but she did not answer.

Then he turned toward the hall.

---

At the booth, Asgar stopped and looked back.

Katya remained beside the partition. At this distance he could barely distinguish her face. Only the pale blouse, the dark line of her hair, the hand gripping the edge of the desk.

He opened the door and lifted the receiver.

“I’m here.”

“I hear you.”

Asgar wanted to ask how long the next transition would last.

Instead, he looked at the threshold.

“There are people outside. They’re staying here.”

“Understood. Step fully inside before you close the door.”

He crossed the red edge and brought the other foot in after him. Turned inside, shifted the receiver so the cord would not lie across the opening. With his free hand he took the inner handle.

Katya was visible through the open doorway.

Asgar hesitated.

Behind the partition, she raised her head. He did not know whether she could see his face or only his shape in the doorway.

He wanted to go back if only to say it differently.

No explanations.

No attempt to make her agree.

He pulled the door.

The panel moved heavily.

Asgar kept his eyes on the pale blouse beyond two layers of glass until the door settled into the frame.

“Closed,” he said.

He released the inner handle.

Something clicked in the receiver.

Katya remained where she was.

Then the line of the partition shifted. The edge of the desk stretched through her figure; Asgar lurched forward and struck his palm against the booth glass.

The hall vanished.

He stood with his hand pressed to the cold surface and stared at daylight outside.

---

Katya held on to the edge of the desk.

Where the red door had been a moment earlier, there was only concrete. The employee beside her called someone over the radio and reached toward the intercom; the light beside the microphone went dark.

She was still looking into the hall when a straight line on the floor bent.

At first Katya thought the glass had trembled.

Then the yellow stripe on the concrete shifted relative to the wall, and the section of hall beyond it seemed to grow shorter. A table by the far wall stretched sideways and immediately returned to normal.

The employee beside her stopped speaking.

In the empty place where no one had been a second earlier, a human figure began to appear.

He did not step out of the booth—the booth was no longer there.

Asgar appeared directly in the hall, as though several mismatched pieces of space had finally assembled around him. He stood sideways with one arm extended, as if he had just tried to brace himself against a wall.

Then he spun around.

Katya recognized his face before she understood anything else.

His cheeks had hollowed.

His hair was longer.

His clothes were different.

“Asgar?”

He saw her.

Katya knew from the way his face changed all at once.

Asgar shouted something and ran toward the control room. She turned to the door and pressed the control beside the opening.

“Open it. He’s back.”

The employee was already at the desk. The panel began sliding aside.

Asgar almost reached the pale line on the floor. His right foot crossed it, and he stumbled as though the step had not landed where he expected.

He stopped and looked down.

Katya pressed a palm to the glass.

Asgar raised his hand toward hers.

His mouth moved again.

The speaker was silent.

Katya pulled the door toward herself.

Asgar stepped along the pale line, and the floor between them stretched.

She did not see it at once: he was moving toward her, but the distance was not shrinking.

Then it grew.

“This way, Asgar. I’m opening it. Wait.”

He stopped and reached out.

Katya held the edge of the door and stretched her free hand toward him even though several meters still separated them.

Asgar moved again.

The pale line split in two.

A different, light-colored floor showed through the concrete near his feet, then the edge of a table.

Asgar turned sideways, trying to go around it.

The table moved with him.

Katya struck the glass with her palm.

Asgar lunged toward her and fell forward with one arm outstretched.

For an instant his body stretched with the changing hall, then a white wall from another room showed through his shoulder.

“Asgar!”

He was still looking at her.

Then he disappeared.

The floor became straight again.

The yellow stripe returned to its place.

Beyond the partition lay the same empty technical hall, and Katya kept her arm extended toward the opening for several more seconds.
