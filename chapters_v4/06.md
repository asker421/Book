Chapter 6. The Right to Your Own Name

He walked down the street because there was supposed to be an intersection farther down.

At first he moved confidently. Around the bend he expected a long wall with gates, then a shop on the corner and the descent he had driven down many times. The wall was gone. Between two buildings, a broad staircase lined with shrubs led downward. Asgar passed it, then came back and looked down. The sea was visible from there.

He stood for a moment, matching the opening to what he remembered, then kept walking. A few minutes later the street brought him to a road he finally recognized. The facades had changed, a row of low buildings was gone, but the turn remained. Asgar even quickened his pace.

Already in the crosswalk, he caught himself preparing to tell Katya how much they had rebuilt here.

He stopped in the middle of the lane. A car turning right braked. The driver raised a hand, letting him pass. Asgar reached the sidewalk and stopped by the barrier.

The phone in his pocket was silent. He took it out again. Opened the chat with Katya. The last message he had sent was the address of the repair depot. Above it were her ordinary messages, photos of the children, a question about what time he would be home. Asgar scrolled back a little and stopped on a picture: the younger boy sat on the floor holding something in both hands, the older one leaned toward him and was blurred by movement.

He enlarged the younger boy's face. At that age, children changed even over a few months. Asgar put the phone away without closing the picture and went to look for a taxi.

---

He found a taxi stand near a shopping center. He recognized it by the cars lined up along the curb, though at first he had taken it for an ordinary parking area.

The first driver was sitting with the window open, eating from a rectangular container. Asgar gave the address. The man clarified the street and nodded toward the rear door.

The cabin smelled of warm bread. Asgar sat down and pulled the seat belt across. Only after it clicked into place did he notice that the driver was still holding the container.

"Can I pay cash?" he asked.

"Sure. Register the trip."

The driver pointed to a small device by the front seat. A map was displayed on the screen.

Asgar leaned toward it.

"My phone doesn't work. Can't you do it yourself?"

"You can do it without a phone. Press there."

He pressed where indicated. A choice of trip-registration methods appeared. Some of the words were familiar, but the combination irritated him: everything wanted him to confirm something through something he did not have.

"Do you have ID?" the driver asked.

Asgar took out his identification card.

The driver set the container on the passenger seat and turned toward him with more attention now.

"Old one. Try the reader. Under the screen."

Asgar inserted the card into a narrow slot. The mechanism pulled it all the way in.

His name appeared on the screen.

The simplicity of it made him feel better. First name, surname, everything correct. So at least here he still existed.

Then the display changed.

The driver stopped wiping his fingers with a napkin.

"What?" Asgar asked.

"Document check."

He turned his own screen toward himself, read something, and looked in the mirror.

Asgar pressed the return button. The card did not come out.

"Give me my document back."

"I'm not holding it."

"Then cancel it."

The driver touched his screen. Asgar heard a short signal, but the reader remained closed.

"It says an employee is required."

"What employee? I asked you to take me to an address."

"I see that."

The driver turned toward him.

"Is that your ID?"

Asgar looked at his hands. At the folded napkin, the grease mark near a fingernail.

"Mine."

"The system says the owner is dead."

For several seconds Asgar heard only the air conditioner running.

Then he yanked the door handle, got out, and walked around to the driver's window.

"Give it back."

"I'll call an administrator now."

"I don't need an administrator. I need my document."

"Listen, I can't get it out of there with my hands."

The driver's earlier laziness was gone. The container was already closed; both hands rested on the steering wheel.

Asgar bent toward the window.

"Do you understand that I'm standing right here?"

"I understand that I have either someone else's document or an error in my car. I can't resolve that."

He pointed toward the entrance of the shopping center.

"There's the transport desk. An employee will come and open it. Don't break anything."

Only then did Asgar notice he was gripping the car door so tightly it looked as though he meant to tear it off, and he let go.

---

The woman from the transport desk came with a tablet and a key on a short cord. First she spoke to the driver in Azerbaijani, then asked Asgar to wait nearby. He replied in the same language that he was waiting only for his ID.

She opened the side cover of the device.

The card lay between the rollers inside. The woman took it out, compared the photograph to Asgar's face, and lingered on the date of birth.

"Is the document confiscated?" he asked.

"No. The automatic check stopped the registration. Take it."

She held out the card. Asgar put it straight into his wallet.

Now he could leave. Another car, cash, no device like this. If that failed—on foot. He would get home.

"There's a municipal citizen services center nearby," the woman said. "We only handle trips. They deal with documents and archived statuses. Entrance from the side street."

"Later."

He turned toward the road.

"They'll reject this document again," she called after him.

Asgar stopped.

"For everything?"

"I don't know. We only handle trips here. They'll tell you at the center."

She had already turned back to the driver, asking whether the car could be cleared for the next fare.

Asgar stepped beneath an awning. Took out his wallet and counted the money. The banknotes looked exactly as they had that morning: one folded in quarters, another with a torn corner. He tried to remember how many years banknotes stayed in circulation after a replacement. Couldn't.

Another taxi stood across the road.

He took a few steps toward the crossing and stopped.

Even if they drove him there, what would happen at the door?

The apartment could have been sold. The building could be different. Katya could have moved a year ago, ten years ago. He would reach the entrance and start pressing buttons, telling strangers his surname.

Asgar went back to the employee.

"Can you give me a phone? I need to call my wife."

She looked at him, then at the tablet.

"Give me the number."

He dictated it. The woman dialed and put the call on speaker.

After several rings, a man answered.

Asgar leaned closer at once.

"Can I speak to Katya?"

"You have the wrong number."

"Wait. This number..."

He repeated the digits. The man confirmed them.

"Katya used this number. Ekaterina. Tell me, how long have you..."

"About seven years. I don't know any Ekaterina."

The call ended.

The employee lowered the tablet.

Asgar stared at it. He knew several more numbers by heart. His parents. Old friends. Rauf. He could call everyone one by one, ask, explain, ask again.

"Where's the entrance?" he said.

The woman pointed toward the corner of the building.

---

Above the glass doors it said: "Municipal Citizen Services Center." It was cool inside. A strip of freshly washed floor was drying near the entrance; Asgar walked around it and approached the counter. A young man with his sleeves rolled up sat behind it.

Asgar placed his ID in front of him.

"I need to find my wife. For some reason this document says I'm dead."

The young man stopped typing.

"Have you already contacted us about the record?"

"No."

"May I check it?"

Asgar pushed the card toward him.

This time he watched every hand movement. The employee placed the document on flat glass beside the keyboard, waited for a signal, and returned it.

"Please state your full name."

Asgar did.

"And your date of birth."

He answered. The young man looked at the screen, then at him again.

"I'll call a specialist. Please have a seat."

"How long?"

"I'll check now."

"I only need to contact my wife. Find her address or phone number."

"I'll pass that on."

Asgar remained by the counter until the employee made the call. He heard his own name, words about an old ID, then the man lowered his voice. Asgar wanted to lean over the desk and look at the screen himself.

Instead he went to the chairs.

An elderly woman beside him was rummaging through her bag. She took out glasses, a case, a small rolled packet. A folder of paper documents lay on her knees. Asgar watched her smooth a bent corner of one sheet and suddenly felt grateful for that folder. For the fact that it was paper, for the ordinary elastic band stretched around its spine.

The phone vibrated in his hand. He almost dropped it. A low-battery warning was on the screen. Asgar turned the screen off and clamped the mobile between his knees.

---

The woman who came out for him was about his age. A crease from her clothing was imprinted on her shoulder, as though she had been leaning against the back of a chair for a long time before this.

She introduced herself as a document-recovery coordinator and invited him into a small room behind the counter.

Asgar went in first. A table, two chairs, a screen on a swiveling stand. A water dispenser stood in the corner. The door remained slightly open.

"Please sit," the woman said. "The employee told me you're looking for your spouse."

"Yes."

"First we'll sort out your document. It opened an old record, and the person in the photograph should be twenty-five years older than you."

"The document is mine."

"I understand. But family data can only be opened after identity verification."

Asgar sat on the edge of the chair.

"I can give you the address. Phone number. Where we got married, when. What else do you need?"

"To begin with, we need to understand why you're using an ID that expired a long time ago."

He looked down at the card.

He could say he had lived abroad for a long time. Lost newer documents. That they had misunderstood him at the taxi stand.

The woman waited.

"This morning it was the year two thousand fourteen," Asgar said. "For me."

She did not reach for the phone. She only pulled her chair closer to the table.

"Tell me where you were this morning."

He started with the repair depot.

At first he spoke quickly, skipping around, then saw the woman making short notes and forced himself to go back to the beginning. Friday, the parking garage, the phone conversation. Waiting behind the city fence. The camera. Katya, whom he had promised to call.

He had to tell the stadium separately.

The woman clarified:

"The first time you entered was August sixteenth?"

Asgar raised his head.

"I haven't told you the date yet."

She turned the screen toward him.

An archive card was on it. Surname, first name. A photograph he remembered: it had been taken for documents, and afterward Katya had said he looked as though he had argued with the photographer.

Below it was the date of the first disappearance.

16.08.2014.

"This came up with your ID," the woman said. "That's why they sent you to me."

Asgar leaned toward the screen.

Return—August twentieth. Second disappearance—August twenty-fourth, two thousand fourteen.

Asgar reread the last line. The day and month matched today.

He ran a finger along the edge of the table.

"Who recorded this?"

"This contains information from the missing-person case. The full documents are still loading."

"Kerimov? Rauf Kerimov?"

She found the relevant field.

"Yes. He's listed in the materials."

Asgar leaned back.

Rauf had stood by the fence and raised a hand. Asgar had seen him through the doorway. Everything that happened after that to the man by the gates had already had time to become material in an old case.

"Call him."

"We'll try. But first we'll finish the verification."

"He saw me. He'll confirm it."

"If we find a current contact, we'll ask. But I still need to establish that you are actually the person on the record."

She spoke patiently, and the patience was beginning to make him angry.

Asgar pointed at the screen.

"That's my photograph. The dates match. I told you about the stadium, about the depot. What else is missing?"

"That you're the person on the record. Everything else could have been learned."

"Why would someone else..."

He stopped. The woman did not help him finish the question.

Asgar looked at the lower part of the record.

It said he had been declared dead.

He read the line in full, together with the reference to the court decision, then went back to the beginning.

"Did they find a body?"

"No. No body was found. The court declared you dead later."

"Who filed?"

The woman looked at the screen.

"The summary record doesn't say."

"Katya?"

"I don't know."

Asgar stood up. The chair shifted and struck the wall.

He walked to the water dispenser, took a cup, but could not line it up under the tap at first. He had to set it on the grate. The water came too slowly.

"I called her today," he said.

The woman stayed silent while he watched the thin stream.

"Before I went in. She told me to take the phone."

The cup overflowed. Asgar pulled it away; water ran over his fingers. He drank several swallows and set the cup on the table without putting a napkin under it.

"How many years did they look for me?"

"I don't know. You can't tell that from the date of the court ruling. We need to open the full case."

He wanted to ask more. Who went to court. Who gathered the documents. How Katya said his name in front of strangers. What they had needed that decision for.

He looked at the clock above the door. The hands moved. Everything here kept going while he stood beside the water dispenser.

"What do you need for verification?"

The woman pushed a small scanner toward him.

"Fingerprints. Then a photograph. There are samples in your archive for comparison. If the match is confirmed, we'll complete an initial identification and can start locating your relatives."

Asgar sat down.

"Let's do it."

---

The scanner had to be wiped down: his palm had left a damp print. The woman gave him a wipe, asked him to place his fingers again, then to look into the camera above the screen.

He obeyed. Sat with his hands on his knees while she compared the results.

Outside the door, someone's child demanded that a bottle be given back. An adult voice answered that the cap was open and everything would spill. Asgar turned toward the sound.

The woman noticed.

"Would you like me to close it?"

"No."

She sent a request for an additional check and explained that they now needed a response from the duty specialist. Asgar asked how long it would take. She did not promise five minutes.

He put his phone on the table.

The ceiling light reflected in the black glass. Asgar turned it on, found the photo of the children, and held it out to the woman.

"These are them."

She looked, then carefully placed the phone between them.

"Keep the photo with you. I need their full names."

He gave them. Then Katya's name.

The woman wrote it down, repeated it to check pronunciation. Asgar corrected one letter, and they went back to waiting. Several times he was about to ask about a charger, but each time he forgot as soon as something changed on her screen. The corridor grew quieter. The elderly woman with the folder passed the doorway, holding the documents to her chest.

At last the coordinator straightened. Instead of answering, she turned the archive photograph and the new image toward herself. Asgar could see both at an angle. In one he was neatly combed; in the other his hair stuck out above his temple. The woman enlarged the dates, then reached for the scanner she had already moved to the edge of the desk and stopped.

"Again?" Asgar asked.

"No. The duty specialist checked the source samples separately. The fingerprints match. The face does too."

She dialed an internal number. Asgar waited for them to start looking for Katya, but the coordinator asked to be connected to the shift supervisor.

"The confirmation came through. I opened both records. Yes, he's sitting in front of me." She listened to a question and looked at Asgar again. "No, this isn't just the old photograph. Today's image was taken here, in front of me. Pass the message to the closed case. And I need access to the complete missing-person file."

Asgar slid the phone toward her hand.

"You said after verification you could look for my family. I did everything. Now I have to wait for someone else too?"

She finished the call, but her fingers remained on the receiver.

"No. Enough. We're looking for your wife now. They can deal with the case separately."

The woman opened another form. Asgar pulled the phone back toward himself. In the photograph, the younger boy was still holding his toy. The older stood beside him, blurred because he had never known how to stay still for a picture.

Anything could have happened over those years. Until now, he had been thinking about the apartment door. About who would open it, how to explain the way he looked, what to say first. He had imagined Katya older, the children grown. In none of those images was there an empty space where someone should have been.

The woman asked him to confirm his wife's last known address.

Asgar gave it and fell silent.

She began typing it in, but he covered the phone lying in front of him with his palm.

"Wait."

The coordinator looked at him.

"First tell me... Can you find out whether she's alive?"

He wanted to add the name even though he had just dictated it.

"Katya. Is she alive?"
