Chapter 6. The Right to One’s Own Name

He went downhill because there was supposed to be an intersection at the bottom.

At first he walked with confidence. Around the bend there should have been a long wall with gates, then a corner shop, then the descent he had driven down countless times.

The wall was gone.

Between two buildings, a broad staircase lined with shrubs led downward. Asgar walked past it, then came back and looked down.

He could see the sea.

He stood there for a moment, trying to reconcile that opening with what he remembered, then kept going. A few minutes later the street brought him to a road he finally recognized. The façades had changed, a row of low buildings had disappeared, but the curve of the road remained.

Asgar even quickened his pace.

Halfway across a crossing, he caught himself preparing to tell Katya how much they had rebuilt here.

He stopped in the middle of the lane.

A car turning right braked. The driver raised a hand to let him pass. Asgar reached the sidewalk and stood beside the barrier.

The phone in his pocket remained silent.

He took it out again. Opened his conversation with Katya. The last message he had sent was the address of the maintenance yard. Above it were her ordinary messages, photographs of the children, a question about what time he would be home.

Asgar scrolled back a little and stopped at one picture: the younger boy sat on the floor holding something in both hands; the older one was bending toward him and had blurred with the motion.

He enlarged the younger boy’s face.

At that age, children changed even over a few months.

Asgar put the phone away without closing the photograph and went looking for a taxi.

---

He found a taxi rank beside a shopping center. He recognized it by the cars lined up along the curb, though at first he had taken them for ordinary parked vehicles.

The first driver sat with the window open, eating from a rectangular container. Asgar gave him the address. The man confirmed the street, then nodded toward the rear door.

The car smelled of warm bread. Asgar got in and pulled the seat belt across himself. Only after it clicked into place did he notice that the driver was still holding his food.

“Can I pay cash?” he asked.

“Yes. Register the ride first.”

The driver pointed at a small device near the front seat. A map filled the screen.

Asgar leaned toward it.

“My phone isn’t working. Can’t you do it?”

“You can do it without a phone. Tap there.”

He touched the indicated spot. A list of ways to register the trip appeared. He recognized most of the words, but the combinations irritated him: everything seemed to require confirming something through a thing he did not have.

“Got ID?” the driver asked.

Asgar took out his identity card.

The driver set the food container on the passenger seat and turned to look at him more closely.

“Old one. Try the reader. Under the screen.”

Asgar slid the card into a narrow slot.

The mechanism swallowed it completely.

His name appeared on the screen.

The simplicity of it brought a rush of relief.

First name, surname, all correct.

So at least here, he still existed.

Then the display changed.

The driver stopped wiping his fingers with a napkin.

“What?” Asgar asked.

“Document verification.”

The man turned his own display toward himself, read something, then looked into the mirror.

Asgar pressed the return button.

The card did not come out.

“Give me my ID.”

“I’m not holding it.”

“Then cancel it.”

The driver touched his screen. Asgar heard a short tone, but the reader remained sealed.

“It says an employee has to release it.”

“What employee? I asked you to take me to an address.”

“I can see that.”

The driver turned toward him.

“Is this your ID?”

Asgar looked at his hands. At the folded napkin, at the trace of grease beside one fingernail.

“It’s mine.”

“The system says the owner is dead.”

For several seconds Asgar heard only the air conditioner.

Then he yanked the door open, got out, and walked around to the driver’s window.

“Give it back.”

“I’m calling an attendant.”

“I don’t need an attendant. I need my document.”

“Listen, I can’t pull it out with my hands.”

The driver no longer sounded lazy. The food container was closed now; both hands rested on the wheel.

Asgar bent toward the window.

“Do you understand that I’m standing right here?”

“I understand that I have somebody else’s ID in my car, or a system error. I can’t fix either one.”

He pointed toward the entrance of the shopping center.

“Transport desk over there. An employee will come open it. Don’t break anything.”

Only then did Asgar realize he was gripping the car door as though he meant to tear it off.

He let go.

---

The transport-desk employee arrived with a tablet and a key on a short cord. She spoke to the driver in Azerbaijani first, then asked Asgar to wait beside the car. He answered her in the same language that all he was waiting for was his ID.

She opened a side cover on the device.

The card lay between two rollers. The woman removed it, compared the photograph to Asgar’s face, and paused at the date of birth.

“Are you confiscating it?” he asked.

“No. Automatic verification stopped the registration. Here.”

She handed it over. Asgar immediately put it back in his wallet.

Now he could leave.

Another car. Cash. No machine like this.

If that failed, he could walk.

He would get home.

“There’s a municipal citizen service center nearby,” the woman said. “We only handle transport. They deal with identity records and archived status. Entrance is on the side street.”

“Later.”

He turned toward the road.

“They’ll reject this document again,” she called after him.

Asgar stopped.

“For everything?”

“I don’t know. We only handle transport. They’ll tell you at the center.”

She had already turned back to the driver, discussing whether the car could be cleared for the next booking.

Asgar stepped beneath the awning. Took out his wallet and counted his cash. The banknotes looked exactly as they had that morning: one folded into quarters, another with a torn corner.

He tried to remember how many years paper money remained in circulation after a redesign.

Could not.

Another taxi stood on the opposite side of the road.

He took several steps toward the crossing and stopped.

Even if someone drove him there, what would be waiting at the door?

The apartment might have been sold. The building might have changed. Katya might have moved a year ago, ten years ago.

He would reach the entrance and start pressing buttons, telling strangers his surname.

Asgar went back to the employee.

“Can I use your phone? I need to call my wife.”

She looked at him, then at the tablet.

“Give me the number.”

He recited it.

The woman dialed and switched to speaker.

After several rings, a man answered.

Asgar leaned closer immediately.

“Can I speak to Katya?”

“You’ve got the wrong number.”

“Wait. This number…”

He repeated the digits.

The man confirmed them.

“Katya used this number. Ekaterina. Tell me, how long have you had—”

“About seven years. I don’t know any Ekaterina.”

The call ended.

The employee lowered the tablet.

Asgar stared at it.

He knew several other numbers by heart. His parents. Old friends. Rauf.

He could call them all, one after another, asking questions, explaining, asking again.

“Where’s the entrance?” he said.

The woman pointed toward the corner of the building.

---

Above the glass doors were the words: *Municipal Citizen Service Center.*

Inside, the air was cool. A strip of freshly washed floor was drying near the entrance; Asgar went around it and approached the desk. A young man with rolled sleeves sat behind it.

Asgar placed his ID in front of him.

“I need to find my wife. For some reason this document says I’m dead.”

The young man stopped typing.

“Have you already filed a request about the record?”

“No.”

“May I check it?”

Asgar slid the card toward him.

This time he watched every movement of the man’s hands. The employee placed the document on a flat glass reader beside the keyboard, waited for a tone, then returned it.

“Please state your full name.”

Asgar did.

“And your date of birth.”

He answered.

The young man looked at the screen, then back at him.

“I’ll get a specialist. Please take a seat.”

“How long?”

“I’ll check now.”

“I only need to contact my wife. Find her address or phone number.”

“I’ll tell them.”

Asgar stayed beside the desk until the employee made the call. He heard his name, words about an old identity card, then the man lowered his voice. Asgar wanted to lean across the counter and read the screen for himself.

Instead, he went over to the chairs.

An elderly woman nearby was rummaging through her bag. She pulled out glasses, a case, a small rolled-up packet. A folder of paper documents rested on her knees. Asgar watched her smooth the bent corner of one sheet and suddenly felt grateful for the folder.

For the fact that it was paper.

For the ordinary elastic band stretched around the spine.

His phone vibrated in his hand.

He almost dropped it.

A low-battery warning was on the screen. Asgar switched it off and clamped the mobile between his knees.

---

The woman who came for him was about his age. A crease from her clothing was pressed into one shoulder, as though she had been sitting against the back of a chair for a long time.

She introduced herself as a document-restoration caseworker and invited him into a small room behind the desk.

Asgar went in first.

A table, two armchairs, a display on a pivoting stand. A water dispenser stood in one corner.

The door remained partly open.

“Please sit,” the woman said. “The front desk told me you’re looking for your spouse.”

“Yes.”

“First we need to deal with your document. It opened an old record, and the person in the photograph should be twenty-five years older than you.”

“It’s my document.”

“I understand. But I can’t open family information until we verify your identity.”

Asgar sat on the edge of the chair.

“I can give you the address. The phone number. Where we got married, when. What else do you need?”

“To begin with, I need to understand why you’re using an ID that expired a long time ago.”

He looked down at the card.

He could say he had lived abroad for years. Lost the newer documents. That the taxi driver had misunderstood him.

The woman waited.

“This morning it was 2014,” Asgar said. “For me.”

She did not reach for a phone.

She only drew her chair closer to the desk.

“Tell me where you were this morning.”

He began with the maintenance yard.

At first he spoke too quickly, skipping ahead. Then he saw the woman making short notes and forced himself back to the beginning. Friday. The parking garage. The conversation through the booth. Waiting behind the city fence. The camera. Katya, whom he had promised to call.

He had to tell the stadium story separately.

The woman clarified:

“You first entered on August sixteenth?”

Asgar lifted his head.

“I haven’t told you the date yet.”

She turned the display toward him.

An archived record filled the screen. His surname. His first name. A photograph he remembered: it had been taken for documents, and Katya had later said he looked as though he had been arguing with the photographer.

Below it was the date of the first disappearance.

16.08.2014.

“This came up with your ID,” the woman said. “That’s why they sent you to me.”

Asgar leaned toward the screen.

Return: August twentieth.

Second disappearance: August twenty-fourth, 2014.

Asgar reread the last line.

The day and month matched today.

He ran one finger along the edge of the table.

“Who entered this?”

“These details come from the missing-person investigation. The full documents are still loading.”

“Kerimov? Rauf Kerimov?”

She found the relevant field.

“Yes. He’s named in the case.”

Asgar leaned back.

Rauf standing by the fence, raising one hand.

Asgar had seen him through the doorway.

Everything that happened to the man at the gate after that moment had already had time to become material in an old case file.

“Call him.”

“We can try. But first we finish verifying you.”

“He saw me. He can confirm it.”

“If we find a current contact, we’ll ask. But I still have to establish that you are actually the person in this record.”

She spoke patiently, and the patience was beginning to make him angry.

Asgar pointed at the display.

“That’s my photograph. The dates match. I told you about the stadium, the yard. What else is missing?”

“Proof that you are the person in the record. Everything else could have been learned.”

“Why would someone else—”

He stopped.

The woman did not help him finish the question.

Asgar looked at the lower part of the record.

It said he had been declared dead.

He read the line in full, including the reference to a court ruling, then went back to the beginning.

“Did they find a body?”

“No. No body was found. You were declared dead by the court later.”

“Who applied?”

The woman checked the screen.

“That isn’t in the summary record.”

“Katya?”

“I don’t know.”

Asgar stood.

The chair moved back and struck the wall.

He went to the water dispenser, took a cup, but could not get it under the tap at first. He had to set it on the grate.

The water came out too slowly.

“I called her today,” he said.

The woman remained silent while he watched the thin stream.

“Before I went in. She told me to take my phone.”

The cup overflowed.

Asgar pulled it away; water ran across his fingers. He drank several swallows and set the cup on the table without putting a napkin under it.

“How many years did they look for me?”

“I don’t know. The court date doesn’t tell us that. We need the full file.”

He wanted to ask more.

Who had gone to court.

Who had collected the documents.

How Katya had said his name in front of strangers.

What they had needed that ruling for.

He looked at the clock above the door. The hands were moving.

Everything here kept going while he stood by the water dispenser.

“What do you need for verification?”

The woman pushed a small scanner toward him.

“Fingerprints. Then a photograph. Your archived record contains comparison samples. If they match, we can establish an initial identification and start looking for your relatives.”

Asgar sat down.

“Do it.”

---

They had to wipe the scanner because his palm left a damp mark. The woman gave him a tissue, asked him to place his fingers again, then to look into the camera above the display.

He complied.

Sat with his hands on his knees while she compared the results.

Outside the partly open door, a child was demanding a bottle back. An adult voice replied that the cap was open and everything would spill.

Asgar turned toward the sound.

The woman noticed.

“Would you like me to close the door?”

“No.”

She sent a request for additional verification and explained that they now needed an on-duty specialist to confirm it. Asgar asked how long that would take.

She did not promise five minutes.

He placed his phone on the table.

A ceiling light reflected in the black glass. Asgar switched it on, found the photograph of the children, and held it toward her.

“That’s them.”

She looked, then carefully set the phone down between them.

“Keep the photograph with you. I need their full names.”

He gave them.

Then Katya’s.

The woman wrote each one down, repeated them to check pronunciation. Asgar corrected one letter, and they waited again.

Several times he meant to ask about a charger, but every time something changed on her screen he forgot. The corridor grew quieter. The elderly woman with the folder walked past the open doorway with her papers held to her chest.

At last, the caseworker straightened.

Instead of giving an answer, she brought the archived photograph and today’s image side by side. Asgar could see them both at an angle. In one he was neatly combed; in the other his hair stuck up above the temple.

The woman enlarged the dates, then reached for the scanner she had already moved to the edge of the desk and stopped.

“Again?” Asgar asked.

“No. The on-duty specialist checked the source samples separately. The fingerprints match. So does the face.”

She dialed an internal number.

Asgar expected her to start looking for Katya, but the caseworker asked for the senior shift supervisor.

“Confirmation is in. I opened both records. Yes, he’s sitting in front of me.” She listened to a question and looked at Asgar again. “No, not just the archived photo. Today’s image was taken here, in front of me. Send a notice through the closed case. And I need access to the full missing-person file.”

Asgar pushed his phone toward her hand.

“You said after verification we could look for my family. I did everything. Now I have to wait for someone else?”

She ended the call, but her fingers remained on the handset.

“No. Enough. We’re looking for your wife now. Let someone else deal with the case.”

The woman opened another form.

Asgar pulled his phone back toward himself. In the photograph, the younger boy was still holding his toy. The older one stood beside him, blurred because he had never known how to stay still for a picture.

Anything could have happened over those years.

Until now, Asgar had been thinking about the apartment door. Who would open it. How to explain the way he looked. What his first words should be.

He had pictured Katya older. The children grown.

None of those pictures had contained an empty space where someone should have been.

The caseworker asked him to confirm his wife’s last known address.

Asgar gave it, then fell silent.

She began entering it, but he covered the phone lying in front of him with his palm.

“Wait.”

The caseworker looked at him.

“First tell me… Can you find out whether she’s alive?”

He wanted to add her name, though he had only just given it.

“Katya. Is she alive?”
