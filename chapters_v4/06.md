Chapter 6. The Right to Your Own Name

He walked downhill because there was supposed to be an intersection below.

At first he moved confidently. Beyond the turn, there should have been a long wall with gates, then a shop on the corner and the descent he had driven down many times. The wall was gone. A broad staircase lined with bushes ran between two buildings. Asgar walked past, then came back and looked down. From there, he could see the sea.

He stood for a moment, comparing that opening with what he remembered, then kept walking. After a few minutes, the street brought him to a road he finally recognized. The facades had changed, a row of low buildings was gone, but the bend remained. Asgar even quickened his pace.

Already on the crossing, he caught himself thinking about telling Katya how much everything here had been rebuilt.

He stopped in the middle of the lane. A car turning right braked. The driver raised a hand, letting him pass. Asgar reached the sidewalk and stood by the barrier.

The phone in his pocket remained silent. He took it out again. Opened his chat with Katya. The last message he had sent was the address of the repair depot. Above it were her ordinary messages, photos of the children, a question asking what time he would be home. Asgar scrolled back a little and stopped on a picture: the younger boy was sitting on the floor holding something in both hands, the older one leaning toward him, blurred by movement.

He enlarged the younger boy's face. At that age, children changed even over a few months. Asgar put the phone away without closing the photo and went to look for a taxi.

---

He found a taxi stand near a shopping center. He recognized it by the cars lined along the curb, though at first he had taken it for an ordinary parking area.

The first driver was sitting with the window open, eating from a rectangular container. Asgar gave the address. The man clarified the street and nodded toward the back door.

The interior smelled of warm bread. Asgar got in and pulled the seat belt across himself. Only after clicking it shut did he notice that the driver was still holding the container.

"Can I pay cash?" he asked.

"You can. Set up the ride."

The driver pointed at a small device near the front seat. A map was displayed on the screen.

Asgar leaned toward it.

"My phone doesn't work. Can't you do it yourself?"

"You can do it without a phone. Press there."

He pressed where indicated. A choice of ride-registration methods appeared. Some of the words were familiar, but the combination irritated him: everything wanted him to confirm something through something he did not have.

"Do you have ID?" the driver asked.

Asgar took out his identity card.

The driver set the container on the passenger seat and turned around, paying closer attention now.

"Old one. Try the reader. Under the screen."

Asgar inserted the card into a narrow slot. The mechanism pulled it all the way in.

His name appeared on the screen.

The simplicity of it brought relief. First name, surname, all correct. So at least here he still existed.

Then the image changed.

The driver stopped wiping his fingers with a napkin.

"What?" Asgar asked.

"Document verification."

He turned his own display toward himself, read something, and looked in the mirror.

Asgar pressed the return button. The card did not come out.

"Give me my document."

"I'm not holding it."

"Then cancel it."

The driver touched his screen. Asgar heard a short signal, but the reader stayed closed.

"It says we need an employee."

"What employee? I asked you to take me to an address."

"I see that."

The driver turned toward him.

"Is this your ID?"

Asgar looked at his hands. At the folded napkin, at the greasy mark beside one fingernail.

"Mine."

"The system says the owner is dead."

For several seconds, Asgar heard only the air conditioner running.

Then he yanked the door handle, got out of the car, and walked around to the driver's window.

"Give it back."

"I'll call an administrator now."

"I don't need an administrator. I need my document."

"Listen, I can't get it out of there by hand."

The driver no longer sounded lazy. The container was already closed, both hands resting on the steering wheel.

Asgar bent toward the window.

"Do you understand that I'm standing right here?"

"I understand that I've got someone else's document in my car or an error. I can't resolve that."

He pointed toward the entrance to the shopping center.

"There's a transport desk over there. An employee will come and open it. Don't break anything."

Only then did Asgar notice that he was gripping the car door so hard as though he meant to tear it off, and he let go.

---

The woman from the transport desk came with a tablet and a key on a short cord. First she spoke to the driver in Azerbaijani, then asked Asgar to wait beside them. He answered in the same language that he was only waiting for his identity card.

She opened the side panel of the device.

The card lay inside between the rollers. The woman removed it, compared the photograph with Asgar's face, and paused on the date of birth.

"Is the document being confiscated?" he asked.

"No. The automatic verification stopped the booking. Here."

She held out the card. Asgar immediately put it into his wallet.

He could leave now. Another car, cash, without that device. If that failed, on foot. He would get home.

"There's a city citizen service center nearby," the woman said. "We only handle rides. They deal with documents and archival statuses. Entrance from the side street."

"Later."

He turned toward the road.

"They'll reject this document again," she called after him.

Asgar stopped.

"For everything?"

"I don't know. We only handle rides. The center can tell you."

She had already turned back to the driver, working out whether the car could be released for the next booking.

Asgar moved under the awning. He took out his wallet and counted the cash. The banknotes looked the same as they had that morning: one folded into quarters, another torn at the corner. He tried to remember how many years banknotes remained in circulation after being replaced. He could not.

Another taxi stood on the opposite side of the road.

He took a few steps toward the crossing and stopped.

Even if someone drove him there, what would be at the door?

The apartment might have been sold. The building might be different. Katya might have moved a year ago, ten years ago. He would reach the entrance and start pressing buttons, telling strangers his surname.

Asgar went back to the employee.

"Can you let me use a phone? To call my wife."

She looked at him, then at the tablet.

"Tell me the number."

He dictated it. The woman dialed and turned on speakerphone.

After several rings, a man answered.

Asgar immediately leaned closer.

"Can I speak to Katya?"

"You have the wrong number."

"Wait. This number..."

He repeated the digits. The man confirmed them.

"Katya used this number. Ekaterina. Tell me, how long have you had—"

"About seven years. I don't know any Ekaterina."

The call ended.

The employee lowered the tablet.

Asgar stared at it. He knew several more numbers by heart. His parents. Old friends. Rauf. He could call everyone one by one, ask, explain, ask again.

"Where's the entrance?" he said.

The woman pointed toward the corner of the building.

---

Above the glass doors were the words: "City Citizen Service Center." It was cool inside. A strip of freshly washed floor was drying near the entrance; Asgar walked around it and approached the desk. A young man with his sleeves rolled up was sitting behind it.

Asgar placed his identity card in front of him.

"I need to find my wife. For some reason this document says I'm dead."

The young man stopped typing.

"Have you already contacted anyone about the record?"

"No."

"May I check it?"

Asgar pushed the card toward him.

This time he watched every hand movement. The employee placed the document on a flat glass surface beside the keyboard, waited for a signal, and returned it.

"Please give me your full name."

Asgar did.

"And date of birth."

He answered. The young man looked at the screen, then at him again.

"I'll call a specialist. Please have a seat."

"How long?"

"I'll check now."

"I only need to contact my wife. Find her address or phone number."

"I'll pass that on."

Asgar remained by the desk until the employee made the call. He heard his own name, the words about an old identity card, then the man lowered his voice. Asgar wanted to lean across the desk and look at the screen himself.

Instead, he went over to the chairs.

Beside him, an elderly woman was rummaging through her bag. She took out glasses, a case, a small folded packet. A folder of paper documents rested on her knees. Asgar watched her smooth down a bent corner of a page and suddenly felt grateful for that folder. For the fact that it was paper, for the ordinary elastic band stretched around the spine.

The phone vibrated in his hand. He nearly dropped it. A low-battery warning was on the screen. Asgar turned the screen off and trapped the mobile between his knees.

---

The woman who came out for him was around his age. A crease from her clothing had imprinted itself on her shoulder, as though she had been sitting against the back of a chair for a long time beforehand.

She introduced herself as a document-restoration caseworker and invited him into a small room behind the desk.

Asgar went in first. A table, two armchairs, a screen on a swivel stand. A water cooler stood in the corner. The door remained partly open.

"Please sit," the woman said. "The employee told me you're looking for your spouse."

"Yes."

"First we'll sort out your document. It brought up an old record, and the person in the photograph should be twenty-five years older than you."

"The document is mine."

"I understand. But family records can only be opened after identity verification."

Asgar sat on the edge of the chair.

"I can give you the address. Phone number. Where and when we got married. What else do you need?"

"First, to understand why you're using an identity card that expired a long time ago."

He lowered his eyes to the card.

He could say he had lived abroad for a long time. Lost the newer documents. That they had misunderstood him at the taxi stand.

The woman waited.

"This morning, it was the year two thousand fourteen," Asgar said. "For me."

She did not reach for the phone. She only pulled her chair closer to the desk.

"Tell me where you were this morning."

He began with the repair depot.

At first he spoke quickly, skipping around, then saw that the woman was making brief notes and forced himself back to the beginning. Friday, the parking garage, the conversation on the phone. Waiting behind the city fence. The camera. Katya, whom he had promised to call.

The stadium had to be told separately.

The woman clarified:

"The first time you went in was August sixteenth?"

Asgar raised his head.

"I haven't said the date yet."

She turned the screen toward him.

An archival record was displayed. Surname, first name. A photograph he remembered: it had been taken for documents, and Katya had later said he looked as though he had been arguing with the photographer.

Below it was the date of the first disappearance.

16.08.2014.

"This came up with your identity card," the woman said. "That's why you were sent to me."

Asgar leaned toward the screen.

Return—August twentieth. Second disappearance—August twenty-fourth, two thousand fourteen.

Asgar reread the last line. The day and month matched today.

He ran a finger along the edge of the table.

"Who recorded this?"

"These are records from the missing-person investigation. The full documents are still loading."

"Kerimov? Rauf Kerimov?"

She found the relevant field.

"Yes. He's listed in the materials."

Asgar leaned back.

Rauf had stood by the fence and raised a hand. Asgar had seen him through the doorway. Everything that happened afterward to the man by the gates had already had time to become material in an old case.

"Call him."

"We'll try. But first let's finish verification."

"He saw me. He'll confirm it."

"If we find a current contact, we'll ask him. But I still need to establish that you are the person on this record."

She spoke patiently, and the patience was beginning to make him angry.

Asgar pointed at the screen.

"That's my photograph. The dates match. I told you about the stadium, about the depot. What else is missing?"

"That you are the person on the record. Everything else could have been learned."

"Why would another person..."

He did not finish. The woman did not help him finish the question.

Asgar looked at the lower part of the record.

It said that he had been declared dead.

He read the entire line, including the reference to the court decision, then went back to the beginning.

"Did they find a body?"

"No. No body was found. The court declared you dead later."

"Who filed for it?"

The woman looked at the screen.

"That isn't shown in the short record."

"Katya?"

"I don't know."

Asgar stood up. The chair shifted and struck the wall.

He went to the water cooler, took a cup, but could not line it up under the tap at first. He had to set it on the grate. The water came out too slowly.

"I called her today," he said.

The woman stayed silent while he watched the thin stream.

"Before I went in. She told me to take my phone."

The cup overflowed. Asgar moved it away; water ran over his fingers. He drank several swallows and put the cup on the table without placing a napkin under it.

"How many years did they look for me?"

"I don't know. You can't tell that from the court date. We need to open the whole case."

He wanted to ask more. Who had gone to court. Who had collected documents. How Katya had said his name in front of strangers. What that decision had been needed for.

He looked at the clock above the door. The hands were moving. Everything here had kept moving while he stood beside the water cooler.

"What do you need for verification?"

The woman pushed a small scanner toward him.

"Fingerprints. Then a photograph. Your archive contains samples for comparison. If the match is confirmed, we'll complete an initial identification and can begin searching for relatives."

Asgar sat down.

"Let's do it."

---

The scanner had to be wiped down: his palm left a damp mark. The woman gave him a tissue, asked him to place his fingers on it again, then to look into the camera above the screen.

He complied. Sat with his hands on his knees while she compared the results.

Outside the door, someone's child demanded his bottle back. An adult voice replied that the cap was open and everything would spill. Asgar turned his head toward the sound.

The woman noticed.

"Would you like me to close it?"

"No."

She sent a request for additional verification and explained that they now needed a response from the duty specialist. Asgar asked how long it would take. She did not promise five minutes.

He placed his phone on the table.

The light reflected in the black glass. Asgar turned it on, found the photograph of the children, and held it out to the woman.

"These are them."

She looked, then carefully placed the phone between them.

"Keep the photo with you. I need their full names."

He gave them. Then Katya's name.

The woman wrote them down, repeated them to check the pronunciation. Asgar corrected one letter, and they went back to waiting. Several times he meant to ask about a charger, but each time he forgot as soon as something changed on her screen. The corridor grew quieter. The elderly woman with the folder passed the open door, holding her papers against her chest.

At last the caseworker straightened. Instead of answering, she turned the archival photograph and the new picture toward herself. Asgar could see both at an angle. In one he was neatly combed; in the other, his hair stuck up above one temple. The woman enlarged the dates, then reached toward the scanner she had already moved to the edge of the desk and stopped.

"Again?" Asgar asked.

"No. The duty specialist checked the source samples separately. The fingerprints match. The face does too."

She dialed an internal number. Asgar waited for them to begin searching for Katya, but the caseworker asked to be connected to the senior shift supervisor.

"The confirmation came through. I've opened both records. Yes, he's in front of me. — She listened to a question and looked at Asgar again. — No, it's not only the old photograph. Today's image was taken here, in front of me. Pass a message along the closed case. And I need access to the full missing-person file."

Asgar pushed the phone toward her hand.

"You said that after verification we could look for my family. I did everything. Do I have to wait for someone else now?"

She ended the call, but her fingers remained on the handset.

"No. That's enough. We're looking for your wife now. Let them deal with the case separately."

The woman opened another form. Asgar pulled the phone back toward himself. In the photograph, the younger boy was still holding his toy. The older one stood beside him, blurred because he had never been able to stay still for a picture.

Anything could have happened over those years. Until now, he had been thinking about the apartment door. About who would open it, how to explain his appearance, what to say first. He had imagined Katya older, the children grown. None of those pictures contained an empty space where someone should have been.

The woman asked him to confirm his wife's last known address.

Asgar gave it and fell silent.

She started entering it, but he covered the phone lying in front of him with his palm.

"Wait."

The caseworker looked at him.

"First tell me... Can you find out whether she's alive?"

He wanted to add the name even though he had just dictated it.

"Katya. Is she alive?"
