Chapter 20. Water at the Threshold

The water by the bank was clear.

Asgar could see flat stones, dark strands on the bottom, and his own boots when he bent to find a place to cross.

The booth stood a little downstream.

Behind its side glass, the water sat differently than it did outside, and every time Asgar tried to follow that boundary, he lost his sense of depth.

Tyra pointed to a shallow section on the right.

“The bottom is higher there. But Bertram checks first.”

The craftsman was already going down to the water with a long pole.

He tested the footing ahead of him, took several steps, and stopped without approaching the red wall.

Tyra watched a small instrument in her hand.

Asgar stood beside her with his fingers tucked beneath the edge of his jacket, waiting for them to finish.

The telephone rang.

He lifted his head.

The sound came from the booth: ordinary, metallic, nothing like the signals he had grown used to in Ar.

Bertram turned and pointed out the path he had taken.

Asgar left the jacket containing his documents and phone on the bank, rolled up his trousers, and entered the river.

At the threshold the water reached his ankles.

He opened the door carefully.

He expected a rush to pour inside, but the surface only trembled beside the red frame.

The telephone kept ringing.

Staying outside, Asgar reached in toward the apparatus on the right and lifted the receiver.

“I’m here.”

“Asgar, this is Mirena. Are you inside?”

He recognized her name before he recognized the voice.

“No. At the door. The booth is in a river.”

“Is the current strong enough to make standing difficult?”

“No. It’s shallow. People checked the approach.”

She asked him to describe the water at the threshold, then told him to wait a little.

Asgar looked at the black telephone.

On the bank, Tyra was holding his jacket.

Bertram stood beside her.

From here Asgar could see they were looking toward him, but could not make out their faces.

“You were gone a long time,” he said.

“One hundred and three days passed for you?”

“Yes.”

Her answer did not come immediately.

“The checks are complete. And there is something else. What we discussed last time is now possible: we can close the connection.

“If you want, you can stay where you are.”

Asgar shifted his foot.

The stone beneath his sole rocked slightly, and he took the door frame with his free hand.

“Permanently?”

“Permanently. We can separate you from the booth so you no longer have to leave with it.”

He looked toward the bank.

Tyra was still holding the jacket.

One sleeve hung almost to the ground. She caught it and folded it over.

“And it won’t keep appearing because of me?”

“Yes. That connection will close.”

“When do I have to decide?”

“You have most of the day.

“That’s our limit, not the booth’s. I still don’t know how long it will remain here.

“You don’t need to stand by the telephone. If you decide to stay, come back here and tell me. Before separation, we’ll check everything again.”

He waited for the rest.

Conditions.

Caveats.

Reasons to hurry.

Mirena remained silent.

“What if I just don’t come back?”

“Then the separation won’t happen.

“If you decide to stay, come back and say so. I’ll explain what to do.

“If you decide to continue, tell me that too.”

Asgar ran his thumb along the seam of the receiver.

“Would you stay?”

“I didn’t live these days in Ar.”

He looked down at the water beside his boot.

The open door was reflected in it, trembling.

“I’ll think.”

“All right. You can go back to the bank now.”

He replaced the receiver, left the door open, and followed the same shallow path back.

Bertram offered his hand on the final rise.

Asgar climbed out, unrolled his trousers, and took the jacket from Tyra.

“What did they say?”

He was still looking at his wet boots.

“I can stay.”

Tyra did not answer at once.

“Here?”

“Yes. Close the connection to the booth.”

She looked toward the river, then back at him.

“Then come on. Change your shoes.”

---

At the house, Asgar sat on the step and removed his boots.

His socks were wet around the ankles.

He wrung them out, hung them over a rail beside the door, and put the shoes in the light.

Tyra brought a clean cloth from inside for his feet and left it beside him.

“I have to go back to the water intake. I’ll come by after lunch.”

“You’re not going to ask?”

“You said you’d think.”

She stood looking at the open boots.

“I want you to stay.

“You can factor that in immediately.”

Asgar raised his head, but Tyra had already turned toward the path.

He watched her go, dried his feet, and went inside.

The fasteners for tomorrow’s work lay on the table.

The board stood by the window.

Tobin had moved it the day before so no one would catch it with an elbow.

Asgar remembered telling him not to take up the whole table and the boy answering that no one needed the whole table anyway.

Now Asgar walked between the table and the bed, sat, then stood again.

He could put his documents in the cabinet.

Finally sort his things so he did not have to dig through them every time he needed clean clothes.

Ask Bertram to look at the phone without wondering whether the booth would return before the craftsman found time.

Find out how much it would cost to get a translator of his own and stop borrowing one from the community.

Asgar went to the shelf.

The T-shirt lay separately, folded with the collar inward.

He took it out and unfolded it.

The fabric had softened after washing. A short thread stuck out at one seam.

He could not remember whether it had been there when Katya packed his things for the maintenance yard.

Back then, he had still been planning to return.

Asgar held the shirt for a long time.

Then he placed it on the bed and turned to the fasteners.

He checked them, arranged them by size, found one with a damaged edge.

Set it aside.

The job was small, but it required him to look rather than think.

When Bertram appeared in the doorway, Asgar was nearly finished.

“For tomorrow?”

“Yes. This one should probably be replaced.”

The craftsman took the fastener, ran a thumbnail along the edge, and put it separately.

“There’s another.”

He did not leave immediately.

His gaze passed over the shirt on the bed, the jacket by the door, but all he asked was:

“Should I find a second person for the morning?”

Asgar reached for the last piece.

“I don’t know yet.”

Bertram nodded.

“Tell me by evening. I can ask someone else.”

“I will.”

The craftsman took the prepared fittings.

Without them, the table immediately looked empty.

---

Arved told him about the council’s decision in the courtyard.

Asgar came for food and found him beneath the shade.

Arved was speaking with two people who had taken part in the argument after the climate-system failure.

When he noticed Asgar, he gestured for him to come over.

This time the translator was clipped to Asgar’s clothing. His own command of the language still was not enough for a conversation like this.

Access to shared installations had been restricted.

He could continue working in the workshop, but he could approach some nodes only with the person responsible for that section.

For the time being, he would also remain housed beside the outer courtyard.

“I heard you can end the connection,” Arved added.

“Mirena confirmed it.”

“If you close it and we see that this really is over, we’ll revisit the restrictions.”

“And until then?”

“You stay in the house. Work where you’re allowed. No one is asking you to leave Ar.”

Asgar looked at the people beside him.

One was staring toward the shade structure.

The other listened without looking away.

“And you’ll be able to tell the connection really closed?”

“We’ll watch our instruments and see whether anything else appears.

“We won’t have anyone else’s confirmation.”

Asgar wanted to force Arved to name a period.

Conditions.

An exact threshold after which people would stop checking him.

“I haven’t decided yet,” he said.

“When you do, tell Tyra. While the booth is still in the river, we’re keeping people away from it anyway.”

At the meal, Asgar sat in his usual place.

The man across from him asked for the salt.

Someone was talking about a delivery of parts, and Bertram stopped as he passed to correct the name of the component they needed.

Asgar only half listened.

Afterward, he carried his own dishes away.

At the wash station he had to wait while a woman scrubbed a large container without paying any attention to him.

He stood with his cup until she finished, then rinsed it and set it to dry.

When he returned home, Tyra was already waiting at the door.

---

She sat by the window.

Asgar remained standing beside the table, his hands resting on the back of a chair.

“I keep thinking I can stay,” he said. “Eat now. Sleep after. Go to work with Bertram tomorrow. And nothing happens.”

“There can still be an ordinary bad day.”

He gave a short smile.

She did not.

“I know.”

“No. I mean it. You can get tired of work here too. Argue with someone. Get sick. Arved will keep arguing with somebody.

“You don’t have to choose Ar as though everything here has to be good forever.”

Asgar sat.

Through the window he could see the place where his boots were drying.

He had positioned one badly.

A drop from the roof struck it every so often.

He noticed and kept watching instead of going out to move it.

“It isn’t about Arved.”

“I know.”

“You can’t know.”

Tyra leaned back.

“All right. Tell me.”

He ran a palm across the tabletop.

“I can live here. I already do.

“But if I close the connection, how do I ever find out whether I could have gone back?

“Who answers that for me?

“Saima finds another text, another copy. But they’ll all be traces.”

Tyra listened without interrupting.

“I keep thinking: one more transition, and there’ll finally be a person who actually knows.

“Doesn’t guess.

“Someone who can tell me what to do.”

“And if they can’t?”

“Then at least they’ll tell me themselves.”

Asgar fell silent.

Tyra looked at the empty place where the fasteners had been.

“You want to leave.”

He did not answer immediately.

“Yes.”

She nodded, her eyes lowered.

“I don’t know whether it’s the right thing,” he added.

“Neither do I.”

Asgar reached toward her hand on the edge of the table, then stopped.

Tyra looked at his hand and covered his fingers briefly with her own palm.

“I’m sorry you’re leaving,” she said. “And I don’t want to help you convince yourself there’s nothing holding you here.”

“There is.”

He said it while looking past her—

at the bed, where the unfolded T-shirt still lay.

“Then don’t tell yourself later that you had no choice.”

He closed his hand around hers.

Another drop fell outside.

Asgar stood and went to move the boot.

---

On the way to the workshop, Asgar passed the water node.

The man who had spoken at the council was kneeling beside an open housing, pulling a long bundle out of it.

He looked up when he saw Asgar, then went back to the bundle.

Asgar stopped.

“I’m leaving today.”

The man kept working.

“Heard.”

Asgar waited a little longer.

He wanted the man to stand.

Say something in farewell.

Swear, at least.

The man pulled the bundle farther out, checked a mark, and released it.

“I’ll move the panel by your house anyway,” he said. “At least now I won’t have to carry it back.”

“Probably better.”

“Better.”

He bent over the housing again.

Asgar stood there another moment, then walked on.

Bertram was in the workshop.

Asgar waited until he finished explaining something to a worker at the bench, then said he would not be there tomorrow.

The craftsman set down the part in his hand.

“You’re leaving today?”

“Yes.”

“All right. I’ll arrange someone.”

He turned toward the tool set, took out a small tool, and held it toward Asgar.

Asgar recognized the rotating head and the wear on the handle.

The same one he had used on his first latch.

“That’s yours.”

“Now it’s yours. Just don’t use it to open anything you don’t understand.”

Asgar turned the tool in his palm.

“Thank you.”

Bertram shook his hand.

Hard.

Brief.

Then held on a moment longer before letting go.

“If you change your mind, I’m bringing the second person anyway. Three is faster.”

Asgar nodded.

He left the workshop with the tool in his pocket and spent several steps unable to decide where to go.

Then he remembered the bag.

Tyra brought it herself.

Set it on the bed, opened it, and showed him the inner compartment.

“Take this one. Better for small things.”

He began packing.

The old T-shirt went in first.

Then a local change of clothes, another shirt, clean socks.

Tyra watched him trying to fit everything and removed the extra shirt.

“You’ve never worn this.”

“Might need it.”

“There’s already a spare.”

Asgar left the shirt on the shelf.

He put the tool in the inner compartment.

The phone beside it, wrapped in soft fabric.

Checked the documents and put them into the inside pocket of the jacket.

A cup stood beside the sink.

He picked it up, turned it in his hand, then rinsed it and put it back.

Tyra folded the things that would remain without speaking.

Tobin arrived when the bag was already packed.

He stopped in the doorway, looked at it, and immediately turned toward the board.

“We didn’t finish.”

“I know.”

The boy sat and adjusted one of the three pieces still in play.

“Your move.”

Asgar looked at Tyra.

She went outside and left the door open.

He sat opposite Tobin.

They made several moves in silence.

Asgar tried to remember what he had intended the day before, but all he could see were the boy’s fingers closing around the piece.

When he made a mistake, Tobin pointed to the open square he could have used.

“You don’t usually help me.”

The boy shrugged.

Asgar brought his hand back toward the board.

“Let’s play like usual.”

“Usually you come back tomorrow.”

He had no answer.

They finished the game.

Tobin won, gathered two pieces, and left the third—the one with the flat side—in front of Asgar.

“Take this one.”

“Then you won’t have enough.”

“I’ll make another.”

Asgar picked it up.

A small notch remained on the uneven back, the mark by which he always recognized it among the others.

“I’ll remember I owe you another game.”

Tobin frowned.

“You lost this one. You don’t owe anything for it.”

Asgar nodded.

“Then a new one.”

The boy did not answer.

He stood, took the board and the other two pieces.

At the door he turned back.

“I’m going to the river too.”

After he left, Asgar put the piece into the inner compartment of the bag beside the tool.

---

By the time they left the house, the light had softened.

Asgar locked the door and automatically tested the handle.

Tyra stood beside him.

He almost asked who he should give the house back to, then remembered it had never belonged to him and she already knew what to do.

“Everything on the shelf is clean,” he said.

“All right.”

“And by the window…”

“I saw.”

He fell silent.

They walked slowly to the river.

Tobin kept running ahead and then coming back.

Bertram caught up with them at the turn.

There was no tool in his hand anymore.

Asgar noticed and adjusted the bag strap on his shoulder.

People were still watching the area from the bank.

The booth stood where it had been.

Water flowed around the red wall, and the black telephone remained visible through the glass.

Asgar set the bag on a dry rock.

“I’ll talk first.”

Tyra nodded.

He followed the shallow route.

The door was still open.

At the threshold, cold water seeped through his boots.

Asgar lifted the receiver without going inside.

“Mirena.”

“I hear you.”

“I decided to continue.”

She asked him to wait while she checked.

Asgar looked toward the bank.

Now people he knew were standing there:

Bertram with his hands at his belt.

Tobin crouched beside the bag.

Tyra watching him.

“Checked. You can still stay or continue. Separation remains available.”

“I understand.

“I’m continuing.”

“Then bring your things. Once you’re inside, stay on the line until the door closes.”

He replaced the receiver and went back.

Tobin rose from the rock.

Asgar crouched in front of him and hugged him.

The boy stood still at first, then wrapped both arms tightly around Asgar’s neck.

When he let go, Asgar could not stand up immediately.

Bertram shook his hand once more.

Tyra came last.

He hugged her, pulled her close, and felt her exhale against his shoulder.

“I’ll miss you,” he said.

“I already do.”

He wanted to add something about coming back.

He said nothing.

Tyra stepped away, straightened the twisted strap on his shoulder, and let go.

Asgar took the bag.

He walked to the door looking down at his feet.

Crossed the threshold, placed the bag between his boots, and lifted the receiver.

Inside, the booth felt smaller than he remembered.

“I’m in.”

“I hear you. Door still open?”

“Yes.”

He turned toward the bank.

Tobin stood beside Tyra.

Bertram a little behind.

Asgar raised his free hand.

The boy answered at once.

Then Asgar took the handle.

The door moved heavily.

He pulled it toward himself, careful not to catch the bag.

The opening narrowed.

For a moment it still held the water, the pale bank, and part of Tyra’s figure.

“Closing,” he said.

He had to press the final centimeters with his shoulder.

The lock clicked.

Asgar stood with the receiver to his ear and one hand on the handle until the floor shifted beneath him.

The sound of the river vanished.

He kept his balance with a palm against the door.

The bag slid and touched his boot.

He remembered that tomorrow Bertram would still go to the lower passage—

just with someone else.
