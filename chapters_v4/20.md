Chapter 20. Water at the Threshold

The water by the bank was clear. Asgar could see flat stones, dark strands on the bottom, and his own boots when he leaned down to see where he could cross. The booth stood a little downstream. Behind its side glass, the water held at a different level than outside, and every time he tried to follow that boundary he lost his sense of depth.

Taira pointed to a gently sloping section on the right.

"The bottom is higher there. But Bertram will check first."

The craftsman was already going down to the water with a long pole. He tested the footing ahead of him, took several steps, and stopped without approaching the red wall. Taira watched a small device in her hand. Asgar stood beside her with his fingers tucked under the edge of his jacket and waited for them to finish.

The telephone rang.

He raised his head. The sound came from the booth, ordinary and metallic, completely unlike the signals he had grown used to in Ar. Bertram turned and showed where he had walked.

Asgar left the jacket with his documents and phone on the bank, rolled up his trouser legs, and stepped into the river. At the threshold, the water reached his ankles. He carefully opened the door. He expected water to rush inside, but the surface only trembled beside the red frame.

The ringing continued.

Remaining outside, Asgar reached to the telephone on the right and lifted the receiver.

"I'm here."

"Asgar, it's Mirena. Are you inside?"

He heard her name before he had time to recognize the voice.

"No. At the door. The booth is in the river."

"Is there a current making it hard to stand?"

"No. It's shallow. People checked the approach."

She asked him to describe the water at the threshold, then to wait a little. Asgar looked at the black telephone. On the bank, Taira was holding his jacket, Bertram standing beside her. From here he could see that they were looking his way, but he could not make out their faces.

"You took a long time to appear," he said.

"One hundred three days passed for you?"

"Yes."

The answer did not come immediately.

"The check is finished. And there's one more thing. What we discussed last time—we can do it now: the connection can be closed. If you want, you can stay here."

Asgar shifted his foot. The stone beneath his sole rocked slightly, and he gripped the door frame with his free hand.

"Completely?"

"Completely. We can separate you from the cabin so you won't have to leave with it anymore."

He looked at the bank.

Taira was still holding the jacket. One sleeve hung almost to the ground; she caught it and folded it in half.

"And it won't appear because of me anymore?"

"Yes. This connection will close."

"When do I have to decide?"

"Almost the whole day. That's our deadline, not the cabin's: I still don't know how long it will remain. You don't need to stand by the phone. If you decide to stay, come back here—and before the separation itself we'll check everything once more."

He waited for more: conditions, qualifications, reasons to hurry. Mirena was silent.

"What if I simply don't come back?"

"Then there will be no separation. If you decide to stay, come back and tell me. I'll explain what to do. If you decide to continue, tell me that too."

Asgar ran his thumb along the receiver seam.

"Would you stay?"

"I haven't lived these days in Ar."

He lowered his eyes to the water by his boot. The reflection of the open door moved in it.

"I'll think."

"All right. You can return to the bank now."

He hung up, left the door open, and went back along the same shallow section. Bertram offered a hand on the final climb. Asgar got out, unrolled his trousers, and took the jacket from Taira.

"What did they say?"

He was still looking at his wet boots.

"I can stay."

Taira did not answer immediately.

"Here?"

"Yes. Close the connection to the booth."

She looked toward the river, then back at him.

"Then let's go. Change your shoes."

---

At the house, Asgar sat on the step and took off his boots.

His socks were wet around the ankles. He wrung them out, hung them on a rail by the door, and set the shoes in the light. Taira brought a clean cloth for his feet from the house and left it beside him.

"I have to go back to the water intake. I'll come by after lunch."

"You aren't going to ask?"

"You said you'd think."

She stood there looking at the open boots.

"I want you to stay. You can take that into account right away."

Asgar raised his head, but Taira had already turned toward the path. He watched her go, dried his feet, and went inside.

The fasteners for tomorrow's work lay on the table. The board stood by the window. Tobin had moved it the day before so no one would accidentally hit it with an elbow. Asgar remembered telling him not to take up the entire table, and the boy replying that the whole table wasn't needed anyway.

Now he walked between the table and the bed, sat down, then stood again.

He could put the documents in the cupboard. Finally sort his things so he did not have to go through all of them every time he looked for clean clothes. Ask Bertram to look at the phone—without thinking the booth might return before the craftsman was free. Find out how much it would cost to make his own translator and stop borrowing the community one.

Asgar went to the shelf.

The T-shirt lay separately, folded collar inward. He pulled it out and unfolded it. The fabric had grown softer after washing; a short thread stuck out from one seam. He could not remember whether it had been there when Katya passed the clothes to the repair depot.

Back then, he had still been planning to return.

Asgar held the T-shirt for a long time. Then put it on the bed and turned to the fasteners.

He checked them, sorted them by size, found one with a damaged edge. Set it aside. The work was small, but it required looking rather than thinking. By the time Bertram looked into the doorway, Asgar was already finishing.

"These are for tomorrow?"

"Yes. This one should be replaced."

The craftsman took the fastener, ran a fingernail along the edge, and put it aside.

"There's another."

He did not leave immediately. He looked at the T-shirt on the bed, the jacket by the door, but only asked:

"Should I find a second person for the morning?"

Asgar reached for the last part.

"I don't know yet."

Bertram nodded.

"Tell me by evening. I can call someone else."

"I will."

The craftsman took the prepared pieces. Without them, the table immediately looked empty.

---

Arved announced the council's decision in the courtyard.

Asgar came for food and saw him by the canopy. Arved was talking to two people who had been present during the argument after the climate failure. When he noticed Asgar, he gestured for him to come over. This time the translator was attached to Asgar's clothing; his own grasp of the language was still not enough for a conversation like this.

Access to the shared installations had been restricted. His work in the workshop remained, but he could approach some nodes only with the person responsible for that section. His placement by the outer courtyard would also remain for the near future.

"I was told you can end the connection," Arved added.

"Mirena confirmed it."

"If you close it and we see that everything truly ended, we'll revisit the restrictions."

"And before that?"

"You stay in the house. Work where you're allowed. No one is asking you to leave Ar."

Asgar looked at the people nearby. One looked toward the canopy; the other listened without taking his eyes off him.

"And you'll be able to tell that the connection is really closed?"

"We'll see what our instruments show and whether anything else appears. We won't have outside confirmation."

He wanted to force Arved to name a deadline, conditions, a precise boundary after which they would stop checking him.

"I haven't decided yet," Asgar answered.

"Once you do, tell Taira. While the cabin is by the river, we're keeping people away anyway."

At the meal, Asgar sat in his usual place. The person opposite asked him to pass the salt. Someone discussed a parts delivery, and Bertram, walking past, stopped to correct the name of the needed piece. Asgar listened only half-heartedly.

After lunch, he carried his own dishes away.

At the washing area he had to wait: a woman was scrubbing a large container without pausing for him. He stood with his cup until she finished, then rinsed it and set it to dry.

When he returned home, Taira was already waiting by the door.

---

She sat by the window. Asgar remained standing at the table with his hands on the back of the chair.

"I keep thinking I can stay," he said. "Eat now, sleep afterward. Go with Bertram tomorrow. And nothing will happen."

"An ordinary bad day might happen."

He gave a short laugh, but she did not smile.

"I understand."

"No, I'm serious. You can get tired of work here too, argue, get sick. Arved will argue with someone again. You don't have to choose Ar as though everything here must always be good."

Asgar sat down.

From the window he could see the place where his boots were drying. He had positioned one badly: a drop fell on it from the roof from time to time. He noticed and kept looking instead of going out to move it.

"This isn't about Arved."

"I know."

"You can't know."

Taira leaned back.

"All right. Tell me."

He ran a palm across the table.

"I can live here. I already do. But if I close the connection, how will I ever find out whether I could have gone back? Who will answer me? Saima will find another text, another copy. But they'll all be traces."

Taira listened without interrupting.

"I keep thinking: one more transition, and finally there'll be someone who really knows. Doesn't assume. Someone who can tell me what to do."

"And if they can't?"

"Then at least they'll say so themselves."

Asgar fell silent.

Taira looked at the empty place where the fasteners had been.

"You want to leave."

He did not answer immediately.

"Yes."

She nodded, lowering her eyes.

"I don't know whether it's right," he added.

"Neither do I."

Asgar reached toward her hand lying at the edge of the table, then stopped. Taira looked at his hand and covered his fingers with her palm for a moment herself.

"I'm sorry you're leaving," she said. "And I don't want to help you convince yourself there's nothing holding you here."

"There is."

He said it while looking past her—at the bed where the unfolded T-shirt still lay.

"Then don't say later that there was no choice."

He squeezed her hand.

Outside, another drop fell. Asgar stood and went to move the boot.

---

On the way to the workshop, Asgar passed the water node.

The man who had spoken at the council was kneeling beside the open cover, pulling a long bundle from it. He raised his head, saw Asgar, and returned to the bundle.

Asgar stopped.

"I'm leaving today."

The man did not stop working.

"Heard."

Asgar waited a little longer. He wanted the man to stand up, or say something in farewell, or at least curse. The man pulled the bundle farther out, looked at a mark, and released it.

"I'll move the panel by your house anyway," he said. "At least now I won't have to carry it back."

"Probably better that way."

"Better."

He lowered his head toward the cover. Asgar stood there, then walked on.

He found Bertram in the workshop.

Asgar waited until he finished explaining something to a worker at the bench, then said he would not come tomorrow.

The craftsman put down the part.

"Leaving today?"

"Yes."

"All right. I'll arrange it."

He turned toward the set, took out a small tool, and held it out to Asgar. Asgar recognized the rotating head, the worn spot on the handle—the same one he had used to take apart his first latch.

"That's yours."

"Now it's yours. Just don't try opening anything with it that you don't understand."

Asgar turned the tool over in his palm.

"Thank you."

Bertram shook his hand. Hard, brief. Then held it a moment longer.

"If you change your mind, I'll bring a second person anyway. Three is faster."

Asgar nodded.

He left the workshop with the tool in his pocket and for several steps could not decide where to go. Then he remembered the bag.

Taira brought it herself. Set it on the bed, opened it, and showed him the inner compartment.

"Take this one. It's better for carrying small things."

He began packing clothes. The old T-shirt went in first. Then a local change, another shirt, clean socks. Taira watched him try to fit everything at once, pulled out the extra shirt, and put it on the shelf.

"You never wore this one."

"Might need it."

"There's already a change."

Asgar left the shirt on the shelf. He put the tool in the inner compartment. The phone went beside it, wrapped in soft cloth. He checked the documents and put them in the inner pocket of his jacket.

The cup stood by the washbasin. He picked it up, turned it over in his hands, then rinsed it and returned it to its place.

Taira folded the remaining things in silence.

Tobin arrived when the bag was already packed.

He stopped in the doorway, looked at it, and immediately turned toward the board.

"We didn't finish."

"I know."

The boy sat and adjusted one of the three remaining pieces.

"Your move."

Asgar looked at Taira. She left, keeping the door open.

He sat opposite Tobin.

They made several moves in silence. Asgar tried to remember what he had planned yesterday, but all he could see were the boy's fingers gripping the piece. When he made a mistake, Tobin pointed to an open space he could have used.

"You usually don't help me."

The boy shrugged.

Asgar brought his hand back to the board.

"Let's play like always."

"Like always, you'll come again tomorrow."

He found no answer.

They finished the game. Tobin won, collected two pieces, and left the third, the one with the flat side, in front of Asgar.

"Take this one."

"Then you won't have enough."

"I'll make another."

Asgar picked up the piece. A small notch remained on the uneven back, the mark by which he recognized it among the others.

"I'll remember I still owe you a game."

Tobin frowned.

"You lost this one. You don't owe for it."

Asgar nodded.

"Then a new one."

The boy said nothing. He stood, took the board and the two pieces. At the door he turned around.

"I'm going to the river too."

When he left, Asgar placed the piece in the inner compartment of the bag beside the tool.

---

When they left the house, the light had already softened.

Asgar closed the door and automatically checked the handle. Taira stood beside him. He wanted to ask who to give the house to, then remembered the house did not belong to him and she knew what to do.

"Everything on the shelf is clean," he said.

"All right."

"And by the window..."

"I saw."

He fell silent.

They walked slowly to the river. Tobin would run ahead, then come back. Bertram caught up with them at the turn. He no longer had a tool in his hands; Asgar noticed and adjusted the bag strap on his shoulder.

People remained on the bank watching the area. The booth stood where it had been. Water bent around its red wall, and the black telephone was still visible through the glass.

Asgar put the bag on a dry stone.

"I'll talk first."

Taira nodded.

He crossed at the shallow place. The door remained open. At the threshold, water chilled his feet through the boots. Asgar lifted the receiver without entering.

"Mirena."

"I hear you."

"I decided to continue."

She asked him to wait for the check. He looked at the bank. Now people he knew stood there: Bertram with his hands on his hips, Tobin crouched beside the bag, Taira looking at him.

"Checked. Both staying and continuing are possible. The separation option is still available."

"I understand. I'll continue."

"Then collect your things. Once you're inside, stay on the line until the door closes."

He hung up and returned.

Tobin got up from the stone. Asgar crouched in front of him and hugged him. At first the boy stood motionless, then wrapped his arms tightly around his neck.

When he let go, Asgar could not stand immediately.

Bertram shook his hand once more. Taira came last. He embraced her, pulled her close, and felt her exhale into his shoulder.

"I'll miss you," he said.

"I already do."

He wanted to add something about returning. He kept silent.

Taira stepped back, straightened the twisted strap on his shoulder, and let go.

Asgar took the bag.

He walked to the door looking at his feet. Crossed the threshold, set the bag between his boots, and lifted the receiver. Inside, it was tighter than he remembered.

"I'm inside."

"I hear you. Is the door still open?"

"Yes."

He turned toward the bank. Tobin stood beside Taira, Bertram slightly behind. Asgar raised his free hand. The boy answered immediately.

Then Asgar took the handle.

The door moved heavily. He pulled it toward himself, trying not to hit the bag. The opening narrowed; water, the pale bank, and part of Taira's figure remained in it.

"Closing," he said.

He had to push the last few centimeters with his shoulder.

The lock clicked. Asgar stood with the receiver to his ear and his hand on the handle until the floor trembled beneath his feet.

The sound of the river vanished.

He held himself steady with his palm against the door. The bag shifted and touched his boot.

He remembered that tomorrow Bertram would still come to the lower passage—with someone else.
