Chapter 21. Sky Underfoot

His sole slipped on the wet floor.

Asgar caught himself on the door handle and nudged the bag closer with his boot so it would not get in the way.

Something clicked in the receiver.

He pressed it to his ear, expecting Mirena, but light had already appeared beyond the glass, and the words he had been about to say vanished from his mind.

At first he saw white bands on blue.

They filled almost the entire lower window.

Higher up ran a curved edge, faintly outlined in blue. Beyond it was blackness.

Asgar raised his head, looking for the wall that might be displaying the image.

There was no wall.

Instead he saw a long pale surface covered with rows of structures. It stretched away, rose, and continued into the place where the sky ought to have been.

He stepped back until his shoulders touched the wall beside the telephone.

“Mirena.”

“I hear you. What happened?”

Asgar opened his mouth, but for a moment all he could do was breathe.

It felt as though the floor sloped toward the glass.

The bag stood level.

The water by his boot did not run toward the window.

He still pressed his back harder against the wall.

“Earth is out there.”

“You can see the planet?”

“Below me. To the side… I don’t know. I don’t know where down is here.”

He looked at his feet, then outside again.

Between the booth and the edge of the passage lay a solid surface.

Beyond that began a transparent barrier, and behind it the blue surface curved away beneath him.

Asgar bent his knees as though that might make him more stable.

“Don’t open the door yet,” Mirena said. “We’re checking the external environment.”

The white bands moved slowly.

Or the place he was standing moved around them.

Asgar tried to choose one point and hold his eyes on it.

That made it worse.

He turned toward the telephone.

Black housing.

Round metal dial.

A screw near the lower edge.

At least those things stayed where they were.

“Are we in space?”

“The booth is inside some kind of artificial structure. There’s air outside. Pressure is normal. We’re still checking everything else.”

“What about the floor?”

“Down is stable here. Do you feel yourself being pulled in any direction?”

“No.”

He relaxed his grip on the handle.

Wet marks remained on the metal.

Mirena asked him to describe what he could see directly in front of the threshold.

Asgar forced himself to turn back:

surface.

Lines along the passage.

Transparent wall on the right.

Far to the left, a figure was moving.

The person stopped, raised a hand toward something near their face, then went back the way they had come.

“The area in front of the door is clear,” Asgar said. “But I don’t want to go out there.”

“We can wait a little.”

He stood until his breathing became more even.

His socks were still wet from the river.

Asgar concentrated on the cold around his toes, the weight of the jacket, the bag between his boots.

Everything he had entered with was still here.

“What year?”

Mirena gave him the date.

February 4, 87,116.

Asgar looked at the blue rim of the planet.

The number barely stayed in his mind.

All he could think about was the fact that beyond the glass there was no ground at all to step onto.

---

When Mirena confirmed that exiting was permissible, he opened the door a little.

Nothing rushed outward.

A faint stream of cool air touched his face.

Asgar stood holding the handle for a moment, then opened the panel wider and cautiously put one foot out.

The surface was solid.

Slightly rough.

He shifted his weight onto it.

The floor held.

His eyes did not care.

The far end of the passage curved upward, and rows of pale structures climbed toward the place where the sky should have been.

Asgar took the second step the way a person walks on ice.

The air smelled of nothing.

No water.

No soil.

No smoke.

Nothing that told him what kind of place he had entered.

Somewhere nearby, something enormous was operating with a steady sound that seemed to come from every direction at once.

Signs lit above the passage.

He stared at them without recognizing a single one.

A sentence sounded outside.

Asgar raised his head.

The voice came from somewhere overhead.

No person was nearby.

“What are they saying?”

“I can’t hear it clearly,” Mirena said. “Local system?”

“Probably.”

The sentence repeated.

Ahead, near the turn, a narrow line of light appeared.

Asgar stepped closer, trying to see where it led, and the receiver pulled his arm backward.

He stopped.

The cord stretched from the telephone through the open doorway.

The light was only a few steps away, but he could go no farther.

Asgar returned to the threshold and freed the cord from the door edge.

“I’m putting the receiver down.”

“All right. If you need the link, return to the telephone.”

He hung up, lifted the bag, and stepped out.

He did not linger beside the transparent wall, but once he had to look.

Below the level of the floor stretched a white band, and in a break between clouds, water glittered.

He had seen something like that once in his life, from an airplane.

Back then there had been an aircraft floor beneath him.

And two hours later, ground.

Asgar fixed his eyes on the walkway and kept close to the opaque wall.

The overhead voice spoke again.

He answered in Russian.

“I don’t understand you. I need translation.”

A pause.

He repeated himself more slowly, then tried English.

The signs above the passage changed.

A line in Latin letters appeared among them, but he did not have time to make sense of it.

It disappeared and was replaced by Russian text.

*Historical language model. Confirm comprehension.*

Asgar read it aloud.

The next question came in Russian.

The system separately offered spoken translation and translation of local signage.

He accepted both and asked it not to display several messages at once.

Labels appeared beside the signs at the turn.

The passage was temporarily closed because of a spatial discrepancy.

The words were new.

The thing behind them was not.

Asgar turned.

The booth stood on the surface behind him:

red, cramped, door wide open.

A line of light surrounded it.

Farther out was another.

He had already crossed the inner one, but not the second.

“Where am I supposed to go?” he asked.

The system directed him to an open area by the wall to the left.

He found it by its new label and set the bag down.

---

Ilar came from the opposite end of the passage.

He stopped at the light boundary, read the message, and raised his head sharply.

He addressed the local system.

Asgar heard the translation:

Ilar needed access to the neighboring sector, where he was expected for a mounting inspection, and the alternate route would take longer.

The system suggested another path.

Ilar listened, looked at the booth, and only then noticed Asgar.

“Do you know how long this will last?”

“No. It appeared with me.”

The man looked back toward the red booth.

For a while he said nothing.

“What channel did you arrive through?”

“That.”

“I can see that. I mean the prepared channel.”

Asgar shook his head.

Ilar studied the booth longer this time.

“And where are you from?”

“In time? Very far away.”

“How far?”

Asgar gave him the year he had first left.

Ilar did not ask him to repeat it.

He looked from Asgar to the red door and back, then finally turned toward his own device.

Someone was still waiting for him at the inspection.

He requested something from the system.

Only part of the reply was translated for Asgar, enough to indicate restricted access.

Ilar did not move closer.

He dictated a short message to the air.

Asgar caught fragments in translation:

passage number.

“no channel.”

“person on site.”

“Then I’ll wait here. I’m Ilar.”

Asgar gave his name.

At close range, Ilar’s face looked ordinary.

No metallic skin.

No luminous eyes.

Nothing Asgar could connect to the place around them.

Only the clothing sat strangely evenly on his body, and his fingers were longer than Asgar expected.

Ilar flexed one hand and took the wrist with the other.

“The grip still feels wrong. This body was made for this work, but I keep picking things up the old way.”

“They replaced your hand?”

“My whole body. Adapted for the local gravity. I’ll work for a few days, then I won’t need it anymore.”

Asgar looked at his face.

“And where do you go?”

“Into another body.”

Ilar said it while checking another message about his route.

Asgar waited for some qualification.

None came.

“So they move you?”

“If you want the crude version, yes. But ask someone else for the theory. I inspect mountings.”

“And this body?”

“Recycled.”

Asgar looked down at his hands.

One rested against his thigh.

The other still touched his wrist.

Ilar was talking about what would be done to his body in a few days.

“And that doesn’t frighten you?”

Ilar finally dismissed the message.

“Changing bodies isn’t what frightens me. Breaking somewhere in between does.

“That’s why I only use systems I trust.”

“How do you know the one who comes out is still you?”

“How would you like to test that?”

Asgar began answering and got lost halfway through.

Memory could be copied.

The person who woke up might believe he was the same person.

But the one who had existed before…

Ilar did not interrupt.

“There are people who once continued from the same version of me,” he said. “At that point our memories were shared. Then they diverged. Now each of us has a separate life.”

“Copies?”

“People.”

Asgar fell silent.

The planet was still visible beyond the barrier.

Suddenly he became acutely aware of his own forehead.

The thin scar above his eyebrow.

The wetness inside his boots.

The body he had never once left and that now seemed like the only thing whose origin he knew anything about.

“Sorry,” he said. “I don’t know what to call that.”

Ilar nodded and left it there.

When the closure was lifted, the label over that section changed.

The system told Asgar to move away: the restriction around the booth would remain, and waiting was now required beyond its boundary.

“What about it?”

Asgar looked back at the booth.

“No one will touch it,” Ilar said. “No one will even approach until they understand what’s happening.”

“Will I be allowed back?”

“Not my decision. Ask whoever comes to register you.”

Ilar pointed down the passage.

“Second turn. There’s a room off to the side. People wait there when this passage closes. It’s empty now.”

He left for his inspection.

Asgar picked up the bag.

Labels lit ahead of him one by one and went dark behind him.

He counted the turns anyway.

He wanted to know the way back.

---

The room was small.

Several chairs lined the wall.

A table.

An opening with no door.

Someone had left a clamp on the table—apparently the closure had interrupted someone else too.

The label beside the entrance changed while Asgar watched.

The translation offered a new phrase:

*temporary registration.*

Asgar had not yet sat down when a woman entered behind him.

She stopped several steps away and did not come closer.

She said there was a person in the closed sector whose arrival had no record, and the record needed to be created now.

“Your name.”

He gave it.

The woman repeated it aloud and checked the ending.

“Where did you come from?”

“Earth. From a river.”

“When?”

Asgar hesitated and gave the year according to the place he had left that morning.

The woman did not respond.

She was looking slightly away from him, toward whatever interface was recording her notes.

“Please repeat that.”

He did.

“That is the year count used at the place you departed from?”

“Yes. I lived there.”

She repeated the number herself, slowly, breaking it into sections, then said something aside—not to him.

Only a request to recheck the interpretation of the number reached him in translation.

“We’re using a historical language model,” she said. “It sometimes maps year counts incorrectly. I’m ruling out my own error first.”

Then she asked the system to display the number as it would have been written where Asgar came from.

Familiar digits appeared above the table.

“Is this the number?”

“Yes.”

The woman looked at it longer than she had looked at anything else in the room.

Her voice remained unchanged, but she did not ask the next question immediately.

“Is the red booth in the passage yours?”

“I travel in it. I don’t know who it belongs to.”

The rest of the questions came quickly.

Asgar answered them just as briefly.

He had entered it somewhere else.

It transported him.

He had already emerged in other times.

He did not choose the date and did not know it in advance.

No one he had ever met controlled it.

The woman paused.

Asgar understood she was marking something in her record.

“I’m entering those as your statements,” she said. “Not as established facts. Do you understand the difference?”

“Yes.”

“If you remember something differently later, tell us. We’ll correct the record.”

Then she returned to his arrival and explained what transitions normally required here:

a request.

a window.

agreement at both ends.

someone receiving the arrival.

None of that existed in his case.

No one had been expecting him.

“The sector log is empty for this interval,” she said, almost to herself. “No freight arrival. No personal arrival.”

Then she explained where he could go.

The residential sector and ordinary passages were open.

Access to the object required coordination with the people working there.

The boundary would identify itself; he did not need to ask separately every time.

“Are you keeping me here?”

“You’re not being detained anywhere. But until your arrival is explained, we need to know where you are.”

She reached the end of the record and looked up.

“Do you need anything? Water, dry clothes, a doctor?”

“Water.”

She marked it.

“The restriction around the booth is staying in place for now,” she added from the doorway. “The instruments disagree with one another there.

“That is no longer your account. That part is measured.”

“Is that bad?”

“It’s unclear.

“Usually something like that is resolved on site within an hour.

“This one wasn’t.”

She lingered as though deciding how much he needed to know now.

“They called Tessa. She works in transport geometry.

“I’m not saying your case belongs to her field. Only that everything else has already been tried.

“She’ll need what you saw yourself, not your conclusions.”

The woman left.

Asgar sat beside the opaque wall.

They had offered him a place with a view of the planet.

He had asked for another.

The bag stood between his feet.

He opened it, took out dry socks, then closed it again without changing in someone else’s waiting room.

Water arrived in a recess beside the table almost immediately.

The cup felt warm to the touch though the water inside was cold.

Asgar drank and remained seated with it in his hands.

That morning he had been walking along a river and thinking that tomorrow Bertram would go to the lower passage anyway—only with somebody else.

That “tomorrow” had happened so long ago here that counting was meaningless.

And not only Bertram.

All of Ar.

Everyone who had worked there, eaten there, argued there had lived out their lives.

Then as much time again had passed.

And again.

Asgar looked at his boots.

The water inside his socks came from that river.

It had not even had time to dry.

Tessa arrived later.

She introduced herself and said she had been given a short record:

person in closed sector.

arrival without channel.

object nearby.

previous transitions according to his own account.

And the first measurements.

“I read it,” she added. “Now I need to work out which parts I can actually verify.”

“And which parts can’t you?”

“Almost all of them.

“That you arrived without a channel is visible in the log. That is no longer only your statement.

“That the booth moves you through time—I have no way to verify that.

“For now I’m keeping it in mind.”

“So you don’t care where I came from.”

“No.”

She said it without looking away.

For the first time Asgar noticed she was studying him longer than was necessary merely to listen.

“If your record is confirmed, more than one piece of my work will need to be reconsidered,” she said more slowly.

“But I still can’t start there.

“There is nothing in your story I can work with yet.”

She asked whether he was willing to come with her to the site.

“What’s there?”

“Two systems show the same wall differently. I was called to find exactly where their measurements diverge.”

She paused.

“So far I can’t.”

Asgar lowered the cup onto his knee.

“I’ve seen that.”

Tessa did not answer immediately.

“Where?”

“Not a wall. A shutter in a passage. One system was closing a section that another one considered open.”

He paused.

“By my count, that was 2191.”

She gave him the same look the first woman had given the date and said nothing about the year.

“How did it end?”

“They physically stopped the drive and got the people out.”

“So it didn’t end.”

“No.”

Asgar waited.

Tessa watched him in silence.

“It can cut things,” he said. “At the beginning it sliced off part of a metal leg. A camera tripod. The person setting it up had already moved away.”

“You saw that yourself?”

“I did. I was told not to touch it, and I didn’t.”

He paused.

“That’s why I left my people then.”

“Then you’ll tell me that again at the booth, separately.”

She did not soften what followed.

“I can’t tell you whether it’s dangerous now.

“That is why the restriction exists.

“We don’t know how large the region behaving incorrectly is, so the boundary was set conservatively.”

Tessa looked toward the transparent part of the wall.

“This is your first time off a planet’s surface?”

“Yes.”

She altered their route slightly.

They used an interior passage from which the blackness could not be seen.

After several steps, Asgar stopped pressing himself against the opaque wall.

Once the black disappeared from view, his body stopped expecting to fall.

At the site, he saw the booth again.

The door remained open.

Nothing had been connected to it.

People and equipment were all beyond the marked boundary.

“Can I approach it?” Asgar asked.

“You can. You were already inside it.

“Just tell me before you do. I need to know who went near it and when.”

Tessa showed him two images of the same wall.

In one, the red surface followed a slightly different line from the other.

Asgar recognized the problem before he had worked out exactly what was different.

“You checked the instruments?” he asked anyway.

“First thing.

“The instruments, the people operating them, and a second installation.

“The discrepancy remained.”

Asgar waited for her to name the next cause.

In the hall in 2191 they had started with their instruments too.

“My list ends there,” Tessa said. “Usually by this point we know where the error is.

“Here we don’t.

“So we’re changing the way we look.”

“They aren’t sending anything into it?”

“Right now we only listen to what arrives on its own.

“We aren’t illuminating it with anything.”

Asgar nodded.

In the ruins, it had taken them longer to reach that idea.

Tessa asked him to recount both earlier cases from the beginning.

He told her about the hall in 2191.

Then about the machines outside.

When he reached the external field, he stopped himself.

“That part was told to me by the woman on the telephone.

“I only saw the result after the scanning stopped.”

Tessa separated one record from the other.

“We’ll keep it that way.”

“Can you talk to her?”

“If we need to, we’ll establish a separate channel.

“For now your account and our own measurements are enough.”

Asgar looked back toward the passage.

Someone was coming around the turn.

Labels lit one by one ahead of the person just as they had several hours earlier for Asgar.

“The man I spoke to said he’ll be moved into another body in a few days,” Asgar said. “All of him.”

“He will.”

“Then how can you not know what this is?”

He pointed at the booth.

“You can do that.”

Tessa did not answer immediately.

“We can move an object from here to the neighboring sector,” she said. “But first we build a channel.

“From both sides.

“Both endpoints exist.

“Both are known.

“Both are agreed.

“Your booth arrived where no one built anything.

“Not at either end.”

“It always arrives that way.”

“I believe you’ve seen that.

“Nothing here arrives that way.”

Asgar fell silent.

“What about backward?” he asked. “A person. Back into their own time.”

“No.”

She answered immediately, in the same tone she had used when talking about instruments.

“There are regions where time passes more slowly than it does nearby.

“You can arrive later than everyone else without personally living the full interval.

“Forward, by a little, and only where we built the system ourselves.

“Backward—no one can do that.

“At least, I have never heard of anyone who can.”

Asgar stared past her toward the red wall.

“So you don’t know what this is either.”

“I don’t.

“That’s why I’m standing here with two instruments that disagree with each other.”

She unfolded an image above the boundary.

Not a photograph.

Something alive.

A section of passage.

The booth inside it.

A grid around the structure, changing spacing where the readings diverged.

“We’re good at calculation,” Tessa said.

“This is a model.

“I put in what we measured. It reconstructs the rest.

“Without the booth, it resolves the sector completely.

“With the booth, it doesn’t.”

Asgar watched the grid quiver beside the red wall.

The instability did not extend far.

At roughly the same ring around the booth, the spacing became regular again and the model settled.

“Where does it stop diverging?”

Tessa touched the scale beside the ring.

“Eleven point eight here.

“A little more than twelve on the opposite side.

“It drifts, but returns to about the same distance.”

Asgar looked at the red shell, then back at the ring.

“So you’ll calculate the rest.”

“The model calculates from what we give it.

“So far we’ve given it very little.”

Tessa removed the image.

“And almost everything that arrived here with the booth is you.”

“Me?”

“You came with it and you keep moving around it.

“I need to separate what changes when you move from what changes on its own.

“Otherwise I can’t separate you in the model.”

Asgar looked at the open door.

He wanted to hear Mirena, though he had nothing to say to her.

---

They assigned him a small room near the closed sector.

He could sleep there, wash, leave his belongings.

Local translation worked through a device they provided.

They showed him how to call for help and where Tessa’s messages would appear.

The first evening he could not fall asleep for a long time.

Whenever he closed his eyes, the pale surface of the ring curved upward again.

It felt as though the bed was slowly rotating.

The other side of the wall was quiet.

Asgar caught himself listening for weather.

Rain.

Wind.

Anything that happened outside.

There was no outside around this place.

Several times he sat up, tested the floor with his feet, and looked at the bag.

Tobin’s piece lay inside between the clothes and the wrapped phone.

Asgar took it out and stood it on the table.

Because its base was uneven, it rocked slightly on the smooth surface.

He turned it until it sat more steadily and went back to bed.

The next day Tessa asked him to walk several times through a marked area near the booth.

Then to stand at different points.

There were nine marks.

He stood on each one, waited until someone beyond the boundary told him he could move, then went to the next.

By midday he had begun counting in his head so he would know how much standing remained.

Two other people worked beyond the boundary.

They did not look at him or speak.

Once, the woman in pale clothing asked him to turn his shoulders slightly.

That was all anyone said to him for a long time.

The floor beneath his boots was level and cool.

The red wall stayed three steps away.

Asgar watched it because looking at the rest of the place brought the familiar nausea back into his throat.

“Does all of this go into the model?” he finally asked.

“All of it.

“Where you stood.

“How long.

“I want to know what changes with you and what changes on its own.”

“And what are you finding?”

“Some of the readings follow you.

“I don’t yet know whether the effect repeats reliably enough.”

By evening his legs ached as though he had been carrying something heavy.

All he had done was stand.

Food was served in a common room in the residential sector.

Asgar took whatever could be eaten by hand, sat near the edge, and ate slowly.

People looked at him once, briefly, the way people look at a stranger, then stopped paying attention.

The lighting in the passages did not change.

Evening here was not something you saw in the sky.

You knew it by the number of people disappearing from the corridors.

Asgar learned to notice that and began eating later, when only two or three remained.

The issued device occasionally displayed short messages from Tessa.

What time tomorrow.

What to bring.

He answered *all right* and then held the device in his hand for a long time because no one else wrote to him.

On the third day they had to repeat one series.

It turned out the dataset had included the bag in a different position.

Asgar picked it up more sharply than necessary.

“It’s always with me.”

“Exactly why I need it separated.”

He started to object, then remembered leaving the bag by the wall the day before when he approached the telephone.

“Yes.

“I did take it off.”

Tessa corrected the interval and went back to work.

That evening he met Ilar near the residential-sector entrance.

Ilar was still in the same body, carrying a long sealed case and complaining that the closure had delayed the inspection.

Asgar offered to take the other end.

“It’s light,” Ilar said. “Just awkward.”

They carried it together anyway.

The case had to stay off the ground and be turned sideways at the corners.

Ilar walked first and warned him before each one.

At the door he lowered his end and shook out his hand.

“Still gripping things the old way.”

Asgar set his own end beside it.

“How much longer in that body?”

“Two days.”

He said it the way someone might describe the time remaining on a work shift.

This time Asgar did not ask how many bodies Ilar had had.

---

On the fourth day, Asgar woke with the thought that the booth was gone.

That had happened before.

Before, he had woken in his own place.

Asgar dressed faster than usual and went to the closed sector without stopping for food.

The booth was still there.

Red.

Door open.

Two people were already working beyond the boundary.

He stood there until the feeling passed and only then realized he had not said hello.

From that day onward he arrived before the scheduled time.

Tessa did not ask why.

The work became familiar.

He knew where to stand.

When he could lower his hands.

How long one series lasted.

On the fifth day he himself pointed out that he had stood slightly differently during the second repetition.

Tessa stopped the recording and ran it again.

---

On the sixth day Tessa called him to the worktable.

The message had arrived earlier than usual and contained two more words than her messages normally did.

Asgar read it twice on the way over.

Translated labels appeared after he confirmed the language mode.

A diagram unfolded before him:

regions joined by lines.

branches converging.

diverging.

ending.

He stared at it, trying to work out which part represented the booth and which part represented him.

“Is this a route?”

“A model of possible states.

“These are our measurements.

“These are your reports of earlier appearances, kept separate.

“It does not predict future dates.”

Tessa highlighted the region that changed when Asgar moved.

“We measured the effect of position.

“The effect of your mass is still only calculated.”

Asgar moved closer to the table.

He followed the highlighted lines, trying to imagine which part of the diagram would remain without them.

“What if you remove me?”

Tessa looked at him.

“From the model?”

“Yes.

“Everything connected to my body.

“Not remove me from the booth now. From the calculation.”

She returned to the source set.

“We can.

“But don’t confuse the result with reality.

“We’re removing you from a calculation, not from the world.

“It tells us how the model behaves, not what would actually happen without you.”

Asgar nodded.

Tessa created a second version beside the first.

For a while nothing seemed to change.

Then several lines disappeared.

Some connected differently.

Where the original had shown a narrow passage between possible states, the new version allowed more options.

Asgar leaned forward.

“There. Now it’s different.”

“Partly.”

She did not enlarge the changed region.

Instead she moved both diagrams back until each could be seen whole.

Asgar studied them.

The branches were different.

The overall direction remained.

“You removed everything?”

“Everything the model directly tied to your current state.

“Now I’m checking whether any indirect dependency on you remains.”

She worked in silence.

Asgar stood beside her with one palm resting on the edge of the table.

Somewhere in the room, a chair moved.

He waited for Tessa to find an error and for the lines to finally point somewhere else.

She produced another calculation.

The overall direction remained.

“Is that direction toward the source?” he asked.

“No. These data don’t give an endpoint.”

“But toward wherever the return started?”

Tessa turned her head.

“You’re the one calling it a return.

“All I can see is this:

the process is directed somewhere either way.”

Asgar lifted his hand from the table.

He remembered asking Mirena whether they could return him.

Remembered telling Tyra that one more transition might bring him to a person who truly knew the answer.

Every one of those conversations had contained the same assumption:

somewhere there was a place he had to reach.

On the diagram, his contribution had been removed.

The lines continued.

“Then what exactly is returning?” he asked.

Tessa looked at both models.

“That is precisely what I don’t know.”

Asgar pulled a chair closer and sat in front of the table.

“Leave both versions up.

“I want to look at them again.”
