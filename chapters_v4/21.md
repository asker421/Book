Chapter 21. Sky Underfoot

His sole slipped on the wet floor.

Asgar caught himself on the door handle and nudged the bag closer with his boot so it would not get in the way. Something clicked in the receiver. He pressed it to his ear, expecting Mirena, but light had already appeared beyond the glass, and the words he had been about to say vanished from his mind.

At first he saw white bands on blue.

They filled almost the entire lower window. Higher up ran a curved edge, faintly outlined in blue; beyond it was blackness. Asgar raised his head, looking for the wall that might be displaying it. Instead of a wall, he saw a long pale surface lined with rows of structures. It stretched into the distance, rose, and continued into the place where the sky should have been.

He stepped back until his back hit the wall beside the telephone.

"Mirena."

"I hear you. What happened?"

Asgar opened his mouth, but at first all he could do was breathe in. It felt as though the floor tilted toward the glass. The bag stood level, the water by his boot did not run toward the window, and yet he pressed his back harder against the wall.

"Earth is out there."

"You can see the planet?"

"Below. To the side... I don't know. I don't understand where down is here."

He looked at his feet, then outside again. A solid surface lay between the booth and the edge of the passage. Beyond it began a transparent barrier, behind which the blue surface curved away beneath him.

Asgar bent his knees as if that might make his position more stable.

"Don't open the door yet," Mirena said. "We're checking the external environment."

The white bands moved slowly. Or the place he was in was moving. Asgar tried to pick one point and hold his gaze on it, but that made it worse. He turned toward the telephone. Black housing, round metal dial, a screw near the lower edge. At least everything here stayed where it was.

"Are we in space?"

"The cabin is inside some kind of artificial structure. There's air outside, pressure is normal. We're still checking the rest."

"And the floor?"

"Down is stable here. Are you being pulled anywhere yourself?"

"No."

He loosened his fingers on the door handle. Damp marks remained on the metal.

Mirena asked him to describe what he could see immediately beyond the threshold. Asgar forced himself to turn back: the surface, markings along the passage, a transparent wall on the right. Far to the left, a figure was moving. The person stopped, raised a hand toward something near their face, and went back.

"The area in front of the door is clear," Asgar said. "But I don't want to go out there."

"We can wait a little."

He stood until his breathing became steadier. His socks were still wet from the river. Asgar focused on his cold toes, the heavy jacket, the bag between his boots. Everything he had entered with was here.

"What year?"

Mirena gave him the date: February fourth, eighty-seven thousand one hundred sixteen.

Asgar looked at the blue edge of the planet. The number barely stayed in his mind. He could only look at the fact that beyond the glass there was no ground at all to step onto.

---

When Mirena confirmed that exiting was permissible, he opened the door a little.

Nothing rushed outward. A faint current of cool air touched his face. Asgar stood holding the handle for a moment, then opened it wider and cautiously put one foot out. The surface was firm, slightly rough.

He shifted his weight onto that foot. The floor held. His eyes did not find that reassuring: the far end of the passage rose, and pale rows of structures extended into the place where the sky should have been. Asgar took his second step the way people walk on ice.

The air had no smell. No water, no earth, no smoke—nothing that could tell him where he had emerged. Somewhere, something large was running with a steady hum, and the sound came not from one direction but from everywhere at once.

Signs lit up above the passage.

He stared at them without understanding a single one.

A phrase sounded outside. Asgar looked up. The voice came from somewhere overhead; there was no person nearby.

"What are they saying?"

"I can't hear it clearly," Mirena replied. "Is that a local system?"

"Probably."

The phrase repeated. Ahead, near the turn, a narrow strip of light appeared. Asgar stepped closer, trying to see where it led, and the receiver pulled his arm backward.

He stopped.

The cord stretched from the telephone through the open doorway. The strip was only a few steps away, but he could go no farther. Asgar returned to the threshold and freed the cord from the edge of the door.

"I'm going to hang up now."

"All right. If you need contact, come back to the telephone."

He replaced the receiver, picked up the bag, and stepped out.

He did not linger by the transparent barrier, but he had to look once. Below floor level ran a white band, and water gleamed through a break in the clouds. He had seen something like it once in his life, from an airplane. Back then there had been an airplane floor beneath him, and in two hours there had been ground.

Asgar shifted his gaze to the surface underfoot and stayed closer to the solid wall from then on.

The overhead voice spoke again, and Asgar answered in Russian:

"I don't understand you. I need translation."

There was a pause.

He repeated it more slowly, then tried English. The signs above the passage changed. A line of Latin letters appeared among them, but he did not have time to make sense of it either: it disappeared and was replaced by Russian text.

"Historical language model. Confirm comprehension."

Asgar read it aloud.

The next question sounded in Russian. The system separately offered speech translation and translation of local signage. He agreed to both and asked it not to display several messages at once.

Labels appeared beside the signs at the turn.

The passage was temporarily closed because of a spatial mismatch.

The words were new. The thing behind them was not.

Asgar turned around.

The booth stood on the surface, red and cramped, its door wide open. A luminous boundary ran around it. Another began farther out. He had already crossed the first, but had not reached the second.

"Where should I go?" he asked.

The system asked him to move to an open area by the wall on the left.

He found it by the new label and set the bag by the wall.

---

Ilar appeared from the opposite end of the passage.

He stopped before the luminous boundary, read the message, and snapped his head up. He spoke to the local system. Asgar heard the translation: the man needed to reach the neighboring sector, where he was expected for a fastening inspection, and the detour would take longer.

The system suggested another route.

Ilar listened, looked at the booth, and only then noticed Asgar.

"Do you know how long this will last?"

"No. It appeared with me."

The man looked back at the red cabin. He was silent for a while.

"What channel did you come through?"

"Through that."

"I can see that. I'm asking about the prepared channel."

Asgar shook his head.

Ilar looked at the cabin longer this time.

"And where are you from?"

"In time—very far away."

"How far?"

Asgar gave the year he had first left from.

Ilar did not ask him to repeat it. He looked from Asgar to the red door and back, then finally turned to his own device: they were waiting for him at the fastening inspection.

He requested something from the system. The full answer was not translated for Asgar, only a designation for restricted access. The man did not come any closer.

He dictated something briefly to the side. Asgar caught fragments in translation: passage number, "without channel," "person on site."

"Then I'll wait here. My name is Ilar."

Asgar gave his name.

Up close, Ilar's face looked ordinary. No metallic skin, no glowing eyes—nothing Asgar could use to connect him to the surroundings. Only his clothes sat strangely evenly, and his fingers were longer than Asgar expected.

Ilar flexed his hand and gripped the wrist with the other.

"Unfamiliar grip. They made this for the job, and I still pick things up the old way."

"Did they replace your hand?"

"My whole body. For the gravity here. I'll work a few days, then I won't need it anymore."

Asgar looked at his face.

"And where do you go?"

"Into another body."

He said it while checking a message about his route. Asgar waited for him to add some kind of qualification.

"So they'll transfer you?"

"Very roughly—yes. But you'd be better asking someone else about the theory. I inspect fastenings."

"And this body?"

"It'll be recycled."

Asgar looked down at his hands. One rested on his thigh, the other touched his wrist. Ilar was talking about what would be done with his body in a few days.

"And you're not afraid?"

Ilar finally dismissed the message.

"I'm not afraid of changing bodies. I'm afraid of being cut off on the way. That's why I only use what I trust."

"How do you know it'll still be you?"

"How would you like to test that?"

Asgar started answering and got tangled. Memory could be copied. The person who woke up could believe he was the same person. But the one who had existed before...

Ilar did not interrupt.

"There are people who once continued from the same version of me," he said. "At that point the memory was shared. Then it diverged. Now each of us has a separate life."

"Copies?"

"People."

Asgar fell silent.

The planet was still visible beyond the barrier. Suddenly he became intensely aware of his own forehead, the thin line above his eyebrow, his wet feet inside his boots. His entire body, which he had never once left and which now seemed like the only thing whose origin he understood at all.

"Sorry," he said. "I don't know what to call that."

Ilar nodded and added nothing more.

When the closure was lifted, the label on that section changed. The system asked Asgar to leave the area: the restriction around the cabin would remain, and he was now supposed to wait beyond its boundary.

"And it?" Asgar looked back at the booth.

"No one will touch it," Ilar said. "No one will even approach it until they figure out what's going on."

"Will I be able to get back to it?"

"That's not for me to decide. Ask whoever comes to register you." Ilar pointed along the passage. "Go to the second turn. There's a room off to the side. People wait there when the passage is closed. It's empty right now."

He left for his inspection.

Asgar picked up the bag. Labels lit up one by one ahead of him and went dark behind him. He counted the turns anyway, so he would know how to get back.

---

The room was small: several chairs along the wall, a table, an open doorway with no door. Someone had left a clip on the table—apparently the closure had caught someone else at a bad time too.

The label by the entrance changed while Asgar was looking at it. The translation supplied a new term: "temporary registration."

Asgar did not have time to sit down. A woman entered after him, stopped several steps away, and came no closer. She said there was a person in the closed sector whose arrival had no record, and that the record had to be created now.

"Your name."

He gave it. The woman repeated it aloud and clarified the ending.

"Where did you come from?"

"Earth. From a river."

"When?"

Asgar hesitated and gave the year as it had been counted in the place he had left that morning.

The woman did not answer. She was looking not at him but slightly to the side, toward wherever her record was being made.

"Please repeat that."

He did.

"That's according to the calendar of the place you left?"

"Yes. I lived there."

She said the number herself, slowly, separating its parts, then said something aside—not to him. The translation carried only a request to recheck how the number had been parsed.

"We're using historical translation here," she said. "Sometimes it expands year counts incorrectly. I want to rule out my own error first."

Then she asked the system to display the number as it would have been written where he came from. Familiar digits appeared above the table.

"Is that it?"

"Yes."

The woman looked at them longer than at anything else in the room. Her voice remained the same, but she did not ask the next question immediately.

"Is the red cabin in the passage yours?"

"I travel in it. I don't know who it belongs to."

After that, the questions came short, and he answered just as briefly. He had entered it somewhere else. It transported him. He had already ended up in other times. He did not choose the date and never knew it in advance. No one he had met controlled it.

The woman paused. Asgar understood that she was marking something in her record.

"I'm recording this as information from you," she said. "Not as an established fact. Do you understand the difference?"

"Yes."

"If you remember it differently, say so. We'll correct the record."

Then she returned to his arrival and explained what a transition here was expected to have: a request, a window, consent from both sides, someone waiting to receive the arrival. None of that had happened, and no one had been expecting to receive him.

"The sector log is empty for this period," she said, more to herself than to him. "No cargo and no personal transit."

Then she listed where he was allowed to go. The residential sector and connecting passages were open. Access to the object required agreement with the people working there. The device would show the boundary itself; there was no need to ask separately.

"Are you going to keep me here?"

"No one is keeping you anywhere. But until your arrival is explained, we need to know where you are."

She reached the end of the record and looked up.

"Do you need anything? Water, dry clothes, a doctor."

"Water."

She noted it.

"The restriction around the cabin will stay in place for now," she said at the door. "The instruments there disagree with each other. That's no longer your account. That's a measurement."

"Is that bad?"

"It's unclear. Usually something like that gets resolved on site within an hour. This hasn't."

She paused as if deciding how much he needed to know right now.

"They've called Tessa. She works with transport geometry. I'm not saying your case belongs to her field. It's just that they've already tried the other things. She'll need what you saw yourself, not your conclusions."

The woman left.

Asgar sat down by the solid wall. They had offered him a place with a view of the planet, but he asked for another. The bag stood between his feet; he opened it, took out dry socks, then closed it again, unable to make himself change in a strange room.

Water arrived in a recess by the table almost at once. The cup felt warm to the touch even though the water inside was cold. Asgar drank and remained seated, holding it in both hands.

That morning he had been walking along the riverbank, thinking that tomorrow Bertram would come to the lower passage anyway—with someone else this time. That "tomorrow" had passed here so long ago that counting it made no sense. And not just Bertram: all of Ar, everyone who had worked there, eaten, argued, had lived their lives to the end, and then the same span had passed again, and again.

He looked down at his boots. The water in his socks was from that river. It had not even had time to dry.

Tessa came later.

She introduced herself and said she had been given a short record: a person in a closed sector, arrival without a channel, object nearby, movement history based on his account. And the first measurements.

"I read it," she added. "Now I need to understand what part of it I can verify."

"And what can't you?"

"Almost all of it. The log shows that you arrived without a channel, so that part is no longer just your statement. But I have no way to verify that the cabin moves you through time. For now I'm just keeping it in mind."

"So you don't care where I'm from."

"No."

She said it without looking away, and Asgar noticed for the first time that she was watching him longer than necessary just to listen.

"If your record is confirmed, more than one piece of my work will have to be reconsidered," she said more slowly. "But I still can't start with that. Right now there's nothing in your account I can get a grip on."

She asked whether he was ready to walk with her to the site.

"What's there?"

"Two systems show the same wall differently. They called me to see exactly where they diverge." She paused. "I can't see it yet."

Asgar lowered the cup onto his knee.

"I've seen something like that."

Tessa did not answer immediately.

"Where?"

"Not a wall. A panel in a passage. One system closed off an area another system considered clear." He paused. "By my calendar, that was in the year two thousand one hundred ninety-one."

She looked at him the same way the woman before her had looked at the digits and said nothing about the year.

"How did it end?"

"They stopped the drive by hand and got people out."

"So it didn't end."

"No."

Asgar waited. Tessa looked at him and remained silent.

"It can cut things," he said then. "At the beginning it cut off a piece of metal. A tripod leg. The man setting it up had already stepped away."

"You saw that yourself?"

"I did. I was told not to touch it, and I didn't." He paused. "That's why I left my family that time."

"Then tell me that again by the cabin, separately." Tessa did not soften the rest: "I can't tell you whether it's dangerous now. That's why they set the restriction. We don't know how large the area behaving incorrectly is, so the boundary was placed with a margin."

Tessa looked at the transparent part of the wall.

"Is this your first time off a planetary surface?"

"Yes."

Tessa changed the route slightly. They took an interior passage from which the blackness could not be seen. After several steps, Asgar stopped pressing himself against the solid wall. Once the blackness was out of his field of vision, his body stopped expecting to fall.

At the site he saw the booth again. The door remained open. Nothing had been connected to it; people and equipment stayed beyond the marked boundary.

"Am I allowed near it?" Asgar asked.

"You are. You've already been inside it. Just tell us before you approach. I need to know who went near it and when."

Tessa showed him two images of the same wall. In the first, the red surface ran slightly differently than in the second.

Asgar recognized it before he could make out exactly what was different.

"Did you check the instruments?" he asked anyway.

"First thing. The instruments, the people, and a second setup. The discrepancy stayed the same."

Asgar waited for her to name the next cause. In the hall, they had started with their own instruments too.

"That's where my list runs out," Tessa said. "Usually by this point you can already see where the error is. Here you can't. So we change how we're looking."

"They aren't sending anything into it?"

"Right now we're only listening to what comes out on its own. We aren't illuminating it with anything."

Asgar nodded. In the ruins, it had taken them longer to reach that point.

Tessa asked him to recount both incidents in full. He told her about the hall in 2191 and about the machines outside, and stopped himself at the outer field.

"The woman on the phone told me that. I only saw the result after shutdown."

Tessa separated one record from the other.

"That's how we'll keep it."

"Can you talk to her?"

"If we need to, we'll establish a separate channel. For now, your account and our measurements are enough."

Asgar looked back at the passage. Someone was walking around the turn, and labels lit up ahead of them one by one, just as they had for Asgar several hours earlier.

"The man I spoke to said they'll move him into another body in a few days," he said. "Completely."

"They will."

"Then why can't you tell me what this is?" He pointed at the cabin. "Look at what you can do."

Tessa did not answer immediately.

"We can move an object from here to the neighboring sector," she said. "But the channel is built first. From both sides. Both points exist, both are known, both are coordinated. Your cabin arrived somewhere no one had built anything. Neither end."

"That's how it always arrives."

"I believe that's what you've seen. Nothing arrives that way here."

Asgar was silent for a moment.

"And backward?" he asked. "A person—back to their own time."

"No."

She answered immediately, in the same tone she had used about the instruments.

"There are areas where time passes more slowly than nearby. You can end up later than everyone else without living through that interval. Forward—a little, and only where we've built everything ourselves. No one knows how to go backward. At least I've never heard of it."

Asgar looked past her toward the red wall.

"So you don't know what this is either."

"I don't. That's why I'm standing here with two instruments that disagree with each other."

She unfolded an image above the boundary—not a picture, but something live: a section of the passage, the cabin in it, and a grid around it whose spacing changed where the readings diverged.

"We're good at calculating," Tessa said. "This is the model. I put in what we've measured, and it reconstructs the rest. It can reconstruct the sector without the cabin completely. With the cabin, it can't."

Asgar watched the grid tremble finely near the red wall. The tremor did not extend far: along the same ring around the cabin, the spacing of the lines almost immediately evened out, and beyond it the model became calm again.

"Where does it stop diverging?"

Tessa touched the scale by the ring.

"Here it's eleven point eight. On the opposite side, a little over twelve. It fluctuates, but comes back to roughly the same place."

Asgar looked at the red body, then back at the ring.

"So you'll calculate it eventually."

"It calculates from what we put into it. So far we've put in very little." Tessa dismissed the image. "And almost everything that appeared here together with the cabin is you."

"Me?"

"You came in with it and move around near it. I need to know what changes when you move and what changes on its own. Otherwise I can't separate you in the calculation."

Asgar looked at the open door.

He wanted to hear Mirena even though he had nothing to say to her.

---

They gave him a small room not far from the closed sector. He could sleep there, wash, and leave his things. Local translation worked through a device they provided; Asgar was shown how to call for help and where Tessa's messages would appear.

The first evening, he could not fall asleep for a long time.

When he closed his eyes, the bright surface of the ring rose overhead again. It felt as though the bed was slowly turning.

It was quiet beyond the wall. Asgar caught himself listening for weather—for rain, wind, anything that happened outside. There was no outside around this place.

Several times he sat up, checked the floor with his feet, and looked at the bag.

Tobin's figurine lay inside, between the clothes and the bundle with the phone.

Asgar took it out and set it on the table. On the smooth surface it rocked slightly because its base was uneven. He turned it until it stood more securely and lay down.

The next day Tessa asked him to walk several times across a marked area near the cabin. Then to stand in different places.

There were nine marks. He stood on each one, waited until someone at the side said he could move, then went to the next. By midday he began counting to himself so he would know how much longer he had to stand.

Two others worked beyond the boundary. They did not look at him or speak to him. Once, a woman in light clothing asked him to turn his shoulders a little. No one said anything else to him during that time.

The floor beneath his boots was level and cool. The red wall remained three steps away. Asgar looked at it because everything else brought the familiar sickness up into his throat.

"Does all this go into the model?" he finally asked.

"All of it. Where you stood, and for how long. I want to understand what changes with you and what changes by itself."

"And what does it show?"

"Some of the readings follow you. I don't yet know whether it repeats reliably enough."

By evening his legs ached as though he had been carrying something heavy, though all he had done was stand.

Food was served in a common room in the residential sector. Asgar took things he could eat with his hands, sat at the edge, and ate slowly. People looked at him once, briefly, the way they look at a stranger, and did not look again. The lighting in the passages did not change; evening here was recognized not by the sky but by there being fewer people. Asgar learned to notice that and started going to eat later, when only two or three remained.

The device he had been given occasionally displayed short lines from Tessa. What time tomorrow. What to bring. He replied "okay" and then held the device in his hand for a long time, because no one else wrote to him.

On the third day, one series had to be repeated: it turned out the data set being used had recorded the bag in a different position. Asgar picked it up more sharply than necessary.

"It's always with me."

"Exactly. That's why I need the bag separated."

He wanted to object, then remembered how yesterday he had left the bag by the wall before going to the telephone.

"Yes. I took it off."

Tessa clarified the moment and went back to work.

That evening he met Ilar at the entrance to the residential sector. He was still in the same body, carrying a long closed case and complaining that the closure had delayed the inspection.

Asgar offered to take the other end.

"It's light," Ilar replied. "Just awkward."

They carried it together anyway. The case had to be held off the floor and turned sideways at the corners. Ilar walked first and warned him at each one.

At the doors he set down his end and shook his hand.

"Still picking things up the old way," he said.

Asgar lowered his end beside it.

"How much longer in this one?"

"Two days."

He said it the way people talk about how much of a shift remains.

This time Asgar did not ask how many bodies he had had.

---

On the fourth day, he woke with the thought that the cabin was already gone.

It had happened to him before, but before he had woken up in his own place. Asgar dressed faster than usual and went to the closed sector without stopping for food. The cabin was still there, red, its door open, and two people beyond the boundary were already working.

He stood there until the feeling passed, and only then realized he had not said hello.

From that day on he arrived earlier than scheduled. Tessa did not ask why.

The work became understandable. He knew where to stand, when he could lower his arms, how long one series lasted. On the fifth day he himself said that on the second run he had been standing slightly differently than on the first. Tessa stopped the recording and redid it.

---

On the sixth day Tessa called him over to the worktable.

The message came earlier than usual, and it contained two more words than it normally did. Asgar read it twice on the way there.

Translated labels appeared after Asgar confirmed the language mode. A diagram unfolded in front of him: sections connected by lines, several branches that converged, diverged, and ended.

He looked at it, trying to understand what represented the booth and what represented him.

"Is this a route?"

"A model of possible states. It contains our measurements and, separately, the information you gave us about earlier appearances. It doesn't produce future dates."

Tessa highlighted a section that changed with his movements.

"We measured the effect of position. The effect of mass has only been calculated so far."

Asgar moved closer to the table. He followed the highlighted lines, trying to understand what part of the diagram would remain without them.

"What if you remove me?"

Tessa looked at him.

"From the model?"

"Yes. Everything tied to my body. Not from the booth now—from the calculation."

She returned to the original data set.

"We can. Just don't confuse the two: we'll remove you from the calculation, not from reality. This will show how the model behaves, not what would really happen without you."

Asgar nodded.

Tessa created a second version beside the first. For a while nothing changed. Then some of the lines disappeared, several connected differently. Where there had previously been a narrow passage between states, more possibilities appeared.

Asgar leaned forward.

"There. Now it's different."

"Partly, yes."

She did not enlarge the changed section. Instead she moved both diagrams back so they fit in full.

Asgar looked at them.

The branching was different. The overall direction remained.

"Are you sure you removed everything?"

"I removed everything the model directly tied to your current state. Now I'm checking whether any of you remains indirectly."

She worked in silence. Asgar stood beside her with one hand resting on the edge of the table. Somewhere in the room, someone moved a chair. He waited for Tessa to find an error and for the lines to finally diverge.

She produced another calculation.

The overall direction remained.

"Is that direction toward the source?" he asked.

"No. This data doesn't give an endpoint."

"But toward where the return started?"

Tessa turned her head toward him.

"You're the one calling it a return. I can see only one thing: the process is still directed somewhere."

Asgar took his hand off the table.

He remembered asking Mirena whether they would be able to send him back. How he had told Tyra that one more transition might lead to someone who truly knew the answer. In all those conversations he had imagined the same thing: somewhere there was a place that he had to reach.

In the diagram, his contribution had been removed.

The lines continued.

"Then what exactly is returning?" he asked.

Tessa looked at the two models.

"That's exactly what I don't know."

Asgar pulled a chair closer and sat down in front of the table.

"Leave both versions up. I want to look again."
