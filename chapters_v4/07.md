Chapter 7. Familiar Eyes

The caseworker pulled the screen closer to herself.

Asgar watched her face. While she read, her lips moved slightly, and he tried to guess the answer before she said it. The woman opened another record and compared something with the information entered.

"There is a current contact. We'll try to reach her now."

"So she's alive?"

"Yes. The contact information was updated recently. We'll call now."

She put on a headset and turned toward the screen. Asgar straightened and took his hands off the table. His crumpled tissue lay on the chair beside him; he picked it up and put it in his pocket.

The caseworker waited for an answer.

Then she said:

"Ekaterina? Good afternoon. I'm calling from the service center regarding a matter connected with Asgar."

Asgar leaned forward.

He could not hear Katya. He only saw the woman behind the desk stop typing and place her palm beside the keyboard.

"No, this is not a request for your documents. A man came to us today with his identity card. His identity has been confirmed against archived biometric data."

She listened to the reply.

"Yes. He's here."

Asgar stood.

The caseworker looked up at him and raised a palm: wait.

"Yes, I understand. Let's switch on video. You'll see him yourself."

Asgar stood with his thigh pressed against the table. His legs seemed to get in each other's way; he moved one and bumped the chair.

The woman removed the headset.

"She agreed."

He sat down before she could ask him to. He ran a hand over his hair and lowered it to his knee. The screen was turned toward him. For several seconds, it showed an empty field.

Then Katya appeared.

Asgar recognized her before he had time to look closely.

"Katya."

She was sitting very close to the camera. The edge of her hand was visible at the bottom of the image; the picture rocked, as though she had moved the device and had not immediately found something to rest it on.

Katya looked at him.

He waited for her to speak and could not continue himself. Her hair was shorter. A pale strand lay against her face, and she kept pushing it back. He remembered that movement with long hair: the fingers went behind the ear and then slid a little farther down the neck. Now the hand stopped sooner.

"Say something else," she asked.

Her voice was lower than it had been that morning. But on the last word it broke in a familiar way.

"I came out. I started calling right away, only my phone here doesn't—"

Katya covered her mouth with her hand.

Asgar stopped.

She looked over her fingers without taking her eyes off him. He saw the lines beside the bridge of her nose, the pale skin beneath her eyes. For a second he wanted to move the image closer, sharpen it, remove everything that might be caused by a bad camera.

"Where were you?" she asked.

"In the booth."

"All this time?"

"For me... Katya, I talked to you today. About the folding cot. You told me to lower the brace."

Her hand slowly dropped.

"The brace?"

"The leg kept folding. I put a tile under it. You said there should be a brace on the side."

Katya turned away. He saw part of the room behind her shoulder: a pale wall, a narrow shelf, a cup on the table. Nothing familiar.

"I don't remember," she said.

He wanted to remind her. His mouth had already opened.

Katya looked at him again.

"I remember that you called. I remember the last conversation. But not the folding cot."

Asgar lowered his eyes.

His identity card lay on the table between them. That morning, before walking across the yard to the red door, he had checked that it was still there.

The caseworker stood quietly.

"I'll be outside. If you need help, call me."

She left and pulled the door nearly shut.

Asgar waited for the click.

"Are you alone?"

"Right now, yes."

"The children?"

"They're both all right."

He exhaled so sharply that it hurt beneath his ribs.

Katya leaned closer.

"Both alive, Asgar. Everything is all right. I'll call them."

"Are they far away?"

"The older one is outside the city. The younger one is here in Baku. But first I..."

She reached for something outside the frame and brought her hand back empty.

"First I have to understand for myself that it's you."

"It is me."

"I can see that."

Katya wiped her cheek with the heel of her hand. Asgar ran a hand over his own face too, though it was dry.

"I can come to you," he said. "Give me the address. They've already verified me here, I'll—"

"I'll come myself. Stay there."

She stood. Part of her shoulder remained in frame, then the room without her. Asgar heard a drawer slide open.

"Katya?"

"I'm here. Looking for my bag."

"Don't disconnect."

She came back and sat down.

"I'm not disconnecting."

A thin chain was around her neck. Asgar looked at it because looking at her face continuously was becoming difficult. He recognized Katya in the movement of her lips, in the way she lifted her chin slightly while waiting for an answer. Then his gaze would catch on something unfamiliar, and he had to begin recognizing her again.

"Are you at home?" he asked.

"Yes."

"We don't live there anymore?"

Katya shook her head.

"I moved."

"When?"

"A long time ago. When the younger one started living on his own."

Asgar looked back at the closed door. Suddenly he needed somewhere else to look.

The younger one had told him about a toy car today. Breathed into the microphone, been upset that Dad did not know when he would come home.

"He lives by himself?"

"By himself. At first I checked every morning to see if he was awake. Then he asked me to stop."

Katya tried to smile.

"You would have been on his side."

Asgar nodded even though he did not know whose side he would have been on. He had not had a single conversation with this adult man.

"Send me a picture."

"I will. Better if you talk yourselves. But I'll tell them first. I don't want someone else calling them."

She picked up a second phone or something that looked like one. Asgar could see only the edge in her hand.

"Wait a little longer," he asked.

Katya put it back down.

For a while, they sat in silence.

"I saw in the documents," Asgar said. "That I was..."

He could not say the word.

"Yes."

"You knew?"

She slowly straightened.

"I filed the application."

He looked at her.

Katya rubbed the edge of the table with her thumb.

"There were things that had to be finished, and no one would let me finish them without you. They told me: bring the decision. I went somewhere else, and they asked for the same thing. I kept having to prove why you couldn't come yourself."

"I'm not asking why you—"

"You are."

Asgar looked away.

"Probably."

"I didn't want to. Then I had to anyway."

He imagined her in a courthouse corridor with a folder. Tried to place himself beside her, take the documents, speak for her, and that made it worse.

"I'm sorry."

Katya pressed her lips together.

"I can't talk about that right now."

"All right."

"And don't think I spent all that time just sitting and waiting for a call. I worked. The children grew up. We argued, went places, did renovations. Once a pipe burst at night and I was collecting water off the stairs. A lot happened."

She stopped and looked somewhere below the camera.

"I just don't know where to start telling you."

Asgar reached toward the screen. His fingers touched the cold edge.

"Wherever you want."

Katya noticed the movement. Her face crumpled; she closed her eyes and shook her head.

"I need to see you properly."

"Come."

"I'm leaving now."

She reached for the bag again, this time leaving it on her lap. Keys clinked inside.

Asgar suddenly remembered Katya's voice in the stadium service corridor: "Then don't drag it out. And don't forget the water, please." She had been smiling then—he could hear it in her voice. He had been going to buy water and walk into the kitchen where Katya was fussing with the cake.

"And the cake?" he asked.

Katya froze.

"What cake?"

"That day. The sixteenth. Did you..."

He stopped when he heard his own question.

Katya looked at him for a long time.

"We cut it that evening."

Asgar took his hand away from the screen.

He lowered his head. There was pale dust from the repair depot on his jeans by the knee. Asgar rubbed it with his fingers, and the mark spread wider.

"What are you wearing?" Katya asked.

He did not understand at first.

"A T-shirt. From the bag at the depot."

"Stand up."

Asgar pushed the chair back and stood in front of the screen.

Katya looked at him from below. Asgar was already about to sit back down.

"Wait," she said.

He remained standing.

"I folded it," Katya said. "That one."

Asgar took hold of the hem of the T-shirt.

"Yes."

She turned away and began searching for something in the bag. She rummaged for a long time even though the keys were already lying in front of her.

---

When the caseworker returned, Katya asked for the exact address. The woman explained where the center was, then looked at Asgar.

"One more thing. You said the booth appears near you and has already damaged equipment. There are a lot of people here."

Asgar stood up.

He had managed to forget.

"I'd better go outside."

"Yes. They'll take you into the yard. I've already called the duty service; they're looking for a separate room."

Katya listened from the screen.

"Can it appear again?" she asked.

Asgar turned toward her.

"I don't know. The woman on the phone said the connection might remain after I came out."

"Did you talk to her here?"

"No. Only before I went in."

Katya looked at the caseworker.

"Tell me where they take him. I'll come there."

"Keep the connection active. As soon as we have an address, I'll send it."

Asgar picked up his identity card and mobile from the table.

"Katya, I—"

"I'm on the line. They have my number now."

He nodded.

She kept looking at him for several more seconds.

"Just make sure they tell me where first. Don't leave until I know."

---

The yard smelled of heated trash containers. Asgar stood in an open area between the back entrance and the fence. An employee from the center remained by the door and asked anyone coming out to go around the other side.

They had to wait for the car.

Two police officers came out into the yard. The caseworker met them by the door and showed them the case number. One stayed with her, the other came over to Asgar, introduced himself, and explained that the duty unit had received a report about a person found from a closed missing-person case. The same old photograph was on his screen. He asked Asgar's name, compared it to the record, and lingered on Asgar's face.

"You came to the center yourself?"

"Yes. I need to get to my wife. She's already on her way."

The officer went back to the caseworker without putting the image away. Asgar heard a question about the repeat verification. The woman opened the duty specialist's conclusion; the officer read it and called his unit. This time, when he named the year of the disappearance, he turned his whole body toward Asgar.

A woman with a phone stopped beyond the fence. Asgar turned away, but after several steps found himself opposite her again. The police officer went to the fence, asked her not to film, and stayed there until she left.

During that time, Asgar took out his phone twice to look at the photograph of the children, but the screen no longer came on. He pressed the button again, held it longer, then put the mobile away. The charger had remained in the trailer, twenty-five years from here.

The caseworker came out with a tablet and a small bag. She handed him water.

"We have a place. A technical building attached to the archives division. There's an empty hall with a separate entrance. The duty group is already going there."

"Archives?"

"Your case is stored with them. They requested the materials from today's report as soon as the identity confirmation came through."

"The police are going there too?"

"No. They've passed the information to the unit. There's nothing more for them to do here."

"Do they know about the booth?"

"The file contains recordings and reports. I don't handle the technical side."

Asgar unscrewed the bottle cap.

"Does Katya know the address?"

"Yes. She received it."

Asgar screwed the cap back onto the bottle and walked toward the car.

---

A wide ramp led down into the technical hall. Asgar followed an employee of the building down it; the caseworker stayed upstairs, finishing a phone call.

It was cooler below. Pale stripes on the floor marked a path toward the far door. Empty metal carts stood along one wall, and a coiled cable lay dark in a corner. The employee moved the nearest cart into the adjacent room and asked Asgar to remain near the entrance for now.

"Has it been empty long?" Asgar asked.

"Since spring. The equipment was moved."

The man checked the side door and came back.

"No unauthorized people come in here. If you notice anything, say so immediately."

Asgar looked at the ceiling. Concrete beams, pipes, lights. After the street, the room felt too quiet.

He told the employee about the shortened tripod leg. The man listened, looked at the remaining carts, and called someone over the radio. A few minutes later, they were removed too.

The caseworker came down with a portable screen. Behind her, a table and two chairs were brought in. They were set by the entrance, leaving the middle of the hall empty.

"While we're waiting for the specialist, you can look at part of the archive," she said. "You asked about the man at the stadium. We found the recording Kerimov referred to."

Asgar sat down.

The familiar corridor appeared on the screen.

He immediately recognized the spot where he had stood with the mobile. The image was brighter than the one Rauf had shown him. The red wall was visible in the distance. Asgar leaned closer, placing his hands on either side of the screen.

The booth door opened.

A person climbed out and paused beside the body. Then walked down the corridor.

Asgar did not look at himself, even though his own figure was now visible too. He watched the man: lowered shoulders, cautious step, brief stop.

"Slower."

The caseworker reduced the speed.

The man turned his head.

Asgar raised a hand.

"Here. Stop."

The face remained a small pale patch.

"Can you bring it closer?"

She enlarged the area. The space around the head broke into blurred rectangles. The caseworker briefly returned to the original scale. The distortion did not occupy the entire corridor: closer to the turn, the tile seams became straight again, and the interference ended so abruptly it seemed to have an edge of its own.

Asgar stared until his eyes began to hurt.

"In twenty-five years, you didn't do anything with this?"

The caseworker opened the list of attachments to the recording.

"There are processed versions. I'll show you the latest."

She opened a second file. The contours were clearer, but the face still did not resolve into someone who could be recognized.

"And more?"

"That's everything they were able to get."

Asgar pushed the screen away.

He did not know what he had expected. That the future would put a name under the blurred head. That the man would have been found, questioned, would have told them everything long ago, leaving Asgar only to read it.

"Did they even look for him?"

The caseworker opened several documents.

"They did. They checked exits, neighboring cameras, witnesses. His identity was never established."

Asgar looked sideways at the list of documents.

"And Kerimov?"

The caseworker found the relevant field.

"He handled the case until 2019. Then he transferred it. But he returned to it twice more, no longer in an official capacity. The last note is seven years after your disappearance."

By seven years later, Asgar himself had already been legally declared dead for a year. He lowered his eyes to the edge of the table. The man who had told him "that's why I haven't closed the case" had not closed it even after everyone else had.

In the paused frame, the man was still looking toward the booth.

Asgar remembered stepping back to clear the way for him. He could have approached, made sure he did not need help. Could have kept him there for a few seconds.

He reached for the playback control and watched the segment once more.

The man disappeared around the corner.

---

The employee of the building brought the investigation reports. He put them on the table in electronic form and remained standing nearby while the caseworker opened the relevant section.

When she reached the medical record, the man asked Asgar to turn toward the light. Asgar did, expecting another photograph. The employee took nothing. He looked at the date on the report and moved his hand away from the screen.

"I prepared these materials for transfer," he said. "In spring. There were only records there."

Asgar pulled his chair closer to the table. He needed to read what they had found over all those years.

First on the list was the medical report from August twentieth. Asgar opened it and read one line twice: no signs of a condition consistent with four days without water, food, or sleep were found.

Asgar scrolled through several pages. The dates changed. The same frames appeared among diagrams and tables. He stopped on words he understood, trying to connect them into an answer.

"I need to know one thing," he said. "Is there another device like this? Anything at all that can send me back?"

The employee shook his head.

"We don't have a working device."

"You don't. What about anywhere else?"

"I don't know. But not in these materials. They were unable to reproduce the transition."

Asgar returned to the beginning of the document.

"But you studied it."

"They studied recordings and what remained after the appearance. The cabin itself did not appear again after your disappearance."

The man pointed to one of the sections.

"Here are the attempts to reproduce the effect. They were unable to send either a person or an object to another time."

Asgar read the line he was pointing at.

"And that's it?"

The employee did not answer immediately.

"For you, a few hours passed. This work took years."

Asgar looked up at him, then back at the screen.

Surnames were listed below. Dates of interim reports. Signatures beneath failed experiments.

"Sorry," he said. "I didn't mean—"

He did not finish and closed the document.

The caseworker checked a message.

"Ekaterina is almost here. An employee will meet her upstairs."

Asgar turned toward the ramp.

For the first time since leaving the booth, he knew exactly where Katya would come from.

He stood, took several steps, and stopped at the stripe on the floor. He wanted to meet her at the entrance, but looked at the empty center of the hall and stayed where he was.

"Tell her I'm here."

"She knows."

Asgar put a hand into his pocket and felt the dead phone. He took it out and ran a finger across the dark glass.

The light above the table reflected in the screen.

He tilted the phone.

The reflection doubled.

Asgar froze, then looked up.

The light hung where it had before. But beside the far wall, a thin yellow marking stripe was doubling in the same way: one line ran toward the door, another lay beside it for a short stretch.

He blinked.

The stripes merged.

"Look over there," Asgar said.

The employee turned.

For an instant, a red edge appeared on the concrete wall.

Asgar stepped away from the center of the hall.

"Don't bring Katya down yet."
