Chapter 7. Familiar Eyes

The caseworker pulled the display closer to herself.

Asgar watched her face. Her lips moved slightly as she read, and he tried to guess the answer before she said it. The woman opened another record and checked something against the data she had entered.

“There’s a current contact. We’ll try to reach her now.”

“So she’s alive?”

“Yes. The contact information was updated recently. We’ll call now.”

She put on a headset and turned toward the screen. Asgar straightened and took his hands off the desk. His crumpled tissue lay on the chair beside him; he picked it up and put it in his pocket.

The caseworker waited for an answer.

Then she said:

“Ekaterina? Good afternoon. I’m calling from the citizen service center regarding a case connected with Asgar.”

Asgar leaned forward.

He could not hear Katya. He could only see the woman at the desk stop typing and place one hand beside the keyboard.

“No, this is not a request for access to your documents. A man came to us today carrying his identity card. His identity has been confirmed against archived biometric data.”

She listened.

“Yes. He’s here.”

Asgar stood.

The caseworker looked up and held out a palm: wait.

“Yes, I understand. Let’s switch to video. You can see him yourself.”

Asgar stood with one thigh pressed against the table. His legs seemed to get in each other’s way; he shifted one and bumped the chair.

The woman removed the headset.

“She agreed.”

He sat before she could ask him to. Ran a hand through his hair, then lowered it to his knee. The display was turned toward him. For several seconds it showed only an empty field.

Then Katya appeared.

Asgar recognized her before he had time to look.

“Kat.”

She was sitting very close to the camera. The edge of one hand showed at the bottom of the image; the picture rocked as though she had moved the device and not immediately found something to rest it on.

Katya looked at him.

He waited for her to speak and could not make himself continue either. Her hair was shorter. A pale strand lay against her face, and she kept pushing it back. He remembered the gesture with long hair: her fingers would go behind her ear and travel a little farther down her neck.

Now the hand stopped sooner.

“Say something else,” she asked.

Her voice was lower than it had been that morning. But on the last word it caught in the familiar way.

“I came out. I tried calling you straight away, only my phone here wouldn’t—”

Katya covered her mouth with one hand.

Asgar stopped.

She stared at him over her fingers without looking away. He saw the lines beside the bridge of her nose, the paler skin beneath her eyes. For a second he wanted to pull the image closer, sharpen it, remove everything that might merely be a bad camera.

“Where were you?” she asked.

“In the booth.”

“All this time?”

“For me… Kat, I spoke to you today. About the folding cot. You told me to push the brace down.”

Her hand lowered slowly.

“The brace?”

“The leg kept folding. I wedged a piece of tile under it. You said there should be a brace on the side.”

Katya turned away. Over her shoulder he saw part of the room: a light wall, a narrow shelf, a cup on the table.

Nothing familiar.

“I don’t remember,” she said.

He was about to remind her. His mouth was already open.

Katya looked at him again.

“I remember that you called. I remember our last conversation. But the cot—I don’t remember that.”

Asgar lowered his eyes.

His identity card lay on the table between them. That morning he had checked that he still had it before crossing the yard toward the red door.

The caseworker quietly stood.

“I’ll be outside. Call me if you need anything.”

She left and pulled the door nearly shut.

Asgar waited for the click.

“Are you alone?”

“Right now, yes.”

“The kids?”

“They’re all right.”

He exhaled so sharply that it hurt beneath his ribs.

Katya leaned closer.

“They’re both alive, Asgar. They’re okay. I’ll call them.”

“Are they far away?”

“The older one is outside the city. The younger one’s here in Baku. But first I…”

She reached for something outside the frame, brought her hand back empty.

“First I need to understand for myself that this is really you.”

“It’s me.”

“I can see that.”

Katya wiped her cheek with the heel of her hand. Asgar rubbed a hand over his own face even though it was dry.

“I can come to you,” he said. “Give me the address. They’ve already verified me here. I can—”

“I’ll come myself. Stay there.”

She stood. Part of her shoulder remained in frame, then only the room. Asgar heard a drawer slide open.

“Kat?”

“I’m here. Looking for my bag.”

“Don’t hang up.”

She came back and sat.

“I’m not hanging up.”

A thin chain lay around her neck. Asgar looked at it because it was becoming difficult to keep looking at her face. He recognized Katya in the movement of her lips, in the way she lifted her chin slightly while waiting for an answer. Then his eyes would catch on something unfamiliar and he had to begin recognizing her all over again.

“Are you at home?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“We don’t live there?”

Katya shook her head.

“I moved.”

“When?”

“A long time ago. When the younger one started living on his own.”

Asgar glanced toward the closed door. Suddenly he needed somewhere else to look.

That morning the younger boy had been telling him about a toy car. Breathing into the microphone, upset that Dad did not know when he would be home.

“He lives by himself?”

“Yes. At first I checked every morning to make sure he was awake. Then he asked me to stop.”

Katya tried to smile.

“You would’ve taken his side.”

Asgar nodded, though he had no idea whose side he would have taken. He had never had a single conversation with that grown man.

“Send me a picture.”

“I will. Better if you talk to them yourselves. But I’ll tell them first. I don’t want somebody else calling them.”

She picked up a second phone or something like one. Asgar could see only the edge of it in her hand.

“Wait a little longer,” he said.

Katya put it back down.

For a while they sat in silence.

“I saw it in the records,” Asgar said. “That I was…”

He could not say the word.

“Yes.”

“You knew?”

She slowly straightened.

“I filed the petition.”

He stared at her.

Katya rubbed the edge of the table with her thumb.

“There were things that had to be finished, and no one would let me finish them without you. They’d say: bring the court order. I’d go somewhere else, they’d ask for the same thing. I kept having to prove why you couldn’t come in person.”

“I’m not asking why you—”

“You are.”

Asgar looked away.

“I suppose I am.”

“I didn’t want to. Eventually I had to.”

He pictured her in a courthouse corridor carrying a folder. Tried to put himself beside her, taking the papers from her, speaking for her.

It made it worse.

“I’m sorry.”

Katya pressed her lips together.

“I can’t talk about that right now.”

“Okay.”

“And don’t imagine I spent all those years sitting around waiting for a phone call. I worked. The kids grew up. We fought, went places, did renovations. Once a pipe broke in the middle of the night and I was mopping water off the stairs. A lot happened.”

She stopped and looked somewhere below the camera.

“I just don’t know where to start telling you.”

Asgar reached toward the screen. His fingers touched the cold edge.

“Start wherever you want.”

Katya noticed the movement. Her face crumpled; she closed her eyes and shook her head.

“I need to see you properly.”

“Come.”

“I’m leaving now.”

She reached for the bag again and this time left it on her lap. Keys clinked inside.

Asgar suddenly remembered her voice in the stadium service corridor: *The important part is that you come home.* Katya had been smiling then. He had been going to buy water and walk into the kitchen where she was fussing with the cake.

“And the cake?” he asked.

Katya froze.

“What cake?”

“Back then. On the sixteenth. Did you…”

He stopped when he heard his own question.

Katya looked at him for a long time.

“We cut it that evening.”

Asgar took his hand away from the screen.

He lowered his head. Pale dust from the maintenance yard marked the denim near his knee. Asgar rubbed at it with his fingers and made the stain wider.

“What are you wearing?” Katya asked.

He did not understand at first.

“A T-shirt. From the bag at the yard.”

“Stand up.”

Asgar pushed the chair back and stood in front of the screen.

Katya looked him over from below. He was about to sit again.

“Wait,” she said.

He stayed standing.

“I folded that,” Katya said. “That shirt.”

Asgar took hold of the hem.

“Yeah.”

She turned away and began searching through her bag. She kept moving things around for a long time even though the keys were already on the table in front of her.

---

When the caseworker came back, Katya asked for the exact address. The woman explained where the center was, then looked at Asgar.

“One more thing. You said the booth appears near you and has already damaged equipment. This building is full of people.”

Asgar stood.

He had forgotten.

“I should go outside.”

“Yes. Someone will escort you to the courtyard. I’ve already called the duty service; they’re looking for an isolated room.”

Katya was listening through the screen.

“It can appear again?” she asked.

Asgar turned toward her.

“I don’t know. The woman on the phone said the connection might remain after I came out.”

“Have you spoken to her here?”

“No. Only before I went inside.”

Katya looked at the caseworker.

“Tell me where you take him. I’ll come there.”

“Keep this connection open. As soon as we have the address, I’ll send it.”

Asgar picked up his identity card and mobile.

“Kat, I…”

“I’m here. They have my number now.”

He nodded.

She kept looking at him for several more seconds.

“Just make sure they tell me where first. Don’t leave until I know.”

---

The courtyard smelled of sun-warmed trash bins. Asgar stood in an open area between the rear entrance and the fence. A center employee remained by the door, asking anyone who came out to use the other side.

They had to wait for a car.

Two police officers came into the courtyard. The caseworker met them at the door and showed them the reference number. One remained with her; the other came over to Asgar, introduced himself, and explained that the duty desk had received notice that a person from a closed missing-person case had been found.

The same old photograph was on his screen.

He asked Asgar’s name, matched it to the record, and lingered on his face.

“You came to the center yourself?”

“Yes. I need to get to my wife. She’s already on her way.”

The officer went back to the caseworker without closing the image. Asgar heard him ask about a second verification. The woman opened the on-duty specialist’s confirmation; the officer read it and called his station. This time, when he gave the year of the disappearance, he turned his whole body toward Asgar.

Beyond the fence, a woman stopped with her phone raised.

Asgar turned away, but after a few steps he found himself facing her again. The officer went to the fence, asked her not to film, and stayed there until she left.

During that time Asgar took out his phone twice to look at the picture of his children, but the screen no longer came on. He pressed the button again, held it longer, then put the mobile away.

The charger was still in the trailer.

Twenty-five years from here.

The caseworker came out carrying a tablet and a small bag. She handed him water.

“We have a place. A technical building attached to the archive division. There’s an empty hall with a separate entrance. The duty team is already on the way.”

“The archive?”

“Your case is stored there. They requested the material from today’s report as soon as your identity was confirmed.”

“The police are going there too?”

“No. They passed the information to the division. There’s nothing more for them to do here.”

“Do they know about the booth?”

“The file contains recordings and technical reports. I don’t understand that side of it.”

Asgar twisted the cap off the bottle.

“Does Katya have the address?”

“Yes. She received it.”

Asgar screwed the cap back on and walked toward the car.

---

A broad ramp led down into the technical hall. Asgar followed a building employee down it; the caseworker remained upstairs finishing a phone call.

It was cooler below. Pale stripes painted on the floor marked a route toward a door at the far end. Empty metal carts stood along one wall, and a coil of cable lay dark in a corner. The employee moved the nearest cart into the adjoining room and asked Asgar to remain by the entrance for now.

“How long has this been empty?” Asgar asked.

“Since spring. The equipment was moved.”

The man checked a side door and came back.

“No one unauthorized comes through here. If you notice anything, tell us immediately.”

Asgar looked at the ceiling. Concrete beams, pipes, lamps. After the street, the room felt too quiet.

He told the employee about the shortened tripod leg. The man listened, glanced at the remaining carts, and called someone over the radio. Within a few minutes those were taken out too.

The caseworker came down carrying a portable display. Someone behind her brought a table and two chairs. They placed them by the entrance, leaving the center of the hall clear.

“While we wait for the specialist, you can look through part of the archive,” she said. “You asked about the man at the stadium. We found the recording Kerimov referred to.”

Asgar sat.

The familiar corridor appeared on the screen.

He immediately recognized the spot where he had stood with his mobile. The picture was brighter than the one Rauf had shown him. The red wall of the booth was visible in the distance. Asgar leaned closer and placed both hands on either side of the display.

The booth door opened.

A man squeezed out and lingered by the structure. Then he walked down the corridor.

Asgar did not look at himself even though his own figure was visible now. He watched the man: lowered shoulders, careful step, a brief stop.

“Slower.”

The caseworker reduced the playback speed.

The man turned his head.

Asgar raised one hand.

“There. Stop.”

The face remained a small pale patch.

“Can you enlarge it?”

She magnified the area. The space around the head broke into blurred rectangles. The caseworker briefly restored the original scale.

The distortion did not occupy the entire corridor: closer to the bend, the tile seams became straight again, and the interference ended so abruptly it seemed to have an edge of its own.

Asgar stared until his eyes began to ache.

“After twenty-five years, you couldn’t do anything more with this?”

The caseworker opened the list of attachments to the recording.

“There are processed versions. I’ll show you the latest.”

She opened a second file. The outlines were clearer, but the face still would not resolve into a person anyone could identify.

“Anything else?”

“That’s all they managed.”

Asgar pushed the display away.

He did not know what he had expected. That the future would place a name beneath the blurred head. That the man would have been found, questioned, would have explained everything long ago, and all Asgar had to do was read the answer.

“Did they even look for him?”

The caseworker opened several documents.

“They did. They checked exits, adjacent cameras, witnesses. His identity was never established.”

Asgar looked sideways at the document list.

“And Kerimov?”

The caseworker found the relevant field.

“He led the case until 2019. Then he handed it over. But he came back to it twice after that, unofficially. The last entry is seven years after your disappearance.”

By then, Asgar himself had already been legally dead for a year.

He lowered his gaze to the edge of the table. The man who had told him *that’s why I haven’t closed the case* had kept it open after everyone else had closed it.

On the paused frame, the stranger was still looking toward the booth.

Asgar remembered stepping aside to let him pass. He could have asked whether the man needed help. Could have stopped him for a few seconds.

He reached for play and watched the segment again.

The man disappeared around the bend.

---

An archive employee brought over the research reports. He placed them on the table in electronic form and remained standing beside them while the caseworker opened the relevant section.

When she reached the medical record, the man asked Asgar to turn toward the light. Asgar did, expecting another photograph.

The employee did not take one.

He looked at the date on the report and took his hand away from the screen.

“I prepared these materials for transfer,” he said. “In spring. There were only records in the file.”

Asgar pulled his chair closer. He needed to read what they had learned over all those years.

The first item was a medical assessment from August twentieth. Asgar opened it and read one sentence twice: *No signs were found of a physiological state consistent with four days without water, food, or sleep.*

Asgar paged through several screens. The dates changed. The same images appeared among diagrams and tables. He stopped at words he understood and tried to assemble them into an answer.

“I need to know one thing,” he said. “Do you have a device like it? Anything that can send me back?”

The employee shook his head.

“We have no working device.”

“You don’t. What about anywhere else?”

“I don’t know. But not in these materials. They were never able to reproduce the transition.”

Asgar returned to the beginning of the document.

“You studied it.”

“They studied recordings and whatever traces remained after an appearance. The booth itself never appeared again after your disappearance.”

The man pointed to one section.

“These are attempts to reproduce the effect. They never managed to send either a person or an object to another time.”

Asgar read the line he indicated.

“And that’s it?”

The employee did not answer at once.

“For you, a few hours have passed. This work took years.”

Asgar looked up at him, then back at the display.

Surnames stood below. Dates of interim reports. Signatures beneath failed experiments.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I didn’t mean…”

He left the sentence unfinished and closed the document.

The caseworker checked a message.

“Ekaterina is almost here. Someone will meet her upstairs.”

Asgar turned toward the ramp.

For the first time since stepping out of the booth, he knew exactly where Katya would come from.

He stood, took several steps, and stopped beside the stripe on the floor. He wanted to meet her at the entrance, but looked at the empty center of the hall and stayed where he was.

“Tell her I’m here.”

“She knows.”

Asgar put a hand into his pocket and felt the dead phone. He took it out and ran a finger across the dark glass.

The ceiling lamp above the table reflected on the screen.

He tilted the phone.

The reflection doubled.

Asgar froze, then looked up.

The lamp itself hung where it had been.

But near the far wall, a thin yellow floor stripe had doubled in the same way: one line ran toward the door, the other lay beside it for a short stretch.

He blinked.

The two lines merged.

“Look over there,” Asgar said.

The employee turned.

For an instant, a red edge appeared on the concrete wall.

Asgar backed away from the center of the hall.

“Don’t bring Katya down yet.”
