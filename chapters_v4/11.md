Chapter 11. Things After Washing

On the third day, Asgar washed the T-shirt.

Before that, he had twice started explaining to the employee that he needed different clothes, and both times the conversation had drifted elsewhere. She showed him options, asked his size, clarified some conditions for issuing them. He answered, then put it off. It seemed enough to wait a little longer and the question would disappear on its own.

In the morning he smelled the collar and called her himself.

The woman brought two T-shirts and light trousers. She showed him where the laundry compartment opened beside the door, how to put clothes into it, where they would come back. Asgar asked her to repeat the last part.

"They'll return mine specifically?"

The translator relayed the question.

The woman looked at the T-shirt in his hands.

"Yes. Of course."

He folded it even though there was no reason to fold it before washing. The rubbed-in stain from the repair depot still remained on the fabric. Asgar ran a finger over it, then put the shirt into the compartment.

In the evening, the T-shirt came back clean.

He spread it out on the bed. Collar, shoulder seam, a small irregularity at the lower edge—everything was still there. The smell was gone.

Asgar folded it again and put it on a separate shelf.

---

Martin brought the recording of their first conversation after breakfast.

He came in, put the screen on the table, and immediately noticed the second chair.

"So they left the second chair after all."

Asgar pushed a mug toward him.

"There's coffee. I just couldn't figure out how to make it stronger."

Martin tasted it and looked at the mug.

"There's about one and a half times too much water in this."

"I increased everything I could find."

Martin showed him the correct setting on the panel, and they sat side by side.

There were far more pauses in the recording than Asgar remembered. He listened to his own voice, rushed questions, answers begun before the translation had finished. Several times he wanted to stop playback and explain what he had meant.

Martin let him finish, then returned to the recording.

"Here we keep your words. We'll add the clarification separately."

"Why not replace it? I just explained it properly."

"Because at that moment you said it differently. Later someone will compare the recording with the clarification and decide a piece went missing."

Asgar rubbed his eyes.

"Is everything this slow here?"

"After the case with the three 'independent' studies—yes. I've already over-condensed enough."

They reached the point where Asgar asked whether the impulse was connected with his presence. The short description beside it said: "Deviation registered after arrival of witness."

Asgar read the line again.

"That makes it sound as though I walked in and it happened immediately."

Martin pulled the screen closer.

"Yes. Badly written."

He added timestamps and showed him the corrected version.

Asgar reread it and nodded.

After that, Martin began bringing him short fragments of old recordings more often. At first he asked only that Asgar listen: had a word been recognized correctly, had two voices been confused, was it clear what "there" referred to.

Along with the fragments came requests for meetings. After the identity confirmation, Inessa sent the report to the research group; requests spread from there to other specialists. On the fourth day, Martin brought a list. Asgar began to read: examination, discussion of the transition, another discussion. One surname was familiar: Martin had shown him that person's work.

"These are all for me?"

"There were more. I removed the ones asking the same thing."

"When am I supposed to live?"

Martin took the list back.

"That's why I brought it to you first."

They kept one meeting for the following week. He sent refusals to the rest on Asgar's behalf and asked that new requests be coordinated through him. While they were finishing, another request arrived. Martin closed the list, and Asgar turned the work recording back on.

A few days later, Martin came later than usual and sat down without taking off the translator.

"I was asked why the refusals are coming from my name."

"And what did you say?"

"That the refusals aren't mine." He put the screen on the table but did not turn it on. "Part of the group thinks I'm holding you back. Another part thinks I'm wasting time altogether: one witness, nothing can be reproduced, nothing to work with."

Asgar moved the mug aside.

"And you?"

"For three days I've been explaining to people older than me the difference between 'we can't verify this' and 'this didn't happen.' That's usually my side of an argument." Martin rubbed his forehead. "Right now I'm on it alone."

"If this is causing trouble for you—"

"Because of you." He switched on the screen. "I'm not telling you so you'll feel sorry for me. I just have a review tomorrow about these refusals, and I'm going into it alone."

Asgar looked at the shortened list.

"The one whose work you showed me. Which side is he on?"

"The second," Martin said. "And on one point he's right: there's no way to reproduce it. But that doesn't imply anything else yet."

On the fifth day, Asgar stopped the recording before they were halfway through.

"He isn't promising to come in ten minutes."

Martin looked at the translation.

"That's what it says here."

"He says, 'I'm coming now.' That can mean anything. He hasn't even stood up yet."

In the video, the man sat sideways to the camera, talking on the phone. Papers lay on the table beside him; the conversation had nothing to do with the anomaly recording. Martin had found it in another archive where he was checking the work of an old recognition system.

"How do you know he didn't stand?" he asked.

Asgar pointed to the edge of the image.

"The chair. See? And his shoulder is in the same place. But I don't know about ten minutes. Maybe he really intended to be quick. He just didn't say it."

Martin removed the old transcript and marked the disputed section.

"That's exactly what we need. The system didn't get the words wrong. It added meaning the person never said."

He looked at Asgar.

"Would you like to do this a few hours a day?"

Asgar leaned back in the chair.

"You're offering me a job?"

"A trial one. Checking historical speech. Not only for our case. There's a lot of material the machine recognized smoothly and incorrectly. You notice where it stops translating the person and starts inventing for them."

"And then you'll check my work?"

"Of course."

"Great job."

Martin missed the sarcasm.

"Yes. If we can assemble the sample properly, we'll finally see exactly where the system begins to invent."

Asgar looked at him and only then realized he was answering seriously.

---

An employee from the accommodation service brought the terms.

Asgar expected a long discussion about his identity card. Instead, the man showed him a temporary record created after matching the archival data and explained which services were already available. The room remained assigned to Asgar. Food and basic items were covered within the allotted support. Work with the archive came with separate compensation.

Asgar listened through the room translator. Clear text ran across the screen, but some words had to be expanded to see an explanation.

"What if I refuse to work?"

"Nothing. The room stays."

"And food?"

"That too. The work is paid separately."

Asgar returned to the work terms. Read them again, then asked for the automatic inclusion of his personal accounts in the shared corpus to be removed.

The employee reread the clause.

"By meaning, this only applies to the materials you'll annotate. But it's badly written. I'll fix it now."

He sent the request and waited for the response right there in the room.

Asgar confirmed the terms after the revision.

Then he was issued a small work tablet. The employee showed the device number in the record and explained that it was being provided for temporary use. Asgar held it and set it on the table beside the dead phone.

The tablet was thinner. He could barely feel its weight.

That evening, Asgar opened the first paid fragment and stared at the start button for a long time.

Then he played the recording.

---

A routine appeared before he noticed it.

In the mornings, Asgar collected breakfast, opened the window onto the inner courtyard, and sat down to work. The exterior panels moved with a soft rustle. He already knew when the strip of sunlight would reach the edge of the table and moved his mug in advance.

After several recordings, his eyes began to hurt. Then he went out into the covered passage and walked to the junction beyond which the main building began. He still did not go farther without approval.

One day he saw two employees carrying food through the passage closed to him and followed them. He stopped by the door and called Inessa from the work tablet.

"Do I have to ask permission every time?"

She did not answer immediately. People moved behind her; the translation came through the tablet.

"For a new route—for now, yes."

"I've been here more than a week. Nothing appears."

"The sensors twitched again last night."

"Without the booth."

"Yes."

"Then what difference will one week make versus two? Three?"

Inessa looked away, answered someone, then looked back at him.

"All right. After lunch we'll walk it. I won't open the whole building, but we'll review this corridor."

Asgar put the tablet away.

He had already prepared several more objections; after her answer, none of them fit.

After lunch, Inessa really did come. They walked the outer route, avoiding occupied areas. She showed him two exits, checked how his communication worked, and left a new route on the tablet.

From the far end of the path, the sea was visible.

Asgar stopped.

The water lay beyond a broad band of structures he did not recognize. The shoreline curved differently, part of the space blocked by stepped terraces. But under the wind, the same small pale streaks moved across the surface.

Inessa waited beside him.

"Can I come here alone?" he asked.

"Yes. If the sensors catch anything, your tablet will scream before I do."

He nodded.

That evening, Asgar left the room for the first time without documents in his pocket. He had forgotten them in the drawer and remembered only by the sea.

He did not go back.

---

On the night of the tenth day, the tablet gave an alert.

Asgar woke before the translation began speaking. He sat up, hit his foot against a shoe, and swore. On the screen was a message from the duty group: brief measurement discrepancy, no visual appearance, remain in room until further notice.

He got dressed.

Phone, identity card, wallet. He checked everything with his hands, then stood by the door. Several times he leaned toward the small window overlooking the courtyard but saw only the lit path.

Twenty minutes later, a message came that the restriction had been lifted.

Asgar remained standing.

In the morning, he went to Martin and asked from the doorway:

"Why did they get me up if there was nothing?"

"Because last time a similar signal ended with a cabin."

"You understood the question."

Martin set the screen aside.

"I did. The duty team overreacted. And they wrote the message as though you needed to prepare for evacuation. That's bad."

"I already packed."

"I can see."

Asgar turned toward the window.

Martin was silent for a moment, then asked:

"Do you pack your things every time?"

"What am I supposed to do?"

"I'd probably pack too. But we've already had several signals like that without a cabin. So far they haven't learned how to distinguish them in advance."

Asgar looked for a long time at the yellowed leaf in the pot. Martin still had not removed it.

"I keep thinking I'll miss it," he said.

"The ring?"

"The chance."

Martin did not answer immediately.

Then he opened the recording of the nighttime event and showed how long it lasted. They watched the courtyard footage for the same interval. Empty path. Leaves moving. A person at the far end who walked past and noticed nothing.

The next night, Asgar left his clothes on the chair.

When the alert repeated several days later, he read the entire message and remained sitting on the bed. He still could not fall asleep afterward, but he did not put on his shoes.

---

Progress with the phone was slow.

Martin found a specialist in old devices. The specialist first asked for photographs of the case and connector. Asgar took them with the work tablet, checked every image, and sent them. Then he received a list of what would be needed for safe connection.

There was no ready-made cable. An adapter had to be prepared, the voltage checked, the battery condition assessed separately.

Then Inessa added a condition.

Asgar learned about it from Martin: she would not permit power to be applied to a device of unknown origin inside the main building. Not in the workshop, not in the laboratory, not in Asgar's room.

"It's just a phone."

"To her, it's an object that arrived with you," Martin said. "She's seen what happens to things that arrive with you."

Asgar went to her himself.

Inessa listened all the way through without interrupting and did not explain twice.

"Find an isolated room with a power break, then turn it on."

"And if they don't find one?"

"Then they don't find one."

"There are photographs of my family on it."

She did not look away.

"I know. The room is still required."

It took six days to find a separate room.

"They can open the case," Martin said. "But they won't without your permission."

Asgar turned the phone in his hand.

"For now, let them do the power."

"All right."

He put it back in his pocket.

A few days later, the specialist sent a message: preparation was finished, they could schedule the inspection. Asgar read it over breakfast and put the tablet face down.

That evening Martin asked whether he had chosen a time.

"Not yet."

"What's stopping you?"

Asgar looked at the dark glass.

"If there's nothing left on it..."

Martin sat on the second chair.

"Then don't schedule it yet. The phone isn't going anywhere."

Asgar ran a finger over the side button without pressing it.

Before, he had wanted to turn the phone on immediately. Now it might contain the last recordings in which the children were still moving, Katya was speaking off camera, and he himself did not know what would happen that evening at the stadium.

He put the phone in the drawer.

He scheduled the inspection for the end of the week.

---

On the fourteenth day, Asgar laughed for the first time so hard they had to stop the recording.

In an old work conversation, a man repeatedly said, "We're practically finished." A disassembled component lay on the table in front of him. Every new question revealed another task no one had even started.

The machine annotation confidently indicated a high degree of completion.

Asgar rewound.

"Listen," he said to Martin and played it again.

Martin listened through and looked at the annotation.

"Is there an error here?"

"There's a familiar person here."

"You knew him?"

"No. But I worked with him my whole life."

The translator hesitated on the sentence. Martin frowned at first, then understood and laughed.

Asgar laughed with him until he had to wipe his eyes.

Then the room went quiet.

He picked up the mug and drank. The coffee was cold.

Martin did not ask what had happened and did not try to bring the joke back. He opened the next fragment but left it paused.

Asgar sat a little longer.

"Go on," he said.

---

Now they sometimes ordered food together.

Martin came to Asgar's room after work because it was quieter there, and no one removed the second chair anymore. He brought whatever he planned to eat himself and usually remembered too late to warn Asgar about the taste.

One day Asgar bit into a thin flatbread with filling and immediately reached for water.

Martin stopped with one hand raised.

"I was going to tell you."

"Tell me first, then feed me."

"I honestly forgot this is spicy for you."

Asgar wiped his mouth.

"Of course. You eat it."

He pushed the plate away, then broke off another piece anyway, smaller this time.

They talked about Martin's work, about people who signed documents without reading them, about the plant on the windowsill. It turned out Martin watered it too often.

"Did you check?" Asgar asked.

"No. Someone told me, and I believed them."

Asgar looked at him.

"After your whole lecture about sources?"

Martin sighed and looked at the plant.

"Yes. That's exactly how it's dying."

Later, Asgar asked him to bring something similar to ordinary bread.

Martin asked what kind.

Asgar started describing the crust, the crumb, the smell of a hot bag, and suddenly remembered Katya's voice from the stadium: buy a large bottle of water on the way. And the cake waiting at home. He had said then that he was going straight home. Katya had been smiling; he could hear it even through the noise. He wanted to hear once more the word on which that smile had appeared, but all that remained in his head was the request about water.

He stopped in the middle of the sentence.

Martin waited.

"Something simple," Asgar finished. "Show me what there is."

---

On the eighteenth day, he went to the sea after work.

On the way, he bought a drink using the temporary account he had been issued. At first he chose the wrong size, went back, corrected it. The person behind the counter asked something. Asgar recognized the question and answered without the translator.

The answer came out awkwardly. The person asked again, but handed him the right cup.

Asgar walked away gripping it.

By the sea, he sat on a low parapet. The wind moved more freely here than between the buildings. His T-shirt stuck to his back, then cooled. Below, a small machine worked slowly, cleaning a drain. Its wheels left damp tracks.

He already knew what time the place became empty. Knew where there would be shade on the way back. Tomorrow he had four recordings left and a conversation with the phone specialist.

Asgar took out the work tablet and opened his messages.

Martin was asking what time to bring the bread.

He typed: "I'll be home in half an hour."

Read it.

The word remained on the screen.

Asgar deleted it and wrote: "I'll be back in half an hour."

He sent it, finished the drink, and sat by the water a little longer.

---

On the nineteenth day, he woke before the alert.

The room was cool. Asgar lay listening to the exterior panels turning. Then he got up and opened the compartment with clean clothes.

He took that T-shirt from the separate shelf.

Put it on and smoothed it over his stomach. The collar had curled slightly after washing, and Asgar fixed it in front of the mirror.

The phone inspection was scheduled for after lunch. He put the mobile on the table beside the work tablet, then put it in his pocket: that felt safer.

Martin came by breakfast. He brought bread wrapped in thin paper.

"I didn't make it yesterday. They only bake it in the morning."

Asgar unwrapped it.

The crust cracked beneath his fingers. He tore off an edge, smelled it, and nodded.

"This one."

Martin sat down.

They had time to pour coffee. Asgar opened the list of recordings and showed him a fragment in which he could not make out the last word. Martin suggested requesting another audio track if one had survived.

The tablet gave an alert.

Both looked at the screen.

Asgar read the first words and stopped chewing.

Inessa was calling.

He accepted the call. Her face appeared large; an open door was behind her, someone moving past quickly.

"Asgar, stay in your room for now. The object has appeared in another sector."

He stood.

"The booth?"

"Red cabin. We have images from two points. Sending them."

A technical passage opened on the screen. In the distance, beside a row of closed gates, stood a red body. Asgar saw the glass panels, the dark door, the edge of the roof.

"Far?" he asked.

"In another complex. Don't walk there. We're arranging a route now and checking what's happening around it."

Martin was already standing beside him.

"I'm with him," he said.

Inessa nodded.

"Wait for my message. Do not approach the object on your own."

The connection ended.

Asgar stared at the still image for a while. Then he checked the phone in his pocket, went to the drawer, and took out his identity card.

Martin did not hurry him.

"Put the bread away, please," Asgar said. "It'll dry out."

Martin folded the paper over the broken crust.

Asgar returned to the table and closed the work file. The four recordings he had planned to finish today remained for tomorrow.
