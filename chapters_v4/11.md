Chapter 11. Clothes After the Wash

On the third day, Asgar washed the T-shirt.

Before that, twice he had begun explaining to an employee that he needed different clothes, and both times the conversation drifted elsewhere. She showed him options, asked his size, clarified some conditions for issuing them. He answered, then put it off.

It felt as though if he waited just a little longer, the problem would disappear on its own.

That morning he smelled the collar and called her himself.

The woman brought two T-shirts and a pair of light trousers. She showed him how the laundry compartment opened beside the door, where to put the clothes, where they would be returned.

Asgar asked her to repeat the last part.

“They’ll give me back mine?”

The translator delivered the question.

The woman looked at the T-shirt in his hands.

“Yes. Of course.”

He folded it, although there was no reason to fold a shirt before washing it. The smeared patch from the maintenance yard was still visible on the fabric.

Asgar ran one finger over it, then put the shirt into the compartment.

It came back clean that evening.

He spread it out on the bed.

The collar, the seam at the shoulder, the small irregularity near the hem—everything was still there.

Only the smell was gone.

Asgar folded it again and put it on a separate shelf.

---

Martin brought the record of their first conversation after breakfast.

He came in, set a display on the table, and immediately noticed the second chair.

“They left the second chair after all.”

Asgar pushed a mug toward him.

“There’s coffee. I couldn’t figure out how to make it stronger.”

Martin tasted it and looked at the mug.

“There’s about one and a half times too much water in this.”

“I increased everything I could find.”

Martin showed him the right setting on the panel, and they sat beside each other.

There were far more pauses in the recording than Asgar remembered.

He listened to his own voice, the rushed questions, answers begun before translation had finished. Several times he wanted to stop playback and explain what he had meant.

Martin let him finish each time, then returned to the recording.

“Here we keep your original words. The clarification goes separately.”

“Why not replace it? I just explained it properly.”

“Because at that moment, you said it differently. Later someone will compare the recording with the clarification and decide a piece went missing.”

Asgar rubbed his eyes.

“Is everything this slow with you?”

“Since the episode with the three ‘independent’ studies? Yes. I’ve done enough over-compressing.”

They reached the point where Asgar had asked whether the impulse was connected with his presence. The summary beside it read: *Deviation registered after witness arrival.*

Asgar read the line again.

“That sounds like I walked in and it happened immediately.”

Martin pulled the display closer.

“Yes. Badly written.”

He added timestamps and showed Asgar the revised version.

Asgar reread it and nodded.

After that, Martin began bringing him short fragments of old recordings more often. At first he asked only for listening checks: had a word been recognized correctly, had two voices been confused, was it clear what “there” referred to?

Along with the fragments came requests for meetings.

Once Asgar’s identity was confirmed, Inessa sent the assessment to the research group; from there, requests spread to other specialists.

On the fourth day, Martin brought a list.

Asgar began reading: examination, interview about the transition, another interview. One surname was familiar—Martin had shown him that person’s work.

“All of this is for me?”

“There were more. I removed the ones asking the same things.”

“When am I supposed to live?”

Martin took the list back.

“That’s why I brought it to you first.”

They kept one meeting for the following week. Martin sent refusals to the others on Asgar’s behalf and asked that new requests be coordinated through him.

While they were finishing, another request arrived.

Martin closed the list, and Asgar started the work recording again.

A few days later, Martin came later than usual and sat down without removing the translator.

“I was asked why the refusals are coming from my name.”

“What did you say?”

“That they aren’t my refusals.” He put the display on the table but did not turn it on. “Part of the group thinks I’m keeping you to myself. Another part thinks I’m wasting time entirely: one witness, nothing reproducible, nothing to work with.”

Asgar set his mug aside.

“And you?”

“For three days I’ve been explaining to people older than me the difference between ‘we can’t verify this’ and ‘this didn’t happen.’ Usually that’s my side of the argument.” Martin rubbed his forehead. “Right now I’m standing there alone.”

“If this is causing you trouble because of me—”

“It is because of you.” He switched on the display. “I’m not telling you so you’ll feel sorry for me. I just have a review tomorrow over these refusals, and I’ll be going into it alone.”

Asgar looked at the shortened list.

“The one whose work you showed me. Which side is he on?”

“The second,” Martin said. “And he’s right about one thing: we have nothing that can reproduce it. Nothing else follows from that yet.”

On the fifth day, Asgar stopped a recording before they reached the middle.

“He isn’t promising to come in ten minutes.”

Martin looked at the translation.

“That’s exactly what it says here.”

“He says, ‘I’m coming.’ That can mean anything. He hasn’t even stood up.”

In the video, a man sat sideways to the camera talking on the phone. Documents lay on the table beside him; the conversation had nothing to do with the anomaly records. Martin had found it in another archive while checking the performance of an old recognition system.

“How do you know he didn’t stand up?” Martin asked.

Asgar pointed to the edge of the frame.

“The chair. See? And his shoulder’s in the same place. But I don’t know about ten minutes. Maybe he really meant to hurry. He just didn’t say it.”

Martin removed the old transcript and marked the disputed section.

“This is exactly what we need. The system didn’t get the words wrong. It added meaning the speaker never expressed.”

He looked at Asgar.

“Would you like to do this for a few hours a day?”

Asgar leaned back.

“Are you offering me a job?”

“A trial. Historical speech verification. Not only material from our case. There’s a lot the machine transcribed smoothly and incorrectly. You notice where it stops translating the person and starts thinking for them.”

“And then you’ll check my work?”

“Of course.”

“Wonderful job.”

Martin missed the sarcasm.

“Yes. If we build the sample properly, we may finally see exactly where the system starts inventing.”

Asgar looked at him and only then realized he was answering seriously.

---

An employee from the accommodation service brought the terms.

Asgar expected a long conversation about his identity document.

Instead, the man showed him a temporary record created after the archival match and explained which services were already available. The room remained assigned to Asgar. Food and basic items were covered within the approved allowance.

The archival work would be paid separately.

Asgar listened through the room translator. The text on the display was understandable, though he had to open explanations for some words.

“What if I refuse the work?”

“Nothing. The room remains yours.”

“And the food?”

“That too. The work is separate and paid separately.”

Asgar returned to the employment terms. He read them once more, then asked to remove the automatic inclusion of his personal accounts in the shared corpus.

The employee reread the clause.

“By intent, this only applies to material you annotate. But it’s written badly. I’ll correct it now.”

He sent a revision request and waited for the response in the room.

Asgar approved the terms after the change.

Then they issued him a small work tablet. The employee showed him the device number on the receipt and explained that it was being provided for temporary use.

Asgar held it, then placed it on the table beside the dead phone.

The tablet was thinner.

He barely felt its weight.

That evening, Asgar opened his first paid fragment and stared at the play button for a long time.

Then he started the recording.

---

A routine appeared before he noticed it happening.

In the mornings Asgar collected breakfast, opened the window toward the inner courtyard, and sat down to work. The exterior panels moved with a quiet rustle. He already knew when the stripe of sunlight would reach the edge of the desk and moved his mug before it got there.

After several recordings, his eyes began to hurt.

Then he would go into the covered passage and walk to the junction beyond which the shared building began. For now, he did not go farther without authorization.

One day he saw two employees carrying food through a passage closed to him and followed them. He stopped at the door and called Inessa from the work tablet.

“Do I have to ask permission every time?”

She did not answer immediately. People moved behind her; the translation came from the tablet.

“For a new route, for now, yes.”

“I’ve been here more than a week. Nothing appears.”

“The sensors jumped again last night.”

“No booth.”

“No.”

“Then what changes if one week becomes two? Three?”

Inessa looked away, answered someone nearby, then turned back to him.

“All right. After lunch we’ll walk it. I won’t open the whole building, but we’ll reassess that corridor.”

Asgar put the tablet away.

He had prepared several more arguments.

After her answer, none of them applied.

After lunch, Inessa actually came.

They used an outer route, bypassing occupied areas. She showed him two exits, checked how his communication link worked, and added the new route to his tablet.

From the far end of the path, the sea was visible.

Asgar stopped.

Water lay beyond a broad band of structures he did not recognize. The shoreline curved differently, and stepped terraces hid part of the view.

But the wind drew the same small bright streaks across the surface.

Inessa waited beside him.

“Can I come here alone?”

“Yes. If the sensors catch anything, your tablet will start screaming before I do.”

He nodded.

That evening Asgar left his room for the first time without his documents in his pocket.

He had forgotten them in the drawer and remembered only when he reached the sea.

He did not go back for them.

---

On the tenth night, the tablet sounded an alert.

Asgar woke before the translation began speaking. He sat up, struck a foot against one shoe, and swore.

A message from the duty team filled the screen: brief measurement discrepancy, no visual appearance, remain in the room until further notice.

He got dressed.

Phone, identity card, wallet.

He checked all three by hand, then stood beside the door. Several times he bent toward the small window overlooking the courtyard, but saw only the lit path.

Twenty minutes later a message said the restriction had been lifted.

Asgar remained standing.

In the morning he went to Martin and asked from the doorway:

“Why did they wake me up if there was nothing?”

“Because last time a similar signal ended with a booth.”

“You understood the question.”

Martin set his display aside.

“I did. The duty team overreacted. And the message was written as though you needed to prepare for evacuation. That’s bad.”

“I already packed.”

“I can see that.”

Asgar turned toward the window.

Martin was quiet for a moment, then asked:

“Do you pack your things every time?”

“What am I supposed to do?”

“I’d probably pack too. But we’ve already had several of these signals without a booth. They don’t know how to distinguish them in advance yet.”

Asgar stared for a long time at the yellowed leaf in the plant pot.

Martin still had not removed it.

“I keep thinking I’m going to miss it,” Asgar said.

“The ring?”

“The chance.”

Martin did not answer immediately.

Then he opened the record of the night’s event and showed how long it had lasted.

They watched the courtyard footage over the same interval.

An empty path.

Leaves moving.

A person at the far end who walked through without noticing anything.

The next night Asgar left his clothes on the chair.

When the alert repeated several days later, he read the message all the way through and remained sitting on the bed.

He still could not get back to sleep.

But he did not put on his shoes.

---

Progress with the phone was slow.

Martin found a specialist in old devices. First the specialist asked for photographs of the casing and connector. Asgar took them with the work tablet, checked every image, and sent them.

Then he received a list of what would be needed for a safe connection.

There was no ready-made cable.

An adapter would have to be built, the voltage checked, the battery condition assessed separately.

Then Inessa added a condition.

Asgar learned about it from Martin: she would not allow power to be applied to an object of unknown origin inside the main building.

Not in the workshop.

Not in the laboratory.

Not in Asgar’s room.

“It’s a phone.”

“To her, it’s an object that arrived with you,” Martin said. “She’s seen what happens to things that arrive with you.”

Asgar went to see her himself.

Inessa heard him out without interrupting and did not explain herself twice.

“Find a separate room with isolated power, then turn it on.”

“And if they don’t find one?”

“Then they don’t.”

“My family photos are on it.”

She did not look away.

“I know. You still need the room.”

It took six days to find one.

“They may need to open the casing,” Martin said. “But not without your permission.”

Asgar turned the phone in his hand.

“For now, just prepare the power.”

“All right.”

He put it back in his pocket.

A few days later the specialist sent a message: preparation was complete and an inspection could be scheduled.

Asgar read it over breakfast and put the tablet face down.

That evening Martin asked whether he had chosen a time.

“Not yet.”

“What’s stopping you?”

Asgar looked at the dark glass.

“If there’s nothing left on it…”

Martin sat in the second chair.

“Then don’t schedule it yet. The phone isn’t going anywhere.”

Asgar ran a finger along the side button without pressing it.

Before, he had wanted the phone switched on immediately.

Now it might contain the last recordings in which the children still moved, Katya spoke from behind the camera, and Asgar himself had no idea what would happen that evening at the stadium.

He put the phone into the drawer.

He scheduled the inspection for the end of the week.

---

On the fourteenth day, Asgar laughed for the first time hard enough that they had to stop the recording.

In an old work conversation, a man repeated several times: “We’re practically finished.”

A disassembled part lay on the table in front of him.

Every new question uncovered another task no one had started.

The machine annotation confidently assigned a high completion level.

Asgar wound the recording back.

“Listen,” he told Martin, and played it again.

Martin listened to the end and looked at the annotation.

“Is the label wrong?”

“That’s a familiar person.”

“You knew him?”

“No. But I worked with him my entire life.”

The translator hesitated over the sentence.

Martin frowned at first, then understood and laughed.

Asgar laughed with him until he had to wipe his eyes.

Then the room went quiet.

He picked up his mug and drank.

The coffee was cold.

Martin did not ask what had happened and did not try to bring the joke back. He opened the next fragment but left it paused.

Asgar sat for a little longer.

“Go on,” he said.

---

Now, sometimes, they ordered food together.

Martin came to Asgar’s room after work because it was quieter there, and no one removed the second chair anymore. He brought whatever he had planned to eat himself and usually remembered too late to warn Asgar about the flavor.

Once, Asgar bit into a thin filled flatbread and reached for water at once.

Martin froze with one hand raised.

“I meant to warn you.”

“Warn me first. Feed me second.”

“I really forgot this is spicy for you.”

Asgar wiped his mouth.

“Of course. You eat it.”

He pushed the plate away, then broke off another piece anyway, smaller this time.

They talked about Martin’s work, about people who signed documents without reading them, about the plant on the windowsill.

It turned out Martin watered it too often.

“Did you check?” Asgar asked.

“No. Someone told me and I believed them.”

Asgar looked at him.

“After your entire lecture about sources?”

Martin sighed and looked at the plant.

“Yes. This is exactly how it dies.”

Later, Asgar asked him to bring something like ordinary bread.

Martin asked what kind.

Asgar began describing the crust, the crumb, the smell of a warm bag—and suddenly remembered Katya’s voice from the stadium: buy a large bottle of water on the way.

And the cake waiting at home.

He had said he was coming straight home.

Katya had smiled; he could hear it even through the noise.

He wanted to hear again the word she had smiled on, but only the request about water remained in his head.

He stopped in the middle of the sentence.

Martin waited.

“Something simple,” Asgar finished. “Show me what there is.”

---

On the eighteenth day, he went to the sea after work.

On the way he bought a drink using the temporary account they had issued him. At first he chose the wrong size, went back, corrected it. The person at the counter asked something.

Asgar recognized the question and answered without the translator.

His answer came out awkward.

The person asked him to repeat it, but handed him the correct cup.

Asgar walked away holding it tightly.

At the sea, he sat on a low parapet. The wind moved more freely here than between the buildings. His T-shirt stuck to his back, then cooled.

Below, a small machine moved slowly along a drain, cleaning it. Its wheels left damp tracks.

He already knew when this place would become quiet.

Knew where the shade would fall on the way back.

Tomorrow he had four recordings left and a conversation with the phone specialist.

Asgar took out the work tablet and opened his messages.

Martin was asking what time to bring the bread.

He typed: *I’ll be home in half an hour.*

Read it.

The word remained on the screen.

Asgar deleted it and wrote: *I’ll be back in half an hour.*

Sent it.

Finished his drink and sat by the water a little longer.

---

On the nineteenth day, he woke before the alert.

The room was cool. Asgar lay listening to the exterior panels turning.

Then he got up and opened the compartment with the clean clothes.

He took that T-shirt from its separate shelf.

Put it on and smoothed it over his stomach. The collar had curled slightly after washing, and Asgar straightened it in front of the mirror.

The phone inspection was scheduled for after lunch.

He put the mobile on the table beside the work tablet, then moved it to his pocket.

That felt safer.

Martin came for breakfast. He brought bread wrapped in thin paper.

“I couldn’t get it yesterday. They only make it in the morning.”

Asgar unwrapped it.

The crust cracked beneath his fingers.

He broke off an end, smelled it, and nodded.

“This one.”

Martin sat.

They had time to pour coffee. Asgar opened the list of recordings and showed him a fragment where he could not make out the final word. Martin suggested requesting another audio track if one survived.

The tablet sounded an alert.

Both of them looked at the screen.

Asgar read the first words and stopped chewing.

Inessa was calling.

He accepted.

Her face filled the display; an open door was behind her, with someone moving quickly past.

“Asgar, stay in your room for now. An object has appeared in another sector.”

He stood.

“The booth?”

“A red booth. We have images from two locations. Sending them now.”

A technical passage opened on the screen.

In the distance, beside a row of closed gates, stood a red structure.

Asgar saw the glass panes, the dark door, the roof edge.

“How far?” he asked.

“Another complex. Don’t try to walk there. We’re arranging a route and checking the surrounding area now.”

Martin was already standing beside him.

“I’m with him,” he said.

Inessa nodded.

“Wait for my message. Do not approach the object independently.”

The connection ended.

Asgar stared at the still image for a while.

Then he checked the phone in his pocket, went to the drawer, and took out his identity card.

Martin did not hurry him.

“Put the bread away, please,” Asgar said. “It’ll go stale.”

Martin folded the paper over the broken crust.

Asgar went back to the desk and closed the work file.

The four recordings he had planned to finish today would wait until tomorrow.
