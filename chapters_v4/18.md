Chapter 18. By Word of Mouth

The next day, Tyra came when Asgar was already sitting by the open door with his shoes on.

“Have you been waiting long?”

“No.”

It was a lie.

Asgar had woken to the sound of boxes being moved, washed, checked the dressing, and since then had gone out several times to see whether the tables had been set.

The translator lay on his knees.

He had not needed it during the night, but without it Asgar did not even dare ask for breakfast.

Tyra took the plate and led him into the courtyard.

After they ate, she showed him where he could move without an escort: the tables beneath the shade, the water, his room, and the path between them.

Beyond that began work areas and residential courtyards.

“What if I need something?”

“Call someone. Or wait for me.”

Asgar looked at the people beside the walls.

The woman from his room was already working with the frames again. The same boxes they had carried out yesterday stood beside her.

“Can I help here?”

Tyra shook her head.

“Not yet.”

“I’m sitting around anyway.”

“Then sit around. Arved wants to verify your account first.”

Closer to midday, Tyra returned to tell him they would not be going to the archive today.

The woman who had been given Asgar’s information had asked for time: some of the material was stored elsewhere, and the copies available here needed to be checked.

“Did she find anything?”

“She found something. She doesn’t want to show it until she understands where it came from.”

Asgar looked toward the workroom.

“How long am I supposed to sit here?”

“I don’t know.”

“Today?”

“Today, definitely.”

He remained beneath the shade.

By evening he already knew who came to collect water, when the tables were moved, and when to pull in his feet because a cart was coming through the courtyard.

On the second day, Tyra allowed him to walk through the entire outer courtyard on his own, but he still stayed away from the work installations and residential passages.

People gradually stopped turning toward him every time, though unfamiliar faces still lingered longer than usual at the entrance.

Asgar asked several times whether he could help.

Only the wording of the answer changed:

archive first.

By the third day, he collected his own water and sat where he would not block anyone.

He caught himself counting the boxes by the wall and remembering which ones had stood elsewhere that morning.

On the fourth morning, Tyra came for him empty-handed.

“We can go now. Bring your documents.”

---

Saima was waiting in a room whose windows looked onto a narrow passage.

Cups were nested inside one another on the windowsill.

Beside them lay an open box filled with small objects, each seated in its own recess. Asgar could not tell what they were for.

A matte tabletop display occupied the middle of the desk. Text filled it, with markers arranged along the edges, too small to distinguish from the doorway.

Saima stood, moved a folded wrap from the free chair, and indicated where he should sit.

Tyra remained by the door.

“I have to go back. I’ll get you when you’re finished.”

Asgar looked at the translator in his hand.

“There’s one here,” Saima said.

Russian came from the desk.

The voice resembled the one he had begun to get used to, but the words followed one another more evenly.

Tyra left.

Asgar put his documents on the table.

Saima asked permission to capture an image, waited for his agreement, and moved a small device over them.

The document itself remained on the table.

She enlarged the image, checked several lines, then asked Asgar to state his name and read the date.

“Does this prove I’m not lying?”

“No. First I want to understand what kind of document it is.”

Saima returned it.

Asgar placed it in the inside pocket of his jacket.

“Arved gave me your account before I searched. Good decision. Now at least I can see which details you gave independently and which you might have seen here.”

“What did you find?”

She turned part of the table display toward him.

A figure appeared beside a red rectangle.

Asgar leaned forward.

It was a drawing.

Long clothing. A face made from several dark strokes. One raised hand.

The red door stood separately, without walls; the artist had placed a pale circle behind it.

“What is this?”

“A late illustration to a retelling. The title is roughly *The Man from the Red Door.*”

He stared at the raised hand.

“Is that supposed to be me?”

“Maybe. But the picture was made much later. I wouldn’t identify anyone from it.”

Saima removed the image.

“Have you ever seen it before?”

Asgar shook his head.

“Never.”

She made a note.

He was still looking at the space where the figure had been.

He wanted her to bring it back, enlarge the face, even though the artist had barely drawn eyes.

Saima opened a text.

“Now we go backward. As long as the chain can still be traced.”

---

The first fragment was short.

Saima started the translation, and Asgar heard a story about a man who disappeared from a place of gathering, returned several days later, and claimed almost no time had passed for him.

Neither his name nor the city appeared in the text.

“Stadium,” Asgar said.

Saima stopped playback.

“You told Arved that. Good. It was already in your account.”

She placed two sections side by side.

One was from Asgar’s statement, the other archival.

He could read neither, but he could see the matching markers she highlighted.

“It says ‘place of gathering’ here?” he asked.

“Here, yes. In an earlier copy it said a sports facility. Then the text was translated and shortened for a collection. The city had already disappeared before that.”

“What about the first record?”

“Didn’t survive. The chain breaks.”

Saima opened several documents.

Lines with dates appeared between them.

Asgar watched her move from one to another: in some places, an entire paragraph survived; elsewhere only a title and a reference to a source no longer accessible.

She did not tell the whole story at once.

She went back, checked, sometimes lingered over a single word.

Once she stopped the translator and angrily deleted an entire sentence.

“What did it say?”

“The translator invented that the man summoned the door himself. The text doesn’t say that. It can just as well mean ‘the door appeared in his presence.’”

Asgar rubbed a palm over his cheek.

“I didn’t summon it.”

“I’ll note that beside it. Separately from the old text.”

The next source mentioned a wife who waited for her vanished husband.

In a later retelling, she waited by the door for many years.

Asgar’s fingers tightened around the edge of the table.

“She did not stand by the door.”

Saima stopped.

“The earlier version doesn’t say she did. There are two returns in it, with years between them. Someone later merged them into one.”

“Can we go earlier?”

She opened another text.

The translation was uneven.

A man returned after a short disappearance, then vanished again.

Years later his identity was verified.

His wife was alive.

Asgar listened without interrupting.

There was no screen on which he first saw an older Katya, no room with a glass partition, no feel of her hands when they embraced.

Only several lines about identity verification and a relative who had been located.

He held the movement of her hand in memory.

That day, her fingers had stopped sooner beside her ear because her hair was short.

Whenever he tried to picture Katya whole, her hair became long again.

Asgar returned to the movement of the hand before he could see her as she had actually sat before the camera.

“That happened,” he said.

Saima waited.

“It was more complicated. But this part—yes.”

She marked the fragment without immediately asking another question.

Asgar leaned closer.

“Does it say what happened to her afterward?”

Saima looked at the neighboring field, then returned to the text.

“No. Not in these materials.”

He nodded.

In 2191, he had already been shown records.

But as soon as he heard the word *wife,* he waited for her voice again.

Saima did not close the fragment immediately.

She returned to the source from which it had been assembled and opened its references.

Asgar watched her follow one after another, closing most almost immediately.

At the fourth or fifth, she stopped.

“This contains somebody’s actual words,” she said. “Not a retelling. The author cites a search report and quotes it.”

“Whose words?”

“The wife’s.”

Asgar did not move.

Saima first checked the provenance of the quotation and noted that the original report had not survived.

Then she opened the translation.

The line was short.

“He said he would stop for water. He never bought the water.”

For a moment Asgar could not look away from the surface.

He remembered standing in the concrete concourse and promising:

a big bottle, home.

How she had answered that the important thing was for the water to make it home too.

Then he had turned into the service passage because it was quieter.

“That’s her,” he said.

“I can’t confirm that,” Saima replied. “The report is gone. The quotation may be exact, may be shortened, may have passed through two translations.”

“That’s her.”

Saima looked at him.

“All right. I’ll record that you recognized the wording. Separately from what I can prove.”

Asgar asked her to leave the line open.

Saima did and moved on to the next document.

He sat there until the unfamiliar symbols surrounding the quotation began to look almost legible simply from staring at them.

---

A later appearance was described in much greater detail.

A different society. A different system of dates. Researchers trying to understand a man who had scarcely changed over an enormous span of time.

Saima showed him a surviving extract from a technical description.

Asgar recognized the room with equipment and the characteristic damage near the booth.

He saw a name that had passed through another writing system and back into translation and asked her to pronounce it again.

“Martin?”

“Most likely. But the name passed through several records and the readings differ.”

He told her who Martin was.

He faltered when he reached the bread.

Saima did not stop him.

“He helped me,” Asgar finished. “We talked. Not only because of the booth.”

She added a note beside the name.

“Here he survives only as a participant in the research.”

Asgar leaned back.

His right leg had gone stiff after so long in the chair. He extended it beneath the table and carefully straightened the knee.

Saima noticed and moved aside a box that was in his way.

“Break?”

“No. Show me the rest.”

There was a reference to a ruined urban area and a red structure near still-functioning old machines.

There were too few details.

Asgar could not tell whether it described the place he had just left.

“It could be,” he said. “I can’t recognize it.”

Saima left the fragment unmarked.

Outside the window, someone rolled a cart past.

One wheel knocked more often than the others; the sound approached, lingered below the window, and gradually faded.

Asgar looked at the bright line between the shutters.

“And after that?”

“There are texts. But after the ruins, the chain almost falls apart.”

“After the ruins.”

Saima returned to the full sequence.

“Some may be about you. There’s no longer any way to prove it.”

“But reports exist?”

“Yes. Some have other red doors. Some don’t mention a person at all. And half of them are just rewriting this collection.”

She opened the familiar drawing again.

Asgar looked at it.

The artist had given the man a straight back, long clothing, a confidently raised hand.

No dirty trousers.

No exhaustion.

No borrowed jacket with sleeves too short.

“He made it convenient,” Asgar said.

The translator rendered the sentence.

Saima looked at the image.

“What exactly?”

“The man knows where he’s going.”

She read the caption.

“That is exactly how they made him.”

---

A research note survived as an appendix to a late copy.

Its author argued against the interpretation that the man consciously chose the dates of his appearances.

Saima read the translation aloud, stopping wherever she doubted the meaning.

The researcher pointed to the man’s confusion in the early testimonies, his requests for help, the damage near the red structure.

In his view, those details fit poorly with the story of controlled travel.

He proposed that the man had become connected to the phenomenon accidentally and did not know how to end that connection.

Asgar listened bent over the table.

“Who wrote this?”

Saima showed the name.

“The name survived. The paper itself didn’t. We have only this fragment because another author argued against it.”

“Why?”

“He thought the man’s fear was part of a ritual.”

Asgar laughed once.

“A ritual.”

He repeated the word more quietly and rubbed a hand over his face.

Saima waited, then brought back the research note.

“He was close,” Asgar said. “The first one.”

“In what way?”

“I went inside because I was trying to talk on the phone. I needed somewhere quiet. There was no ritual.”

Saima checked Arved’s record.

“Good. You mentioned the phone before you saw this text.”

Asgar asked her to read the final lines again.

The researcher admitted there was insufficient evidence to reconstruct the cause.

Then came a reference to a lost section.

“That’s it?”

“That’s it. After this, a hole.”

He looked at the broken text.

Some stranger, living at a time Asgar did not know, had almost managed to see him as he really was:

frightened, confused, not knowing what to do.

And less of that stranger had survived than of the drawing with its confidently raised hand.

Saima closed the appendix.

In its place appeared the final section of the retelling.

The translator began reading, and Asgar heard that the traveler returned to the first door.

“Wait.”

Saima stopped the audio.

“Again.”

The sentence sounded once more.

He returned to the door where the journey had begun and left his wandering behind it.

Asgar moved his chair closer.

“Where did that come from?”

Saima opened an earlier version.

Asgar searched the translation for the familiar sentence.

The story ended sooner.

“Added later,” she said, and opened the borrowed source.

That source also contained a wanderer and a return home.

No booth.

No disappearances.

Saima showed the date: the text had existed long before it was attached to the story of the Red Door.

“In one translation it’s the gate of the home, in another a threshold. That’s where the first door came from.”

“Maybe the author still knew something?” Asgar asked.

“Maybe. But this exact ending already existed in a different text. We can see that.”

“That doesn’t prove I won’t return.”

“No.”

He stared at the final lines.

He wanted to leave them there.

Ask for a copy.

Take them away and read them in the evening.

Everything Mirena could tell him about returning was uncertain.

Here, at least, there was an ending in which the man went back.

“Will this be in your conclusion?”

“I’ll write that the ending was borrowed from an older text.”

Asgar did not answer for a long time.

Then he moved his chair back, opening space between himself and the table.

“Yes. Write it that way.”

Saima removed the story translation.

What remained were documents, references, gaps between copies.

Asgar picked up the cup of water she had put beside him during the reading.

It had gone warm.

---

Tyra returned near the end of the conversation.

Arved came with her.

Saima did not retell the legend to them.

She showed the matches with the account recorded before the search, explained which materials depended on one another and where the source chain broke.

“Identity established?” Arved asked.

“No. No confirmed face. But he named details in advance that we later found in old material.”

Arved read the highlighted sections.

“What about images?”

Saima opened a small, faded frame.

Asgar recognized the general view of the stadium, but the face of the man beside the booth broke into patches.

He leaned closer, trying to make out more.

“Do you have anything better?”

“No. On this copy there is no face, only blotches. And forget the drawings for identification.”

“Enlarge it.”

Saima enlarged the frame.

The face became larger, but no clearer.

Asgar stared at the patches for several more seconds, then leaned back.

Saima closed the image.

Arved read the assessment for a while.

Then he turned to Asgar.

“Stay away from shared installations for now. You can move around the outer courtyard on your own: tables, water, this room. I’ll tell people.”

Asgar nodded.

“Will I get another room?”

“Sleep in this one tonight. Tomorrow we’ll find you a proper one.”

Now he could reach the tables and workroom without Tyra.

Saima asked permission to preserve his explanations with the verification record.

He agreed.

When asked to confirm his name, he hesitated again: over the course of several hours it had been spoken so many times in this room that it had begun to feel like another label on the table.

Tyra waited for him at the door.

It was hot outside.

Asgar opened his jacket but did not remove it; his documents were inside.

They walked several steps in silence.

“Find anything?” Tyra asked.

Asgar looked at the translator.

“Something about myself.”

“Good?”

He remembered the ending of the story.

The first door someone else had already led him back to on paper.

“I don’t know.”

At the workroom, the dark-haired woman was moving boxes into the shade.

She saw Asgar, called to Tyra, and pointed to the top one.

The translator gave a short request: the top box needed to be placed separately.

Asgar looked at Tyra.

“Can I help now?”

Tyra translated.

The dark-haired woman looked Asgar over, then looked at his knee and shifted an empty space beside the wall toward him.

“Light things,” Tyra translated. “If you want.”

Asgar picked up the top box and set it where she had indicated.

The woman checked that he had not struck the neighboring boxes, then handed him one frame.

She ran a finger along its edge, turned it toward the light, and put it into an empty container.

“Damaged ones separately?”

She nodded.

Tyra was already preparing to leave.

“I’ll take the translator after lunch.”

Asgar sat on a free stool.

The woman placed two more frames in front of him and returned to her own table.
