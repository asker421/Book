Chapter 18. From Mouth to Mouth

The next day Taira came when Asgar was already sitting by the open door with his boots on.

"Have you been waiting long?"

"No."

That was a lie. Asgar had woken to the sound of boxes being moved, washed, checked the bandage, and since then had gone out several times to see whether the tables had been set. The translator rested on his knees. He had not needed it during the night, but without it Asgar did not even dare ask for breakfast.

Taira took the plate and led him into the courtyard.

After the meal, she showed him where he could be without an escort: the tables beneath the canopy, the water, the room, and the passage between them. Beyond that began work areas and residential courtyards.

"And if I need something?"

"Call someone. Or wait for me."

Asgar looked at the people by the walls. The woman from his room was already working with the frames again. The same boxes they had carried out yesterday stood beside her.

"Can I help here?"

Taira shook her head.

"Not yet."

"I'm sitting around anyway."

"Then sit. Arved wants to check your story first."

Closer to noon, Taira came back to say they would not go to the archive today. The woman who had been given Asgar's information had asked for time: some materials were in another repository, and the available copies had to be checked.

"Did she find anything?"

"She found something. She doesn't want to show it yet until she understands where each thing came from."

Asgar looked at the workroom.

"And how long am I supposed to sit here?"

"I don't know."

"Today?"

"Today definitely here."

He stayed beneath the canopy.

By evening he already knew who came for water, what time the tables were moved, and where to pull in his feet when a cart passed through the courtyard. On the second day, Taira allowed him to walk around the entire outer courtyard on his own, but he still did not approach the work installations or residential passages. People gradually stopped turning to look at him every time, though unfamiliar faces still paused at the entrance longer than usual.

Asgar asked several times whether help was needed. Only the wording of the answer changed: archive first.

On the third day, he fetched water himself and sat where he would not obstruct the passage. He caught himself counting the boxes by the wall and remembering which ones had stood somewhere else that morning.

On the fourth morning, Taira came for him empty-handed.

"Now we can go. Bring your documents."

---

Saima was waiting in a room whose windows overlooked a narrow passage.

Cups were stacked one inside another on the windowsill. Beside them was an open box with small objects, each in its own recess. Asgar could not tell what they were for. A matte surface covered the center of the table with text on it; markers were arranged along the edge, too small to make out from the doorway.

Saima stood, removed a rolled cloak from the free chair, and showed him where to sit.

Taira remained by the door.

"I need to go back. I'll collect you after the conversation."

Asgar looked at the translator in his hand.

"There's one here," Saima said.

Russian speech came from the table. The voice resembled the one he had begun to get used to, but the words followed one another more evenly.

Taira left.

Asgar placed the documents in front of him. Saima asked permission to capture an image, waited for his consent, and moved a small device toward them. The document itself remained on the table. She enlarged the image, checked several lines, then asked Asgar to say his name and read the date.

"Will this prove I'm not lying?"

"No. First I want to understand what kind of document this is at all."

Saima returned it. Asgar put the document back in the inner pocket of his jacket.

"Arved gave me your account before I searched. Good decision. Now at least we can see which details you gave yourself and which you could have seen here."

"What did you find?"

She turned part of the table surface toward him. A figure appeared beside a red rectangle.

Asgar leaned forward.

It was a drawing. Long clothing, a face represented by a few dark strokes, one hand raised. The red door stood separately without walls; behind it, the artist had placed a pale circle.

"What is this?"

"A later illustration to a retelling. It's called roughly 'The Man from the Red Door.'"

He looked at the raised hand.

"Is that supposed to be me?"

"Maybe. But the drawing was made much later. I wouldn't identify anyone from it."

Saima removed the image.

"Have you seen it before?"

Asgar shook his head.

"Never."

She made a note. He was still looking at the place where the figure had just been. He wanted to bring it back and enlarge the face even though the artist had barely drawn eyes.

Saima opened a text.

"Now we'll go backward. While the chain can still be traced."

---

The first fragment was short. Saima started the translation, and Asgar heard a story about a man who vanished from a place of gathering, returned several days later, and claimed he had barely been gone.

Neither his name nor the city appeared in the text.

"A stadium," he said.

Saima stopped playback.

"You told Arved that. Good. It was already in your record."

She showed him two sections side by side. One from Asgar's account, the other from the archive. He could not read either, but he saw the matching markers she had highlighted.

"Does it say 'place of gathering' here?" he asked.

"Here—yes. In an earlier copy it was a sports structure. Then the text was translated and shortened for a collection. The city was lost even earlier."

"And the first record?"

"Didn't survive. The chain breaks."

Saima opened several documents. Lines with dates appeared between them. Asgar watched her move from one to another: somewhere a whole paragraph survived, somewhere only a title and a reference to a source that was no longer available.

She did not tell the entire story at once. She went back, checked, sometimes lingered over a word. Once she stopped the translation and angrily deleted an entire sentence.

"What did it say?"

"The translator invented that the man summoned the door himself. The text doesn't say that. It can also simply mean 'the door appeared in his presence.'"

Asgar rubbed his cheek with his palm.

"I didn't summon it."

"I'll note that beside it. Separately from the old text."

The next material mentioned a wife who waited for her missing husband. In a later retelling, she waited by the door for many years.

Asgar's fingers tightened on the edge of the table.

"She didn't stand by the door."

Saima stopped.

"That isn't in the early version either. There are two returns with years between them. Later someone merged them into one."

"Can we go earlier?"

She brought up another text.

The translation was uneven. The man returned after a short disappearance, then vanished again. Years later his identity was verified. His wife was alive.

Asgar listened without interrupting. There was nothing in the text about the screen on which he first saw an older Katya, or the room with the glass partition, or her hands when they embraced. Only a few lines about identity confirmation and a relative who was found.

He held in his memory her hand by her ear. That day her fingers had stopped sooner: her hair was short. Whenever he tried to picture Katya whole, the hair grew long again. Asgar returned to the movement of her hand before he could see her the way she had sat in front of the camera.

"That happened," he said.

Saima waited.

"It was more complicated. But that—yes."

She marked the fragment without asking another question immediately.

Asgar leaned closer.

"Does it say what happened to her afterward?"

Saima looked at the neighboring field, then returned to the text.

"No. Nothing in these materials."

He nodded. In 2191 he had already been shown records. But as soon as he heard the word "wife," he waited for her voice again.

Saima did not close the fragment immediately.

She returned to the source from which it had been assembled and opened its references. Asgar watched her open them one by one and close them without lingering. On the fourth or fifth, she stopped.

"Someone else's words are quoted here," she said. "Not a retelling. The author cites a search report and quotes it."

"Whose?"

"The wife's."

Asgar did not move.

Saima first checked the provenance of the quotation and noted that the report itself had not survived. Then she expanded the translation.

The line was short.

"He said he'd stop for water. He never bought the water."

Asgar could not look away from the plate at first.

He remembered standing in the concrete concourse and promising: a big one, straight home. How she had answered that the water should actually make it home. Then he had turned into the service passage because it was quieter there.

"That's her," he said.

"I can't confirm that," Saima answered. "The report is gone. The quotation may be exact, may have been shortened, may have passed through two translations."

"That's her."

Saima looked at him.

"All right. I'll record it this way: you recognized the wording. Separately from what I can prove."

Asgar asked her to leave the line open. Saima left it and moved to the next document. He sat there until he began making out unfamiliar symbols around the quotation that he did not understand.

---

The later appearance was described in much greater detail. A different society, a different system of dates, researchers trying to understand a man who had barely changed over an enormous interval.

Saima showed him a surviving excerpt from a technical description. Asgar recognized the room with equipment and the nature of the damage near the cabin. He saw a name that had passed through another script and back through translation and asked her to pronounce it again.

"Martin?"

"Most likely. But the name passed through several records; the readings differ."

He told her who Martin was. He stopped short when he reached the bread.

Saima did not interrupt him.

"He helped me," Asgar finished. "We were friends. Not only because of the booth."

She added a note beside the name.

"Here he survived only as a participant in the research."

Asgar leaned back in his chair. His right leg had gone numb from sitting so long; he stretched it under the table and carefully straightened the knee. Saima noticed and moved aside a box that was in his way.

"Break?"

"No. Show me more."

There was a mention of a ruined urban area and a red structure near functioning old machines. There were too few details. Asgar could not tell whether it described the place he had come from.

"Could have been there," he said. "I don't recognize it."

Saima left the fragment without marking it as a match.

Outside the window, someone pushed a cart past. One wheel knocked more often than the others; the sound approached, paused beneath the window, then gradually faded. Asgar looked at the pale strip between the shutters.

"And after that?"

"There are texts. But after the ruins, the chain almost falls apart."

"After the ruins."

Saima returned to the overall sequence.

"Some may be about you. There's no way left to prove it."

"But there are accounts?"

"There are. In some, other red doors. In others, no person at all. And half simply copy this collection."

She opened the familiar drawing.

Asgar looked at it again. The artist had given the man a straight back, long clothes, a confidently raised hand. No dirty trousers, no exhaustion, no borrowed jacket with sleeves that were too short.

"He has it easy," Asgar said.

The translator relayed the sentence. Saima looked at the image.

"What exactly?"

"The man knows where he's going."

She read the caption.

"That's exactly how they made him here."

---

A research note was found in an appendix to a later copy. Its author disputed the interpretation that the man consciously chose the times of his appearances.

Saima read the translation aloud, stopping where she was unsure of the meaning.

The researcher pointed to the man's confusion in early testimonies, his requests for help, the damage near the red structure. In his view, those details did not fit well with a story of controlled travel. He allowed that the man might have become connected to what was happening by accident and did not know how to end the connection.

Asgar listened, leaning over the table.

"Who wrote that?"

Saima showed him the name.

"The name survived. The work itself didn't. We only have this piece because another author argued with it."

"Why did he argue?"

"He thought the man's fear was part of a ritual."

Asgar gave a short laugh.

"A ritual."

He repeated the word more quietly and ran a hand over his face. Saima waited, then restored the research note.

"He was close," Asgar said. "The first one."

"In what way?"

"I went in to talk on the phone. I needed somewhere quiet. There was no ritual."

Saima checked Arved's record.

"Good. You said the phone part before you saw this text."

Asgar asked her to read the final lines again. The researcher acknowledged that there was insufficient data to reconstruct the cause. After that came a reference to a lost section.

"And that's all?"

"That's all. A hole after that."

He looked at the broken text. Some stranger, who had lived who knew when, had nearly managed to see him—frightened, not understanding what to do. And less remained of that person than of the drawing with the confidently raised hand.

Saima closed the appendix.

In its place appeared the final section of the retelling. The translator began to read, and Asgar heard that the wanderer returned to the first door.

"Wait."

Saima stopped the sound.

"Again."

The sentence sounded again. He returned to the door where the journey had begun and left his wandering behind it.

Asgar pulled his chair closer.

"Where did that come from?"

Saima brought up an earlier version. Asgar searched for the familiar sentence in the translation. The account ended earlier.

"Added later," she said and opened the source from which it had been borrowed.

There too was a wanderer and a return home. No cabin, no disappearances. Saima showed the date: that text had existed long before it was attached to the story of the Red Door.

"In one translation it's the gate of his home, in another the threshold. That's where the first door came from."

"Maybe the author still knew?" Asgar asked.

"Maybe. But this exact ending already existed in another text. We can see that."

"That doesn't prove I won't return."

"No."

He looked at the final lines. He wanted to leave them in front of himself, ask for a copy, take them away and reread them in the evening. Everything Mirena could say about returning was uncertain. Here, at least, there was an ending in which the man returned.

"Will that be in your conclusion?"

"I'll write that the ending was borrowed from an older text."

Asgar did not answer for a long time. Then he pushed the chair back, clearing space between himself and the table.

"Yes. Write it that way."

Saima removed the story's translation. Documents remained, references, gaps between copies. Asgar picked up the cup of water she had placed beside him while reading. The water had grown warm.

---

Taira returned near the end of the conversation. Arved came with her.

Saima did not retell the legend to them. She showed the matches with the account recorded before the search, explained which materials depended on one another and where the chain broke.

"Identity established?" Arved asked.

"No. The face isn't confirmed. But he named details in advance that were later found in old materials."

Arved reviewed the highlighted sections.

"And images?"

Saima brought up a small dim frame. Asgar recognized the general view of the stadium, but the face of the person beside the booth broke into blotches. He leaned forward, trying to make out more.

"Do you have anything better?"

"No. There's no face in this copy, just blotches. And forget the drawings entirely for identification."

"Enlarge it."

Saima enlarged the frame. The face became larger but no more distinguishable. Asgar stared at the blotches for several more seconds, then leaned back.

Saima closed the image.

Arved read the conclusion for a while. Then turned toward him.

"Don't go to the common installations yet. You can move around the outer courtyard on your own: the tables, water, this room. I'll tell people."

Asgar nodded.

"Will I get another room?"

"You sleep in this one tonight. Tomorrow we'll find a proper one."

He could now walk to the tables and the workroom without Taira.

Saima asked permission to preserve his explanations with the verification. He agreed. When asked to confirm his name, he stumbled again: over several hours it had sounded in the room so many times it had begun to feel like another label on the surface of the table.

Taira waited for him by the door.

It was hot outside. Asgar unfastened the jacket but did not take it off: his documents were in it. They walked several steps in silence.

"Did you find anything?" Taira asked.

Asgar looked at the translator.

"Some things about myself."

"Good things?"

He remembered the ending of the story. The first door someone else had already led him back to on paper.

"I don't know."

At the workroom, the dark-haired woman was moving boxes into the shade. She saw Asgar, called to Taira, and pointed at the top one.

The translator gave a brief request: the top box had to be set separately.

Asgar looked at Taira.

"Can I help now?"

Taira translated the question. The dark-haired woman looked over Asgar, then his knee, and nudged an empty space by the wall toward him.

"Light things," Taira translated. "If you want."

Asgar picked up the top box and moved it where she indicated. The woman checked that he had not hit the neighboring ones, then held out one frame to him. She ran a finger along the edge, turned it toward the light, and placed it into an empty box.

"Damaged ones separately?"

She nodded.

Taira was already preparing to leave.

"I'll take the translator after lunch."

Asgar sat on an empty stool. The woman placed two more frames in front of him and returned to her table.
