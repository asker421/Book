Chapter 13. Under the Open Sky

The receiver clicked beside his ear.

Asgar flinched and pressed it closer.

“I’m here. Can you hear me?”

The answer came through static.

“I hear you. The connection is fading. Prepare to exit.”

He looked through the glass.

A pale sky showed above a ruined wall, but below, between the debris, lay deep shadow. Wind drove dust along the ground.

No lights in windows.

No people.

“Exit where? Everything’s destroyed.”

“Describe the area in front of the door.”

Asgar bent down.

Beyond the threshold lay a sloping slab, with loose stones below. Farther out, the ground looked flatter.

“Concrete. Slight descent. I don’t see anyone.”

The line crackled again. He caught only the end of the sentence.

“…safe to open. Get out.”

“What if I stay?”

“Don’t. This state is ending. I don’t know what happens inside after that.”

Asgar tightened his grip on the receiver.

He wanted her to say something else: where he was, how much time he had, what to do once he stepped outside.

Her voice was already breaking into short fragments.

He replaced the receiver, took the handle, and pulled.

The door opened with a jerk.

Cold air struck his face.

Asgar hunched his shoulders, looked down, and carefully shifted his weight onto the slab outside.

His sole held.

He stepped all the way out.

The wind pulled at the door. Asgar held it for a moment, then let go and began descending sideways, placing his feet across the slope.

Fine gravel crunched under his right boot.

His foot slid.

He went down on one knee, throwing out a palm in time.

Pain struck sharply.

“Fuck…”

He froze, breathing through his teeth.

Then he lifted his hand from the ground and looked at his trousers.

The fabric over his right knee was torn. Blood showed beneath it.

Asgar stood, using the edge of the slab for support.

The booth stood a little higher up.

He looked up and saw that the red upright beside the door had become translucent: the ruined wall was showing through it.

“Wait!”

He started back up and stopped when pain shot through his knee.

The red structure shivered.

The glass edges faded. For an instant stones and wall showed through them—

then the booth was gone.

Dust and several rocks remained on the slab, along with a rectangular patch the wind had not yet covered with grit.

Asgar stared at it until the cold worked through his T-shirt.

Then he turned away.

---

A passage ran between two rows of ruined buildings.

Part of the nearest façade remained intact. Nothing could be seen through the window openings; the upper floors had collapsed inward. Short metal rods jutted from the wall. A strip of faded material hung from one and moved in the wind.

Asgar took several steps, testing the leg.

His knee hurt when bent but supported his weight. Blood spread slowly beneath the fabric.

He stopped beside a wall and listened.

Something hummed in the distance.

The sound appeared, steadied, then faded again. Asgar could not tell whether it came from above or from beyond the buildings.

He took out his phone.

The black glass reflected the sky.

Asgar pressed the button.

The screen remained dead.

Without the phone, he had no map, no translation, no way to reach Martin.

He put it away.

He walked toward where the passage widened.

A few minutes later, he saw wheel tracks.

Two dark bands crossed the layer of dust and turned behind a low wall.

Between them were footprints.

Asgar crouched, bracing one hand on his good knee.

The wind had not yet softened the edges.

He straightened and was about to call out when a hand appeared beyond the wall.

The palm was raised toward him.

Asgar froze.

Then a person in heavy gray clothing leaned into view. Fabric covered the lower half of the face.

The stranger sharply lowered the raised hand, signaling down, then pointed to the ground beside the wall.

Asgar did not move.

The stranger repeated the gesture and crouched himself.

The humming grew louder.

Asgar lowered himself beside the wall, keeping his weight on his left leg and extending the right. His shoulder pressed against concrete.

He held his breath.

A shadow slid over the passage.

Through the gap between walls, he caught sight of a dark body, broad and flat.

It moved without visible wheels, staying above the rubble.

Something beneath it turned and flashed briefly.

Asgar pulled his head down.

The hum slowly receded.

The stranger remained motionless for a long time.

Then he turned his head, looked along the passage, and beckoned Asgar over.

---

A low cargo cart stood behind the wall.

Two closed containers were strapped to it. A woman with closely cropped hair waited by the handle. She held a small instrument, shifting her gaze between it and Asgar.

The man who had called him over pulled the fabric away from his face.

He was younger than he had looked from a distance. Dust had collected in the crease beside his nose; stubble darkened his chin.

He said several words to the woman and pointed toward where Asgar had come from.

She answered sharply.

Asgar raised his hands to show empty palms.

“I don’t understand.”

The woman looked at him.

He repeated it in Azerbaijani, then in English.

The man beside the cart took a flat box from his pocket. He pressed a side control, spoke a short phrase, and held the device toward Asgar.

A distorted voice came from the speaker.

“Speak… more.”

“My name is Asgar. I don’t know where I am. I need help.”

The device hissed.

Symbols changed on the small screen.

The woman pointed impatiently at the box. The man answered, shook his head, and scrolled through several lines.

Asgar waited, shivering from the cold.

At last the speaker spoke again.

“Language old. Speak short.”

He nodded.

The woman pointed first at his pockets, then at the instrument in her own hand.

“Transmission active?”

“What?”

She repeated it.

The translator gave the same word.

Asgar took out the dead phone and showed the black screen.

“Phone doesn’t work.”

The man reached toward it, but Asgar pulled it back.

He stopped.

Pointed to the button, then the dark glass.

Asgar pressed it himself.

Nothing happened.

The woman looked from the instrument in her hand to the phone and signaled: do not switch it on.

Asgar nodded and put the phone away.

“Follow us. Turn nothing on.”

Asgar pointed upward.

“What was that?”

The man took back the box. The woman was already pulling the cart by its handle.

“Old security,” the device translated. “No transmission—no target. Turn on—it checks. Don’t stay here.”

They moved along the wall.

Asgar followed.

---

The man stayed at the rear.

Whenever Asgar fell behind, he waited at the next turn. Sometimes he pointed out where to place a foot; sometimes he made Asgar stop while he looked around a corner himself.

The woman guided the cart along a narrow strip between debris. Its wheels made almost no sound.

On one descent, Asgar bent his knee badly and hissed.

The man came over and pointed at the blood.

“You can walk?”

“I can.”

He looked into Asgar’s face, then offered his forearm. Asgar leaned on it while they stepped down from a broken threshold.

At the lower wall, the man stopped and touched his chest.

“Damir.”

“Asgar.”

“Asgar,” he repeated.

The woman looked back at them.

Damir gave her name.

“Radmila.”

She did not repeat it.

Only gestured for them to move faster.

The route continued beneath an overhead structure.

The darkness smelled of damp and old metal. Asgar ran one hand along the wall for balance and felt smooth cladding beneath his fingers.

Broken tile lay on the floor.

He tried to work out what this place had once been, but Damir touched his shoulder and pointed ahead.

A weak light was burning there.

---

Another man met them at the entrance.

His jacket was open, revealing a worn shirt. The moment he saw Asgar, he asked something.

Radmila answered without letting go of the cart.

The man’s gaze dropped to the bloody knee, then he stepped aside.

It was warm beyond the door.

Asgar stopped just inside.

Pipes ran along the wall. Lamps hung beneath the ceiling in protective cages. From an adjoining room came the clatter of dishes. Someone was speaking quietly.

Radmila pointed to a bench against the wall.

Asgar sat.

His hands were shaking. He hid them between his knees, but had to move the right leg away immediately.

Damir set the translator beside him.

The man in the open jacket brought water, clean cloth, and a small box.

“Nazar,” he introduced himself, touching his chest.

Then he pointed at Asgar’s knee and raised his eyebrows.

Asgar nodded.

Nazar helped peel the stuck fabric away from the abrasion. The water was cool; Asgar clenched his teeth while the dirt was washed out.

“Stone?” the translator asked.

“Fell.”

Nazar examined the scrape and took a clean strip of material from the box.

“Not deep. Needs cleaning. Step carefully afterward.”

Asgar nodded and looked down at the cup.

Radmila returned, placed another cup in front of him, and sat opposite.

“Now tell us.”

Asgar held the cup in both hands.

“I came out of a red booth. Over there, beyond the ruined wall. Then it disappeared.”

Radmila looked at Damir.

He answered her.

They exchanged several sentences without translation. Asgar was about to interrupt when Damir moved the box closer.

“We saw red,” he said. “From the other side. We were going toward it. Then we heard security. You came into the passage.”

Asgar nodded so quickly he spilled water.

“Yes. I came from there.”

Radmila pointed at the cup: put it down.

He did.

“What’s inside?”

“A telephone. A woman talks through it.”

“She’s inside?”

“No. I’ve never seen her.”

Radmila frowned.

The translator rephrased her question.

“Who else came out?”

“No one. Just me.”

Damir leaned against the wall.

Radmila asked where Asgar had come from before the booth.

He kept the answer short: Baku, 2191, a technical complex.

Martin and Inessa had stayed there.

“And before that?”

Asgar looked at the translator.

“I entered this booth in Baku in 2014. There were other exits after that. The last one was 2191.”

The translator went silent.

Nazar tapped its edge, waited for a response, then showed Asgar a screen with numbers.

“Year? This one?”

“Yes.”

Nazar changed a setting.

A second line appeared beside the local symbols. Asgar could read the month names.

The translator spoke with pauses.

“Old calendar. Baku. Comparison.”

Asgar looked at the line again.

“Yes. That one.”

Nazar displayed the current date in the same format.

November third.

2744.

Asgar stared at the numbers.

In 2191, four recordings remained on the table that he had planned to finish that day.

Here, that day had been five hundred and fifty-three years ago.

Someone set dishes down in the room.

A door slammed in a distant passage, bringing a current of cold air.

“Baku?” he asked.

Radmila nodded.

He ran a hand over his face.

“What happened here?”

She looked at Nazar, then gave a longer answer than before.

The translator produced several recognizable words: outages, sectors, old systems.

Then it stumbled over an unfamiliar term and repeated it.

Asgar raised a hand.

“Wait. I didn’t understand.”

Radmila tried again, using short sentences so the translator would not lose the meaning.

“Old systems lost central coordination. Security holds its sectors. Repair keeps repairing. Transport follows old routes. Each has its own rules.”

“They rebelled?”

The translator stumbled on the word.

Asgar simplified.

“Machines. Against people.”

“No one fought,” Radmila said. “No one decided anything. Each one does what it always did.”

“Then what’s happening above?”

“The same thing. Except up there they do it on top of each other.”

Asgar remembered the dark body over the passage.

“They’re connected to one another?”

“In some places. Badly here. One system can destroy something another comes later to repair.”

“Why do they attack people?”

“Enter a closed sector without authorization, and to the system you are a violation. Our old authorizations died a long time ago. One system warns. Another strikes immediately.”

“Can’t you switch them off?”

“Sometimes. Then a neighboring system comes to repair it and switches it back on.”

Asgar looked at the low ceiling, the lamps, the cup.

“And the whole city is like this?”

“No. Different everywhere. We live between sectors. You’ll see tomorrow.”

Damir said something short without looking at Asgar.

Radmila answered even more briefly.

“What?” Asgar asked.

She pulled the translator toward herself.

“He says the man arrived empty-handed. I say that’s already obvious.”

Asgar set the cup on the table.

“I’ll work for it. Tell me what to do.”

“I will. For now remember the rule: you go nowhere except with one of us, you switch nothing on, you touch nothing without asking. That is not a request.”

“Understood.”

Radmila took her cup and left through the far passage.

Nazar watched her go.

“We changed our route yesterday because of your red thing. With cargo. If security had come lower, we would have abandoned the cart and left empty-handed.”

Asgar looked toward the doorway where Radmila had disappeared.

“I didn’t ask you to come after me.”

“I know. We went anyway.”

Nazar shifted closer to the translator.

He still held the empty bandage box and tapped it against his knee.

“What did you have back there? In your year.”

“What do you mean?”

“Just what you had. Outside.”

Asgar thought.

“That evening? A stadium. Tens of thousands of people sitting in one place watching twenty-two men fail to put a ball where they wanted it.”

Nazar began laughing before the translator finished and laughed again when it did.

“Tens of thousands. In one place.”

“One.”

He shook his head and went to put the box away.

---

They gave him a jacket.

Nazar brought two, held one against Asgar’s shoulders, shook his head, and left the other.

The sleeves were slightly short, but it fastened.

“Ours,” he said, pointing at the jacket. “You can wear it. If you leave, say so.”

Asgar nodded.

The translator remained on the table.

Without it, the speech around him dissolved back into separate sounds. Asgar understood gestures, direction of gaze, sometimes a questioning intonation.

Everything else had to be guessed.

He quickly stopped answering unless he was sure.

After they ate, Damir led him into a small room with two sleeping platforms. Folded blankets occupied one. The man moved them, checked the lamp by the door, and showed Asgar where to lie down.

Then he touched the translator box he had brought.

“We’ll talk in the morning. Rest now.”

“The booth may come back.”

Damir waited for the translation.

“Where?”

“I don’t know. Before, it stayed near me. Last time it was far away.”

“If you hear or see anything, call us. Don’t leave alone.”

Asgar sat on the edge of the bed.

“Will you look for it?”

“We’ll check the cameras. The ones that still work.”

Damir took the translator and left.

Asgar was alone.

He carefully extended his right leg. The scrape burned beneath the bandage, but no more blood showed through.

The phone was in the jacket pocket.

Asgar put it beneath the edge of a folded blanket, then immediately took it back out and left it beside his head.

Sleep would not come.

He remembered the room with moving panels beyond the window.

There, he did not have to listen for what might be flying above the roof.

He could go out for coffee, order the wrong size, go back, and fix the mistake.

Asgar reached toward the phone beside his head.

He wanted to write Martin that he had arrived.

That he was alive.

That there were people here too.

He pressed the button.

The black glass did not change.

Asgar left the phone where it was.

Twice during the night, someone walked past beyond the wall. Once, the footsteps stopped beside the door.

Asgar held his breath.

They soon moved on.

Near morning, he fell asleep.

---

Radmila woke him.

She stood in the doorway holding a lamp. She said something, pointed into the corridor, and waited for him to sit up.

Asgar pulled on the jacket and checked the phone.

He stood carefully. The knee hurt more after being still, but improved after several steps.

Damir and Nazar were already sitting in the common room.

A display lay on the table.

Radmila set the translator beside it.

“We found the red booth.”

Asgar approached so quickly that his thigh struck the edge of the bench.

The image was still.

A concrete canopy, a dark void beyond it, and the booth standing beside the edge of a flight of steps.

The upper part of the structure disappeared partly beneath the canopy.

“When?”

“This morning,” Nazar said. “A camera sent the recording. Then they closed the connection.”

“Why?”

Radmila pointed at the display.

“The camera only watches. It sends no requests there. We took the image through a wire.”

Asgar frowned and looked again.

Damir pulled a second sheet closer—a rough map drawn on dense material.

“We’re here.”

He placed a finger near the lower edge.

“The booth is here. Almost two kilometers.”

Asgar traced the distance with his eyes.

“Can we get there?”

Damir did not answer immediately.

A double line ran between the two points on the map.

He traced it with a fingernail, then pointed at the image of the canopy.

“It’s behind the old perimeter.”

Asgar remembered the shadow above the passage.

The hum that had made Damir force him to the ground.

“Same machines?”

“Yes.”

He looked at the red door.

Yesterday he had entered it beside Martin.

Now it stood less than two kilometers away—

and those two kilometers could not simply be walked.

Asgar pulled the bench closer and sat at the table.

“Show me where we can get through.”
