Chapter 15. The Memory of Iron

The blue band swept across the red frame again.

Asgar stood outside, holding the open door with his left hand and pressing the receiver to his ear with his right. Behind him, beside the ruined façade, Damir did not move.

The three dark machines remained where they were: one blocked the street, two stood farther away at diverging access roads. They did not approach the booth, but at regular intervals one of them initiated another scan.

The woman on the line was speaking to someone else. Unfamiliar voices overlapped, then she came back to Asgar.

“The scanning continues. Don’t close the door.”

“Because of them?”

“It looks that way. Every beam coincides with a jump on our side. That’s all I can see for now.”

The nearest machine repeated in English:

“Temporal anomaly. Civilian exclusion protocol active.”

Asgar had already heard the phrase at the door. Then, the important thing had been working out whether the machine would shoot.

Now different words caught his attention.

“Wait,” he said into the receiver. “It said ‘temporal anomaly.’ Why does it even have a category like that?”

No answer came immediately.

“I don’t know.”

“What about the machine?”

The woman paused.

“Ask it.”

Asgar turned toward the machine.

Its voice was old, roughened by metallic distortion, but he could understand the English.

“Protocol origin?”

The answer came after a short delay.

“Historical civil safety standard. Earliest surviving reference: 16 August 2014.”

Asgar’s fingers tightened around the edge of the door.

August sixteenth.

He looked through the open doorway at the black telephone.

“Source?”

“Primary record lost. Derived civilian reports. Archive migrations. Later incident classifications.”

“Cause of event?”

“Unknown.”

Asgar looked at the machine.

“Purpose of red object?”

“Unknown.”

Someone on the line said several quiet words.

The woman asked Asgar to repeat the machine’s answers.

He relayed them in order.

“So they don’t know the booth,” he said. “They know how to behave around something that was once classified as a temporal anomaly.”

“Yes. That’s what it sounds like.”

Asgar ran his thumb along the coiled telephone cord.

Seven hundred and thirty years earlier he had vanished from a stadium and returned four days later.

Cameras had recorded the corridor.

Rauf had measured distances, written down answers, replayed the footage again and again.

The primary record had not survived to reach this system.

The rule had.

He addressed the machine again.

“Protocol changes. Summary.”

The voice began listing short lines.

Asgar did not understand all of them.

At first: clear a civilian perimeter and do not approach unnecessarily.

Later: do not mechanically restrain an unknown boundary.

Later still came a rule against merging contradictory measurements into one averaged model.

Asgar raised his head.

“Repeat the last one.”

The machine repeated it.

At the end came a date:

2191.

Immediately he saw the two images of the same passage and the gray shutter one system had already placed somewhere else.

No one there had been talking about a seven-hundred-year-old protocol.

They had simply stopped the automation and moved the people out.

The next line concerned active measurement: if observation itself alters the state of an unknown boundary, switch to passive containment.

Asgar looked at the blue beam as it crossed the red upright once again.

“Then why are you still scanning?”

“Passive containment available. Civilian access review incomplete.”

“What else needs checking?”

The woman on the phone answered before the machine.

“It hasn’t finished checking your access.”

“It can see me.”

“It can see that you’re human. It doesn’t know who you are.”

Asgar turned toward the machine.

“Identify me.”

“Historical identity unavailable.”

“So you keep an instruction dated to my day for seven hundred years, but you don’t know who I am?”

“Civilian identity not required for exclusion protocol.”

He gave a short laugh, then winced.

His right knee had begun aching again from standing too long. Asgar shifted his leg, careful not to bend it sharply.

“What does it need?” he asked the woman.

“Ask it. Right now it’s answering better than I am.”

The machine listed three conditions.

The person at the boundary had to understand the instruction, be physically capable of moving away, and be present without external coercion.

Asgar looked at Damir.

He stood far enough away beside the façade that they would have had to shout to speak.

His hands were empty.

“And after that the scanning stops?”

“Active intervention may cease. Safe access not established.”

Asgar pressed the receiver closer.

“Now that is a sensible formulation.”

“Even if it stops scanning, don’t enter immediately. I’ll check the boundary first.”

“I heard you.”

The machine ordered him to step away from the door.

Asgar looked at the cord.

He could not go far while holding the receiver.

“I’m going to hang up. Keep the channel open if you can.”

“We’ll try to hold it until the state changes.”

He replaced the receiver.

Before letting go of the door, he pushed it all the way open. The panel rocked on its hinges and stayed at the limit.

“Step away from boundary.”

Asgar took two steps back.

Because of the injured right leg, the second step was shorter.

The machine repeated the command.

He moved farther away until several meters separated him from the threshold.

“Stop.”

Asgar stopped.

“Approach boundary.”

He walked back.

“Stop.”

This time the command came before he reached the door.

Asgar stopped on his own without committing his weight to the next step.

“Step back.”

He moved away.

Damir raised a questioning palm from the façade.

Asgar pointed down: wait.

The machine asked him to repeat the movement.

Asgar walked away from the red door, returned, and stopped again when told.

After the second pass, the voice said:

“Mobility confirmed. Instruction response confirmed.”

“Non-coercive access review.”

Asgar raised both hands, palms outward, then pointed to himself and the door.

“I came here voluntarily.”

“Verbal declaration insufficient.”

“Of course.”

He looked back at Damir.

“What do you need?”

The system requested observation of external conditions: whether any person or machine was physically holding the civilian at the boundary; whether the route of withdrawal was clear; whether emergency coercion was operating inside the perimeter.

Asgar slowly turned around.

Open ground lay between him and the façade.

Damir remained well back.

The dark machines themselves kept their distance.

Nothing was moving through the air now.

“Path clear,” Asgar said. “No one is holding me here.”

The machine was silent longer than before.

The blue beam crossed the door frame again.

Inside the booth, the receiver rested on its cradle.

Then the machine said:

“Civilian access review complete. Non-coercive interaction confirmed. Passive containment authorized.”

The scanning stopped.

The change was small, but visible.

The red edge that had been faintly trembling against the concrete stopped doubling.

The canopy above the booth remained crooked and broken, but the line where the two met no longer drifted.

Asgar waited several more seconds.

Then he approached the door and lifted the receiver.

“Do you see a change?”

“Yes. Scanning stopped and the boundary settled. The edge is almost back where it was before the first beam. Wait a little.”

He stayed at the threshold.

Damir did not move.

One of the dark machines repeated the civilian warning, but without scanning now.

It only maintained the empty zone.

“The primary 2014 record really is gone?” Asgar asked.

“I don’t see that record. The machine says the primary source was lost.”

“But the rule came from it.”

“The earliest surviving reference is 2014. I can’t tell you more right now.”

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the receiver.

“Understood.”

The line went quiet.

Then the woman asked him to describe the door and threshold.

Asgar answered: the slab beneath his feet was intact, the opening clear, nothing intersected the moving panel, the structure outside was not touching any moving equipment.

She repeated some questions in different words.

After a brief pause she said:

“It’s more stable now. You can enter.”

Asgar looked inside.

“Can, or safe?”

A short pause.

“You can enter. I would not call it safe.”

“Better.”

He did not step in.

“Where will I end up?”

“I don’t know.”

In the last two days, people had given him water, bandaged his knee, hidden him from machines, and led him along a route he could not have survived alone.

Damir still stood by the wall.

Radmila and Nazar remained behind, where life continued without the red door.

Asgar lowered the receiver slightly.

“I need to say goodbye.”

“Not for long. If it starts drifting again, we’ll have to recheck everything.”

He returned the receiver to the cradle and walked away from the booth.

Damir was not far, but Asgar did not go all the way to the façade.

He stopped where the man could see his hands and raised one palm.

Damir stepped once out of cover.

Asgar pointed to the booth, then to himself.

Damir gave him a thumbs-up, then pointed at Asgar’s right knee and tapped his own leg twice.

Asgar laughed.

“Yes, I got it.”

Damir waved toward the booth.

Asgar returned the gesture.

The telephone began ringing again.

Asgar went back to the open door and lifted the receiver.

“I’m here.”

“Everything’s holding for now. You can enter.”

“Then I’m going.”

Asgar crossed the threshold.

His right knee protested as he turned toward the door.

He left it open for one more second and looked outside.

Damir stood by the ruined façade.

Three dark machines held the empty perimeter.

No blue beam touched the red structure.

Asgar pulled the door toward himself.

The last few centimeters moved stiffly.

The lock clicked.

The street noise softened.

“Closed.”

“I see.”

The floor dropped briefly.

Asgar bent his knees and braced one hand against the frame.

Beyond the glass, the concrete stretched into a pale band and vanished.

The woman’s voice began breaking apart.

“Transition…”

Then there was only static.

Asgar remained standing until the floor settled beneath him.

Darkness lay outside the windows.

He raised the receiver to his ear, but the old channel was gone.

Asgar replaced it.

Several seconds later, the telephone rang.

He picked it up.

“Asgar?”

It was a different woman’s voice.
