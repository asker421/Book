Chapter 15. Memory of Iron

The blue band passed over the red frame again.

Asgar stood outside, holding the open door with his left hand and pressing the receiver to his ear with his right. Behind him, by the ruined facade, Damir did not move. The three dark machines remained where they were: one blocked the street, two stood farther away on diverging roads. They did not approach the booth, but at regular intervals one of them launched another scanning pass.

In the receiver, the woman was talking to someone else. Other voices overlapped, then she spoke to Asgar again.

"The scanning is continuing. Don't close the door."

"Is it because of them?"

"Looks like it. Every beam coincides with a jump on our side. That's all I can see for now."

The nearest machine repeated in English:

"Temporal anomaly. Civilian exclusion protocol active."

Asgar had already heard that phrase at the door. Then it had mattered more to understand whether the machine was going to fire. Now the words caught on something else.

"Wait," he said into the receiver. "It said 'temporal anomaly.' Where did it even get a category like that?"

The line did not answer immediately.

"I don't know."

"What about it?"

The woman was silent for a second.

"Ask it."

Asgar turned toward the machine. Its voice was old, with a metallic rasp, but he could understand the English.

"Protocol origin?"

The answer came after a brief delay:

"Historical civil safety standard. Earliest surviving reference: 16 August 2014."

Asgar's fingers tightened on the edge of the door.

August sixteenth.

He looked through the open doorway at the black telephone.

"Source?"

"Primary record lost. Derived civilian reports. Archive migrations. Later incident classifications."

"Cause of event?"

"Unknown."

Asgar shifted his gaze to the machine.

"Purpose of red object?"

"Unknown."

Someone quietly said a few words in the receiver. The woman asked Asgar to repeat the machine's answers. He relayed them in order.

"So they don't know the booth," he said. "They know how to behave near something that was once classified as a temporal anomaly."

"Yes. That's exactly what it sounds like."

Asgar ran his thumb along the coiled cord. Seven hundred thirty years ago he had disappeared from the stadium, then returned four days later. Cameras had recorded the corridor; Rauf had measured the distance, written down answers, replayed the recording. The primary record had not reached this system. The rule had.

He addressed the machine again:

"Protocol changes. Summary."

The voice listed short lines. Asgar did not understand all of them. First—clear the civilian perimeter and do not approach unnecessarily. Then—do not mechanically constrain an unknown boundary. Later still came a rule not to merge conflicting measurements into a single averaged picture.

Asgar raised his head.

"Repeat the last one."

The machine repeated it. At the end came a date: 2191.

Two images of the same passage and the gray shutter that one system had already believed was in a different position immediately appeared in his mind. No one there had talked about a seven-hundred-year protocol. They had simply stopped the automation and moved people out.

The next line concerned active measurement: if observation itself changes the state of an unknown boundary, switch to passive containment of the area.

Asgar looked at the blue beam passing over the red post again.

"Then why are you still scanning?"

"Passive containment available. Civilian access review incomplete."

"What else do you need to check?"

The woman in the receiver answered before the machine:

"It hasn't finished checking your access yet."

"It can see me."

"It can see you're human. It doesn't know who you are."

Asgar turned toward the machine.

"Identify me."

"Historical identity unavailable."

"So you've kept instructions with my date for seven hundred years and don't know who I am?"

"Civilian identity not required for exclusion protocol."

He gave a short laugh and immediately grimaced: after standing so long, his right knee had begun aching again. Asgar shifted his foot, trying not to bend it sharply.

"What does it need?" he asked the woman.

"Ask it. Right now it's answering better than I am."

The machine listed three conditions. The person at the boundary had to understand the instructions, be physically able to move away, and be there without external coercion.

Asgar looked at Damir. He stood by the facade far enough away that they would have had to shout to speak. His hands were empty.

"And after that the scanning stops?"

"Active intervention may cease. Safe access not established."

Asgar pressed the receiver closer.

"That's a proper formulation."

"If it stops scanning, don't go in immediately anyway. I'll check the boundary first."

"I heard you."

The machine ordered him to move away from the door.

Asgar looked at the cord. He could not go far with the receiver in hand.

"I'm going to put the receiver down now. Don't close the channel if you can."

"We'll try to hold it until the state changes."

He returned the receiver to the cradle. Before letting go of the door, he opened it all the way. The door swung on its hinges and stayed at the limit.

"Step away from boundary."

Asgar took two steps back. His right leg pulled beneath the bandage, so the step was shorter. The machine repeated the command. He moved farther away until several meters separated him from the threshold.

"Stop."

Asgar stopped.

"Approach boundary."

He walked back.

"Stop."

This time the command came before he reached the door. Asgar stopped on his own without shifting his weight into the next step.

"Step back."

He stepped away.

Damir raised a questioning hand by the facade. Asgar pointed down: wait.

The machine asked him to repeat the movement once more. Asgar moved away from the red door, returned, and again stopped on command.

After the second pass, the voice said:

"Mobility confirmed. Instruction response confirmed."

"Non-coercive access review."

Asgar raised his hands, palms out, then pointed at himself and at the door.

"I came here voluntarily."

"Verbal declaration insufficient."

"Of course."

He looked back at Damir.

"What do you need?"

The system requested observation of the external arrangement: whether a person or machine was physically holding the civilian at the boundary; whether the retreat path was clear; whether emergency coercion was active within the perimeter.

Asgar slowly turned in place. The area between him and the facade was clear. Damir remained far away. The dark machines themselves kept their distance. Nothing was moving through the air now.

"Path clear," Asgar said. "No one is holding me here."

The machine was silent longer than before.

The blue beam crossed the door frame once again. Inside the booth, the receiver lay on the cradle.

Then the machine said:

"Civilian access review complete. Non-coercive interaction confirmed. Passive containment authorized."

The scanning stopped.

The change was small but visible. The red edge, which until then had been trembling slightly against the concrete, stopped doubling. The canopy above the booth remained skewed and ruined, but the line of intersection no longer drifted.

Asgar waited several more seconds, then went to the door and lifted the receiver.

"Do you see a change?"

"Yes. The scanning stopped, and the boundary has settled. The edge has returned almost to where it was before the first beam. Wait a little."

He stayed at the threshold. Damir did not move. One of the dark machines repeated the civilian warning, but without scanning now. It was only maintaining the empty zone.

"Is the primary record from two thousand fourteen really lost?" Asgar asked.

"I can't see that record. The machine says the primary one is lost."

"But the rule comes from there."

"The earliest surviving reference is two thousand fourteen. I can't say more right now."

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the receiver.

"Understood."

The receiver went quiet. Then the woman asked him to describe the door and threshold. Asgar answered: the slab beneath his feet was intact, the opening clear, nothing crossed the door, the body outside was not touching any moving parts. She repeated some of the questions in different words. After a short pause, she said:

"It's more stable now. You can enter."

Asgar looked inside.

"Can I, or is it safe?"

A short pause.

"You can enter. I wouldn't call it safe."

"That's better."

He did not go in.

"Where will I end up?"

"I don't know."

Over the last two days, people had given him water, bandaged his knee, hidden him from the machines, and guided him along a route he could not have crossed alone. Damir was still standing by the wall. Radmila and Nazar remained where life continued without the red door.

Asgar lowered the receiver slightly.

"I need to say goodbye."

"Don't take long. If everything starts drifting again, we'll have to check it all over."

He returned the receiver to the cradle and stepped away from the cabin.

Damir was not far away, but Asgar did not walk all the way to the facade. He stopped where Damir could see his hands and raised a palm.

Damir stepped one pace out of cover.

Asgar pointed at the booth, then at himself. Damir gave a thumbs-up, then pointed at Asgar's right knee and tapped his own leg twice.

Asgar laughed.

"Yes, I got it."

Damir waved toward the cabin.

Asgar returned the gesture.

The telephone began ringing again at the door.

Asgar walked to the open door and lifted the receiver.

"I'm here."

"Everything is holding for now. You can enter."

"Then I'm going."

Asgar stepped over the threshold. His right knee answered when he turned toward the door. He left it open for another second and looked outside.

Damir stood by the ruined facade. The three dark machines held the empty perimeter. Not a single blue beam touched the red body.

Asgar pulled the door toward himself.

The last few centimeters went stiffly. The lock clicked. The noise of the street became duller.

"Closed."

"I see it."

The floor dropped briefly. Asgar bent his knees and braced a palm against the frame. Through the glass, the concrete stretched into a pale band and disappeared.

The woman's voice began to break apart.

"Transition..."

After that there was only crackling.

Asgar stood until the floor returned beneath his feet. Beyond the windows was darkness. He raised the receiver to his ear, but the old channel was already gone. He returned the receiver to the cradle.

Several seconds later, the telephone rang.

Asgar picked it up.

"Asgar?"

The woman's voice was different.
