Chapter 10. At the Edge of Error

In the morning, Asgar could not make himself get up for a long time.

The room was bright. Reflected light from the external panels moved slowly across the ceiling. He lay on his side looking at the phone beside the bed, listening to sounds beyond the door.

People passed several times.

Once, something rolled by on wheels, tapping softly over the joints in the floor.

Then the panel announced that Martin had arrived.

Asgar sat up. His feet found his shoes immediately, but he left them on the floor and went barefoot to wash.

“Five minutes,” he said.

The system asked whether it should relay that answer to the visitor.

“Yes. To him.”

His face in the mirror was still asleep. Puffy eyelids. A crease from the pillow across one cheek.

Asgar turned on the water and held his hands beneath it.

He wanted to leave them there for a long time, go nowhere, and not have to explain why.

When he opened the door, the man in the corridor tucked a small display under one arm.

“Martin.”

The Russian voice from the device on his chest came a little after he finished speaking. Asgar had already nodded, and now nodded again.

Martin was lean, with a short beard and a deep line between his eyebrows. In his free hand he held two closed cups pressed together.

Asgar took the top one before it fell.

“I hope you drink coffee. I brought two and only realized on the way here that I never asked.”

Asgar smelled it through the opening in the lid.

“I do.”

“Lucky me.”

Asgar stepped away from the door.

Martin entered, looked around, and set his display on the table. The room panel still showed the list of yesterday’s searches.

He did not linger on it.

He opened an old photograph on his own screen, looked at Asgar, then closed it without zooming.

“I spent half the night checking where the comparison samples came from. I thought today’s data might somehow have been written into the old record.

“It wasn’t.”

Asgar held the cup in both hands.

“Are they going to do anything else to me?”

“Today I’m going to ask questions. A lot of them. But yours first. Do we talk here, or come to my office?”

“Far?”

“Next building.”

Asgar looked at the bed, at the discarded blanket.

“Let’s go.”

He put on his shoes and checked his pockets.

The phone was the last thing he picked up.

---

A covered passage connected the buildings. Through broad openings in one wall, plantings and the lower floors of the neighboring structure were visible. Beside one of the openings, a worker knelt dismantling a grate in the floor. The removed plates lay nearby, each on a separate pad.

Asgar slowed.

Water gleamed beneath the grate.

The worker said something to Martin and pointed with a tool toward the other side.

They went around.

“What did he say?”

“That we can pass. Don’t step on the removed panels. The barrier hasn’t arrived yet.”

Martin held the next door open.

“Inessa cleared this wing for us. If you see anything like yesterday, tell me immediately.”

Asgar stopped on the threshold.

“You know it appears near me?”

“Yes. That’s why the rig is powered down, no colleagues are allowed in, and we’re sitting separately.”

“And that doesn’t…”

He left the question unfinished.

Martin waited for the translation.

“It worries me. A lot. Curiosity hasn’t gone away either.”

Beyond the door was a small workroom.

Two desks, chairs, cabinets with transparent fronts. On the windowsill stood a plant with yellowing lower leaves. One leaf lay on the desk beside a keyboard.

Martin noticed it, picked it up, and held it for a while between his fingers as he cleared space for his cup.

“Sit here. Easier to look at the same screen.”

Asgar sat sideways to the desk.

Martin unclipped the translator from his chest, set it between them, and touched its edge.

“It’ll use your language profile. If I start sounding incomprehensible, stop me. Some of our terms come through as though scientists never disagreed about anything.”

“You understand Russian yourself?”

“Old texts better than speech. Especially when someone’s angry.”

Asgar took the lid off his cup.

“Then we’ll try to stay calm.”

---

The first thing Martin showed him was a photograph of the red booth.

Asgar put down the coffee.

The structure stood in a room with white walls. The door was open. A black telephone hung inside on the right.

“Where is this?”

“One of the early reconstructions.”

“A real one?”

“The shell was real. They built it, measured it, dismantled it. It never transported anything.”

Asgar pulled the image closer.

The frame was too perfect. The paint shone evenly everywhere, even around the handle.

For the first few seconds, that was enough.

“Why are you showing me this?”

Martin looked at him and removed the photograph.

“You asked yesterday whether anyone had built one like it. I thought I’d start with a dramatic answer. Bad idea.”

Asgar picked up the cup but did not drink.

“Show me. Just explain first.”

Martin nodded.

Another image appeared. This time there was no red shell—only a frame marked with dimensions and several people beside it. Then a diagram, a date, a test description.

“At first they copied everything visible in the old recordings. Material, dimensions, telephone, even the internal panels. Eventually it became obvious that painting a box more and more accurately was pointless, so they stopped reproducing the shape.”

“Because it didn’t work?”

“Because absolutely nothing interesting happened. And the strangest part of your recordings is the space inside. Two walls and a gap cannot produce that.”

Asgar remembered the concrete through the side glass.

The strip of space that did not exist outside.

“They knew that?”

“They knew the geometry didn’t add up. Describing somebody else’s observation doesn’t tell you how to build it.”

Martin opened a list of papers.

It was long.

Asgar scrolled through the dates. The first years after the stadium. Decades. Documents written after he had stood with Katya at the partition.

“Was everyone working on the same thing?”

“No. This is where it gets interesting.”

Martin placed several publications side by side. In the first, Asgar could make out familiar surnames and formulas; in the second, almost nothing.

“These fields did not emerge from the booth from nothing. Questions about time, measurement, and coupled systems existed before your case. What happened to you simply made a lot of people attack the same questions at once. Sometimes that helped. Sometimes it produced a mountain of garbage.”

“And the name?”

Asgar pointed at words repeated across the titles.

“‘Red Corridor,’” the translator read.

Martin rubbed his forehead.

“A later name for several branches of research. It caught on far too well. Then people would read the title and ask where the entrance was.”

“You have to explain that often?”

“More often than I’d like.”

Asgar looked back at the list.

He had wanted to ask exactly that.

---

Martin found a recording of an experiment.

A low sealed apparatus stood in the middle of the image. Around it was far more equipment: thick connections, racks, a separate room visible through an open door.

Asgar tried to judge the dimensions from a person walking past and realized the installation occupied almost the entire hall.

“What does it do?”

“Here they compare the progression of two measurement systems. One is subjected to an interaction; the other is kept isolated. Then they check whether the discrepancy persists and whether ordinary causes can be ruled out.”

“So you aren’t sending anything anywhere?”

“No. At this stage we’re only trying to make two identical systems pass through the same interval slightly differently. Then we have to prove the difference didn’t come from the instruments, temperature, or interference.”

On the recording, a person moved away.

Nothing visible happened.

Martin paused the video and opened the results.

“This section.”

Asgar leaned closer. On the graph, the lines diverged almost imperceptibly.

“How much?”

Martin changed the scale and units.

The translator spoke a fraction of a second that Asgar had to read again.

He looked at the image of the apparatus.

“All of that for this much shift?”

“Yes. And this is one of the successful series.”

“No person inside?”

“No.”

“Could you move an object?”

“To another date? No. These results do not give us a way to send even this cup there.”

Martin picked up his own coffee, realized he had forgotten to open the lid, and removed it.

“This series took months to prepare. Then several short runs, then dismantle equipment again, inspect it, rebuild. So when someone asks me ‘did it work?’ I usually answer with another question: what exactly counts as success?”

Asgar traced the tiny bend in the line again.

Inside the booth, everything took seconds.

A heavy door. A brief drop. Different light.

He had never seen enormous halls of equipment around it.

“Then what does this have to do with the booth?”

Martin set his cup down.

“One measured signature resembles it.

“One.

“That’s why we compare the results. We do not know whether the same physics lies behind them.”

He opened three papers side by side.

“At one point, it looked as though three independent groups had reproduced the result.”

“And then?”

Martin began opening references.

Each paper led through different chains of documents until all three paths converged on the same original set of measurements.

Asgar straightened.

“They all used the same recording?”

“Yes. Processed it differently. Later reviews started citing them as three confirmations.

“I cited them that way too.”

He said it plainly, without smiling.

“When did you figure it out?”

“When I wanted to compare the instruments. I went looking for the specifications of three installations and gradually realized I was looking for one.

“Extremely unpleasant evening.”

Asgar looked at the long chains of references. A moment ago they had seemed like traces of a vast collective effort.

Now he could see how easily the beginning could disappear inside them.

“Is there a lot of that in my case too?”

“Plenty. That’s why I want to show you the provenance of every important item, not just the cleanest-looking conclusion.”

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the table.

“And whatever I tell you now—where does that go?”

---

Martin cleared the papers from the screen.

“For now, only into the record of this conversation. I’ll add anything to the research material only if you agree.”

“You’re already recording?”

“The translation is saved automatically. If you want, we can delete it afterward.”

Asgar looked at the small device between them.

“Don’t put it anywhere yet.”

“All right.”

Martin changed a setting and showed him the confirmation in language he could understand.

Asgar pushed his chair back. His shoulder ached from sitting too long; he moved the arm carefully and stood.

A small green shoot was visible among the dry leaves on the windowsill.

“You want to know what the woman on the phone says.”

“Yes. Especially when we can compare her words with what happens outside.”

“And anything she tells me later.”

Martin was quiet and waited for the translation to finish.

“If you come back and want to tell us.”

Asgar turned toward him.

“That’s the problem. I don’t know where I’ll come out. Suppose I learn something here, then end up earlier. Or they tell me how the thing works and I bring that information to you.

“What happens then?”

Martin folded his hands on the desk.

“No idea.”

“You don’t have a rule for that?”

“We do. For ordinary bad data, rumors, leaks, disputed measurements. We don’t have a separate procedure sitting around for a man who might bring information from the future.”

Asgar looked down at the pocket weighted by the dead phone.

He wanted to ask Martin to solve everything at once.

The booth. The dates. Whether one room could be brought back.

To do that, he would have to tell him everything he had heard and allow any question.

He sat again.

“I can describe what I saw. Where I was standing, how I opened the door, what changed. I’ll tell you immediately about anything dangerous. But I’m not promising to hand over whatever information I may get from other times.”

“Fine. Then that’s how we work.”

“That doesn’t mean I understand something and I’m hiding the solution. I barely know anything myself.”

“I noticed.”

Martin said it without mockery and nodded toward his pocket.

“Even if some device from there turns out to be unusual, first we’d have to understand what it does.”

Asgar took out the phone and set it on the desk.

“This one’s ordinary. Battery’s dead.”

Martin leaned closer but did not reach for it.

“Do you want me to find someone who knows how to work with technology this old?”

Asgar covered the phone with his hand.

“There are family photographs on it. If it can be powered on without deleting anything.”

“I’ll ask. And I won’t let anyone take it apart until they explain to you what they intend to do.”

Asgar put the phone away.

That made continuing a little easier.

---

They went to the rig after speaking with Inessa.

Martin explained that the adjacent room contained a measurement installation used for their own series of observations. He had no intention of showing Asgar an active experiment. Asgar could look at the apparatus from the observation room and then return to the office.

Inessa was waiting at the entrance to the laboratory wing. A technician stood beside her with an open portable unit.

They exchanged several sentences. Martin’s translator delivered the conversation with a delay, forcing Asgar to keep track of who had spoken last.

“The power section is physically open,” the technician said. “Inspection complete.”

“Physically?” Inessa asked.

He pointed toward an open section beyond a barrier.

A visible gap separated two large connectors.

“Here and at the second feed. Residual charge has been removed. Measurement channels are powered independently.”

Inessa inspected it herself, then allowed them into the observation room.

Through a wide window, the rig was visible: a long base, several closed modules, thin connections running toward equipment racks.

Nothing like the booth.

Asgar stopped at the window with his hands in his pockets.

“Why keep the measurements running?” he asked.

Martin waited for the translation.

“Because I want to know what happens when we do nothing. Otherwise any fluctuation near an active run can later be declared a result of the run.”

He pointed to two separate units.

“They have independent power and recording. The interaction section is unpowered.”

Asgar looked at Inessa.

“You brought me here to see whether it activates around me?”

“No. You asked to see the equipment, and I only agreed to a powered-down rig. If Martin wants an experiment involving you, he asks you first, and then me.”

Martin nodded.

“And probably in the opposite order.”

Inessa looked at him.

It was the first time Asgar saw Martin look away first.

“We can leave now,” she said.

Asgar looked through the glass again.

“No. I want to see.”

The technician turned a screen with the live data toward them. Lines moved slowly across it.

Asgar did not yet know which line belonged to which instrument when one segment twitched.

The technician leaned in.

Martin stopped speaking.

A marker appeared on the neighboring screen.

Asgar took his hands out of his pockets.

“What?”

No one answered immediately.

The technician opened another channel.

Martin stepped toward the desk, but Inessa had already raised one hand.

“Do not touch the controls. Record only.”

She said it sharply.

The translation arrived after both men had frozen.

Asgar backed away from the window.

Nothing in the laboratory changed.

The rig lay on its base.

The connectors remained open.

There was no red anywhere.

“Is that it?” he asked.

Martin enlarged the recorded segment.

“I don’t know.”

“Before it appears, the first thing is also—”

“What exactly do you see right now?”

Asgar looked around the room.

The edges of the desk.

The window frame.

The reflection of a lamp.

Everything stayed where it was.

“Nothing.”

Inessa pointed toward the exit.

“Into the corridor. The instruments can record without us.”

Martin lingered by the display.

“I need to preserve the source interval separately before—”

“The technician will preserve it. Martin, out.”

He looked at her, then stepped away from the desk.

Asgar left first.

---

They waited in an empty room two doors away from the laboratory.

Inessa remained in contact with the technician. Martin stood against a wall holding the portable display in both hands.

Asgar sat, then stood almost immediately.

He had the feeling that if he remained seated, the booth might appear somewhere he could not see it.

“Is there a camera in the corridor?” he asked.

Inessa nodded.

“Yes. They’ll tell us if anything appears.”

“I’ll have to approach it.”

“First we find out what happened.”

He was about to argue when a voice came through the device on her clothing.

Inessa listened and asked a question.

Martin’s translator delivered the answer.

“No repeated deviations. Both independent channels preserved the event. Weaker in the control section.”

Martin set his display on the table.

“May I?”

Inessa came over.

Asgar stayed on the opposite side.

Three recordings filled the screen. Two contained a brief bend at almost the same point. The third changed less.

Martin aligned the times and opened the power data.

“The generators were not active,” he said. “And there was no residual discharge at this moment.”

“So the rig couldn’t have done it itself?”

“We’re still checking. But the impulse appears outside the active section too. If the rig caused it, it’s behaving unlike it should.”

Asgar leaned closer.

Martin enlarged the graph.

A short divergence, then a return.

Asgar recognized the shape.

“It looks like that experiment. Why don’t you want to say they’re connected?”

Martin ran a hand over his beard.

“I do.

“Very much.

“Yesterday a booth appeared. Today you arrived. Now two channels jump at the same moment. If I were sitting alone right now, I’d already have spent ten minutes convincing myself we’d found the connection.

“That’s exactly why we check everything else.”

Asgar straightened.

Inessa looked at him, then at Martin.

“That’s enough for today. Power section stays physically open. Nobody goes back to the rig until you’ve analyzed the recording.”

Martin did not argue.

The instruments continued running in the adjoining room. Asgar could hear only the faint flow of air through the grille above the door.

Several times he turned toward the corridor.

The booth did not appear.

---

They walked back more slowly.

The section with the removed floor grate had been cordoned off. The worker was gone, leaving his tools in a locked box. Asgar recognized the place and took the detour first.

Martin walked beside him with the display under one arm.

“I’ll give you a copy of today’s data,” he said.

“I barely understand any of it.”

“Then we’ll start with what you saw yourself. Where you stood, what was switched on, what you did not notice. I’ll label the rest separately.”

Asgar was quiet for a while.

“Just don’t write that I caused it.”

Martin stopped by the door to Asgar’s building.

“I won’t. I’ll write that you were present. We can let someone find a cause before naming one.”

He offered to show Asgar the record before sending it into the shared archive.

Asgar agreed.

At his room, he took out the phone and looked at the black screen again.

Martin noticed.

“I remember about the specialist.”

“Thanks.”

Asgar opened the door.

A clean glass stood on the table. The bed had been made. A second chair, not there in the morning, now stood against the wall.

He would normally have left his shoes beside the bed in the same position. But they were on his feet, and the familiar glance toward the bed landed on empty floor.

“Are you working tomorrow?” he asked, turning back.

“I am.”

“I’ll come look at the record.”

Martin nodded.

“Agreed.”

Asgar closed the door and put the phone on the table.

Then he pulled the second chair up to it.
