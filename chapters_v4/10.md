Chapter 10. At the Edge of Error

In the morning, Asgar could not make himself get up for a long time.

The room was bright. A reflection from the outer panels moved slowly across the ceiling. He lay on his side, looking at the phone beside the bed, listening to sounds beyond the door. People passed several times. Once, something rolled by on wheels, tapping softly over joints in the flooring.

Then the panel announced that Martin had arrived.

Asgar sat up. His feet immediately found his shoes, but he left them on the floor and went to wash barefoot.

"Five minutes," he said.

The system asked whether it should pass the reply to the visitor.

"Yes. To him."

His face in the mirror looked sleep-heavy. Puffy eyelids, a mark from the pillow on his cheek. Asgar turned on the water and put his hands under it. He wanted to keep them under the stream for a long time, go nowhere, explain to no one why.

When he opened the door, the man in the corridor tucked a small screen under his arm.

"Martin."

The Russian voice from the device on his chest came slightly after he finished speaking. Asgar had already nodded and now nodded again.

Martin was lean, with a short beard and a deep line between his eyebrows. In his free hand he held two lidded cups pressed together. Asgar took the upper one before it could fall.

"I hope you drink coffee. I brought two and only realized on the way that I hadn't asked."

He smelled it through the opening in the lid.

"I do."

"Lucky."

Asgar stepped back from the door.

Martin came in, looked around, and put the screen on the table. The room's open panel still showed the list of yesterday's searches. He did not linger on it.

He opened an old photograph on his own screen, looked at Asgar, and closed it without enlarging.

"I spent until late last night checking where the comparison samples came from. I thought today's data might have been inserted into the old record. It wasn't."

Asgar held the cup in both hands.

"Are they going to do anything else to me?"

"Today I'm going to ask questions. A lot of them. But yours first. Shall we talk here or go to my room?"

"Is it far?"

"Next building."

Asgar looked at the bed, at the thrown-off blanket.

"Let's go."

He put on his shoes and checked his pockets. He picked up the phone last.

---

A covered passage ran between the buildings. Through wide openings in its wall, plantings and the lower floors of the neighboring building were visible. By one opening, a worker knelt, taking apart a grate in the floor. Removed panels lay beside him, each on its own support.

Asgar slowed.

Water gleamed behind the grate. The worker said something to Martin and pointed with a tool toward the detour. They went around on the other side.

"What did he say?"

"We can pass. Don't step on the removed panels. The barrier hasn't arrived yet."

Martin held the next door.

"Inessa cleared this wing for us. If you see anything like yesterday, say so immediately."

Asgar stopped on the threshold.

"You know it appears near me?"

"Yes. That's why the rig is shut down, no colleagues have been allowed in here, and we're sitting separately."

"And that doesn't..."

He left the question unfinished.

Martin waited for the translation.

"It worries me. A lot. Curiosity hasn't gone anywhere either."

Beyond the door was a small workroom. Two desks, chairs, cabinets with transparent fronts. A potted plant stood on the windowsill with yellowing lower leaves. One leaf lay on the desk beside the keyboard.

Martin noticed it, picked it up, and held it between his fingers for a while as he made room for the cup.

"Sit here. Easier for us both to see."

Asgar sat sideways to the desk. Martin unclipped the translator from his chest, placed it between them, and touched its edge.

"It'll use your language setting. If I start sounding incomprehensible, stop me. It translates some of our terms as though scientists never argued."

"Do you understand Russian yourself?"

"Old texts better than speech. Especially when someone is angry."

Asgar removed the lid from his cup.

"Then we'll try to stay calm."

---

The first thing Martin showed him was a photograph of the red booth.

Asgar set down his coffee.

The body stood in a room with white walls. The door was open. A black telephone hung inside on the right.

"Where is this?"

"One of the early reconstructions."

"Real?"

"The body is real. They built it, measured it, took it apart. It never moved anywhere."

Asgar pulled the image closer.

The frame was too straight. The paint shone evenly across the whole surface, even by the handle. But for the first few seconds that was enough.

"Why are you showing me this?"

Martin looked at him and removed the photograph.

"You asked yesterday whether anyone had built one. I decided to begin beautifully with the answer. Bad idea."

Asgar picked up the cup but did not drink.

"Show me. Just tell me first."

Martin nodded.

Another photograph appeared on the screen. This time there was no red body: only a frame with dimensions marked and several people beside it. Then a diagram, a date, a test description.

"At first they copied everything visible in the old recordings. Material, dimensions, telephone, even the inner panels. Then it became clear that painting the box more and more accurately was pointless, and they moved away from the shape."

"Because it didn't work?"

"Because nothing interesting happened at all. And the main strange thing in your recordings is the space inside. Two walls and a gap won't produce that."

Asgar remembered the concrete beyond the side glass. The strip of space that had not existed outside.

"They knew that?"

"They knew the geometry didn't add up. But a description of someone else's observation doesn't tell you how to make it happen."

Martin opened a list of studies. It was long.

Asgar scrolled through it, reading dates. The first years after the stadium. Decades. Documents written after he had stood with Katya by the partition.

"Was everyone working on the same thing?"

"No. This is where it gets interesting."

Martin put several publications side by side. In the first, Asgar could make out familiar surnames and formulas; in the second, almost nothing.

"These studies didn't grow from the cabin out of nothing. Questions about time, measurement, and linked systems existed before. Your case simply made a great many people dive into them at once. Sometimes that helped. Sometimes it created a mountain of garbage."

"And the name?"

Asgar pointed at recurring words in the titles.

"'Red Corridor,'" the translator read.

Martin rubbed his forehead.

"A later name for several branches. It stuck too well. Then people read the title and asked where the entrance was."

"Do you explain that often?"

"More often than I'd like."

Asgar looked at the list again.

He wanted to ask exactly that too.

---

Martin found a recording of an experiment.

A low enclosed apparatus stood at the center of the image. There was much more equipment around it: thick connections, racks, a separate room visible through an open door. Asgar tried to estimate the size from a person walking nearby and realized the installation occupied almost the entire hall.

"What does it do?"

"Here they compare the passage of two measurement systems. One is subjected to an influence, the other kept separate. Then they check whether the discrepancy remains and whether ordinary causes can be ruled out."

"So you're not sending anything anywhere?"

"No. For now we're only trying to make two identical systems go through the same interval slightly differently. Then prove the difference didn't come from the instruments, temperature, or interference."

In the recording, a person moved away. Nothing visible happened.

Martin stopped playback and opened the results.

"This section here."

Asgar leaned closer. On the graph, the lines diverged almost imperceptibly.

"By how much?"

Martin changed the scale and units. The translator spoke a fraction of a second that Asgar had to read again.

He looked at the frame with the apparatus.

"All of that for a shift that small?"

"Yes. And this was one of the successful series."

"No person inside?"

"No."

"Can you move an object?"

"To another date—no. These results don't give us a way to send even this cup there."

Martin picked up his coffee, discovered he had forgotten to open the lid, and removed it.

"They prepared this series for several months. Then a few short runs—and then disassemble the equipment again, check it, rebuild it. So when someone asks 'did it work?' I usually answer with another question: what exactly do you consider success?"

Asgar traced the slight bend in the line once more.

In the booth, everything took a few seconds. Heavy door, short jolt, unfamiliar light. He had seen no huge halls full of equipment beside it.

"Then what does this have to do with the booth?"

Martin set down the cup.

"One measured feature is similar. Only one. So we compare the results, but we don't know whether the same physics is behind them."

He opened three papers and placed them side by side.

"At one point it looked like three groups had obtained the result independently."

"And then?"

Martin began opening references. From each paper they followed different paths through other documents until only the same set of measurements remained on the screen.

Asgar straightened.

"They all used the same record?"

"Yes. Processed it differently. Later reviews started citing them as three confirmations. I cited them too."

He said it simply, without smiling.

"When did you figure it out?"

"When I wanted to compare the instruments. I went looking for information on three installations and gradually realized I was looking for one. Very unpleasant evening."

Asgar looked at the long chains of references. Not long ago, they had seemed like traces of a large collaborative effort. Now he saw how easily the beginning could get lost inside them.

"Does that happen with my story too?"

"Constantly. That's why I want to show you the origin of every important piece of material, not just the prettiest conclusion."

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the desk.

"And what I tell you now—where does that go?"

---

Martin cleared the papers from the screen.

"For now—into the record of our conversation. I'll add only what you agree to into the research materials."

"You're already recording?"

"The translation is saved automatically. If you want, we'll delete it after the conversation."

Asgar looked at the small device between them.

"Don't put it anywhere for now."

"All right."

Martin changed a setting and showed him a confirmation in language he could understand.

Asgar moved the chair back. His shoulder ached after sitting for so long; he moved his arm carefully and stood.

On the windowsill, among dry leaves, a small green shoot was visible.

"You want to know what the woman on the phone says."

"Yes. Especially when her words can be compared with what happens outside."

"And everything she tells me later."

Martin was silent for a moment and waited for the translation to finish.

"If you come back and want to tell me."

Asgar turned toward him.

"That's the problem. I don't know where I'll come out. Suppose I learn something from you, then end up earlier. Or they tell me how this thing works, and I bring it back to you. Then what?"

Martin folded his hands on the desk.

"No idea."

"You don't have a rule?"

"We do. For ordinary bad data, rumors, leaks, disputed measurements. We don't have a separate instruction sitting around for a person who may bring information from the future."

Asgar lowered his eyes to the pocket weighed down by the dead phone.

He wanted to ask Martin to solve everything at once. The booth, the dates, whether one room could be restored. To do that, he would have to tell everything he had heard and allow any questions.

He sat down again.

"I can describe what I saw. Where I stood, how I opened the door, what changed. Anything dangerous, I'll tell you immediately. But I won't promise to pass along any information I get from other times."

"All right. Then that's how we'll work."

"That doesn't mean I understand something and I'm hiding a solution. I barely know anything myself."

"I noticed."

Martin said it without mockery and nodded toward his pocket.

"Even if the device from there turns out to be unusual, we'd first have to figure out what it does at all."

Asgar took out the phone and set it on the desk.

"This one's ordinary. It's just dead."

Martin leaned closer but did not reach for it.

"Would you like me to find someone who knows how to work with technology like this?"

Asgar covered the phone with his palm.

"There are family photos on it. If it can be turned on without erasing anything."

"I'll ask. And I won't let anyone take it apart until they explain to you what they're planning to do."

Asgar put the phone away.

After that sentence, continuing became a little easier.

---

They went to the rig after speaking with Inessa.

Martin explained that a measurement area was located in the neighboring room, where they had been running their own observation series. He had no intention of showing Asgar an active experiment. Asgar could inspect the installation from the passage, then return to the room.

Inessa was waiting at the entrance to the laboratory wing. Beside her stood a technician with an open portable unit.

They exchanged several phrases. Martin's translator relayed the conversation with a delay, and Asgar had to hold in mind who had spoken last.

"The power section is physically open," the technician reported. "The check is complete."

"Physically?" Inessa asked.

He pointed at the open section behind the barrier. A gap was visible between two large connectors.

"Here and at the second input. Residual charge removed. Measurement channels are operating independently."

Inessa looked for herself, then allowed them into the observation room.

Through a broad window, the rig was visible: a long base, several enclosed nodes, thin connections running to racks. Nothing resembling a cabin. Asgar stopped by the window with his hands in his pockets.

"Why leave the measurements running?" he asked.

Martin waited for the translation.

"Because I'm interested in what happens when we do nothing. Otherwise any spike near a run can later be declared the result of the run."

He pointed at two separate units.

"They have their own power and recording. The active section is de-energized."

Asgar looked at Inessa.

"You brought me here to see whether it reacts near me?"

"No. You asked to see the equipment, and I agreed only to a shut-down rig. If Martin ever wants an experiment involving you, he'll ask you first and then me."

Martin nodded.

"And probably in the opposite order."

Inessa looked at him.

For the first time, Asgar saw Martin look away first.

"We can leave now," she said.

Asgar looked through the glass again.

"No. I want to see."

The technician turned a display of current data toward them. Lines moved slowly across it. Asgar did not yet know which belonged to which instrument when one section twitched.

The technician leaned closer.

Martin stopped speaking.

A marker appeared on the neighboring screen.

Asgar took his hands out of his pockets.

"What?"

No one answered immediately. The technician opened another channel. Martin moved toward the desk, but Inessa had already raised a hand.

"Don't touch control. Recording only."

She said it briefly. The translation came after both men had frozen.

Asgar stepped back from the window.

Nothing had changed in the laboratory. The rig lay on its base. The connectors remained open. There was no red anywhere.

"Is that it?" he asked.

Martin enlarged the recording segment.

"I don't know."

"Before the appearance it also started with—"

"What exactly do you see right now?"

Asgar looked around the room. Edges of the desk, window frame, reflection of the light. Everything remained in place.

"Nothing."

Inessa pointed toward the exit.

"Into the corridor. The instruments can record without us."

Martin lingered by the screen.

"I need to save the raw segment separately before—"

"The technician will save it. Martin, we're leaving."

He looked at her, then stepped away from the desk.

Asgar went out first.

---

They waited in an empty room two doors away from the laboratory.

Inessa stayed in contact with the technician. Martin stood by the wall holding the portable screen in both hands. Asgar sat down but almost immediately stood again. It seemed to him that while he was sitting, the booth might appear somewhere he would not see it.

"Is there a camera in the corridor?" he asked.

Inessa nodded.

"Yes. If anything appears, they'll tell us."

"I'll have to go to it."

"First we'll find out what happened."

He wanted to argue, but a technician's voice came from the device on her clothing. Inessa listened and asked a question. Martin's translator relayed the answer:

"No repeated deviations. Both independent channels preserved the event. Weaker in the control section."

Martin set the screen on the table.

"May I?"

Inessa came over. Asgar remained on the other side.

Three recordings were displayed on the screen. In two, there was a short bend at almost the same point. The third changed less. Martin aligned the times and opened the power data.

"The generators weren't on," he said. "And there was no residual discharge at that moment."

"So the rig couldn't have done it?"

"We're still checking. But the impulse exists outside the working section too. If the rig caused it, it's behaving differently from how it should."

Asgar leaned closer.

Martin enlarged the graph. Brief divergence, then return—Asgar recognized the shape.

"It looks like that experiment. Why don't you want to say it's connected?"

Martin ran a hand over his beard.

"I do. Very much. Yesterday the cabin appeared, today you arrived, now two channels twitched at the same time. If I were sitting here alone, I'd already have spent ten minutes convincing myself we'd found a connection. That's exactly why we'll check everything else."

Asgar straightened.

Inessa looked at him, then at Martin.

"That's enough for today. The power section stays open. We don't return to the rig until you've analyzed the recording."

Martin did not argue.

The instruments continued working in the neighboring room. Asgar heard only the faint airflow through the grille above the door. Several times he turned toward the corridor.

The booth did not appear.

---

They walked back more slowly.

The area with the removed grate had already been fenced off. The worker was gone, leaving his tools in a closed box. Asgar recognized the spot and turned toward the detour first.

Martin walked beside him with the screen under his arm.

"I'll give you a copy of today's data," he said.

"I barely understand any of it."

"Then we'll start with what you saw yourself. Where you stood, what was on, what you didn't notice. I'll label everything else separately."

Asgar was silent for a while.

"Just don't write that I caused it."

Martin stopped by the door to his building.

"I won't. I'll write that you were there. Let them find the cause first."

He offered to show Asgar the record before sending it to the shared archive.

Asgar agreed.

At his room, he took out the phone and looked again at the black screen. Martin noticed.

"I remember about the specialist."

"Thank you."

Asgar opened the door. A clean glass stood on the table. The bed had been made; a second chair stood by the wall that had not been there in the morning. He would have left his shoes the same way he always did in the morning, but now they were on his feet, and his habitual glance toward the bed met empty floor.

"Are you working tomorrow?" he asked, turning back.

"I am."

"I'll come see the recording."

Martin nodded.

"Agreed."

Asgar closed the door and put the phone on the table.

Then pulled the second chair up to it.
