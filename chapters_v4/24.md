Chapter 24. Where the Shore Used to Be

A branch moved beyond the glass.

Asgar watched until it returned to its former position. The wind came in gusts: leaves turned their pale sides outward, pressed together, separated again. Through them he could see a slope covered in low trees. Higher up ran a strip of bare rock.

He was still holding the receiver to his ear.

“I can see land,” he said. “Plants. Looks like open air.”

Mirena asked him to describe the area in front of the door.

Asgar leaned down.

A smooth surface ran beneath the booth. Beyond it began a strip of fine gravel. To the right, behind branches, the corner of a low building projected into view.

While the checks ran, he watched the band of stone above the trees.

After the ring, an ordinary slope felt almost like home.

His eyes had no trouble finding up and down.

Heavy things lay where they were supposed to.

“The air is compatible. Pressure is normal. You can open the door,” Mirena said.

“Do not drink local water, eat anything local, or touch living organisms until they’re checked on site.”

“Understood. Do we have a date?”

She gave him the year.

Asgar thought he had missed the break between words.

“Repeat that.”

Mirena did.

“Eighteen million four hundred three thousand two hundred twenty-one.”

His hand left the handle.

The branch beyond the glass kept moving.

One leaf caught its edge on another, slipped free, turned over.

Asgar watched that tiny motion and tried to place the number in front of it.

“Millions?”

“Yes.”

He said nothing.

The years of waiting they had discussed before the transition had still belonged to him.

This number belonged to nothing familiar.

He remembered drawings of ancient animals.

Bands marking geological eras.

Small printed captions beneath museum bones.

In his memory, all of that belonged to the past.

When he imagined the future, he imagined cities.

“Asgar?”

“I hear you.

“I’m opening now.”

The door moved heavily.

He held it and breathed in warm air.

It smelled of heated leaves and something sharp, resinous.

Behind the trees came a faint crackling sound.

It shifted, faded, reappeared somewhere else.

A voice came from the building.

Asgar froze at the threshold.

The voice repeated a short sequence of unfamiliar sounds.

“There’s either a system here or a person. Something is speaking. I don’t understand it.”

“Try to configure local translation.

“I’ll remain available through the telephone as long as the channel holds.”

He told her he was putting the receiver down, returned it to the cradle, and lifted the bag.

A dark post stood beside the path outside.

When he approached, a pale symbol appeared near the top.

The voice spoke again.

“I don’t understand.

“Russian.

“Can you speak Russian?”

The symbol changed.

For several seconds nothing happened.

Then the system produced two familiar words with the syllables spaced incorrectly.

Asgar repeated his request more slowly.

The reply became longer.

He caught “speech” and “wait,” then understood the sentence in full.

“Matching historical language record. Continue speaking.”

He gave his name.

Said he was beside a red booth and needed help.

The system asked him to repeat the final word.

Then it announced that translation was available, though some meanings might require clarification.

“Where am I?”

“Ground biosphere node.”

A local name followed.

Asgar could not repeat it.

“Is this Earth?”

“Yes.”

The post asked him not to move away.

Symbols changed several times across its top.

Then the system said immediate presence on the platform was permissible.

Water, food, and contact with local organisms remained restricted pending a separate check.

Another line appeared:

movement through the platform was temporarily closed.

Information about an unknown person and object had been forwarded for review.

Until an answer arrived, Asgar was instructed to remain near the node.

He looked at the branches over the path and took half a step away from them.

---

Olmer came out of the building carrying a long case.

He took two steps and stopped.

His gaze moved over the red structure, dropped to the surface beneath it, returned upward.

He did not put down the case.

Instead he backed toward the wall without turning his back on the booth and said something short toward the post.

The post answered in its own language.

He asked again.

Listened.

Asked one more time, more briefly.

Only then did a comprehensible voice reach Asgar, with a slight delay.

“Move away from the object. Over here, by the wall. Don’t rush.”

Asgar picked up the bag and crossed over.

“Did you open anything there?

“Touch anything outside?”

“The door.

“From inside.”

“Nothing else?”

“Nothing else.”

Olmer looked at his hands, then his face.

“Headache?

“Metallic taste?

“Skin burning?”

“No.”

He addressed the post again.

In translation Asgar caught “radiation,” “field,” and a term rendered as “biological risk.”

The answer took a long time.

Olmer listened without taking his eyes off the booth and once raised a hand when Asgar began speaking.

“The node sees no immediate threat,” he said at last.

“It isn’t supposed to see everything.

“But there’s nothing in there that should put both of us on the ground within the next hour.”

He set the case against the wall.

“All right.

“I still need the path cleared.”

Asgar looked back.

The booth blocked part of the surface.

“I can’t move it.”

“I’m hoping I can move you.

“We leave the object alone.”

Olmer went around the booth in a wide arc without coming within arm’s reach.

He crouched, studied the point where a red upright met the surface, and stayed there longer than Asgar expected.

Then he asked the post another question, waited for the answer, and carried the case into the building.

He came back out empty-handed.

He was a little shorter than Asgar, broad-shouldered, with short hair and a sun-darkened face.

A wet patch marked one sleeve.

He did not pause by the booth again.

He also did not turn his back to it.

“The node says the measurements on the path don’t agree,” he said.

“Now it also says your biology doesn’t match any active registration.

“And it didn’t register your arrival either.

“Where did you come from?”

Asgar pointed at the booth.

Olmer looked at the door.

“That part I understood.

“Before it?”

Asgar had to begin with August.

He kept it short, omitted most names, described the last two places, explained that he did not choose the next exit and never knew the date in advance.

Olmer listened, occasionally glancing at the post.

Once he asked the system to repeat the translation.

When Asgar finished, he rubbed the wet patch on his sleeve and said:

“The red thing is standing in front of me, and yes, its measurements are wrong.

“As for the rest, I’m not saying anything yet.”

“You don’t believe me.”

“I don’t even know what requires belief yet.

“You’re speaking through an extremely old language and arrived in a way this node has never seen.

“For now that’s enough.”

He looked at the post.

“The node already forwarded everything.

“Until we get an answer, don’t wander far.”

Asgar wanted to say that on this extremely old language he had spoken to his wife.

He stayed silent.

Olmer gave his name and asked whether Asgar had pain, difficulty breathing, nausea.

Asgar said he felt fine.

Explained the air had been checked before he came out.

“The air here is fine.

“For food and water, use the node for now,” Olmer said.

“Don’t try anything from the trees even if you see someone else eating it.”

“I understood that already.”

“Good.

“Then let’s get into the shade.

“I still have to finish with these samples, and you can sit down and tell me what you need right now.”

The switch to the familiar form of address carried no obvious tone in translation.

Asgar did not ask how Olmer’s language handled it.

They moved under a canopy.

Beyond the open edge of the platform grew trees with long narrow leaves.

Something moved between their trunks below, but Asgar could not make it out.

Olmer brought water in a sealed container.

Asgar put the bag beside his feet.

Beneath the jacket, his T-shirt stuck to his back.

“Do people live here?” he asked while taking the jacket off.

“I do.”

“I mean… a lot of people?”

“On the surface? Not compared with other places.

“There are settlements, stations, temporary groups.

“Most people stopped living on the surface a long time ago.”

“In space.”

“Among other places.

“You’ve just come from there yourself.”

Asgar nodded.

Olmer opened the long case and began taking out small sealed containers one at a time.

He placed each in a separate position, waited for a mark on the work surface, then took the next.

“Why are you here?”

“I work with these territories.

“And I like living here.

“Right now I like it less: one section has to be bypassed because the animal route changed.

“That’s why I’ve been getting back later than planned for three days.”

He raised one container toward the light, frowned, and set it aside.

“This one has to be repeated.”

Asgar drank water and watched him finish.

Something clicked beneath the canopy as material cooled.

The forest rustled unevenly, with long pauses.

“Baku used to be here,” Asgar said.

Olmer stopped.

“Are you asking or stating it?”

“Asking.”

“Give the system the name.

“And the time period.

“The name alone may not be enough.”

---

At first the map looked almost familiar.

It appeared across the node’s work surface after Olmer finished with the containers.

Asgar leaned over it.

Found a large continental mass.

Altered sea outlines.

A long mountain chain.

He had expected an alien planet.

He had not expected familiar shapes.

“Here,” he said.

Olmer did not zoom the selected area.

“Show it on the map of your time.”

The node found a historical reconstruction.

The image changed.

Now Asgar recognized the Caspian immediately.

He put a finger on the western shore and asked for a closer view.

The peninsula appeared.

He involuntarily moved nearer to the table.

“Here.

“Baku.”

The node confirmed the name.

The old labels had been translated through the same historical layer.

Asgar recognized several neighboring names and began looking for city streets.

The map would not zoom farther.

“More detail?”

The answer concerned the resolution of the selected source.

Blocks and individual buildings required other data.

“For now, at least give me the coast.”

Olmer enabled comparison.

A second line appeared over the old shoreline.

Then the image became three-dimensional.

Asgar lost the peninsula, found it again on the historical layer, and kept his finger there.

“And where is this now?”

“Depends what you mean by the same place,” Olmer said.

“The old coordinates or the same piece of crust?

“After this much time, those are different things.”

Asgar removed his hand.

“The place where the city was.”

Olmer opened a reconstruction of crustal movement.

Several regions were marked by broad blurred boundaries.

“Accuracy is worse here.

“We can talk about an area.

“No one is going to show you your courtyard.”

The terrain changed slowly in the image.

A basin narrowed and reorganized.

Water occupied different areas, disappeared from some, appeared in others.

Asgar asked him to stop.

Go back.

Play forward again.

He followed the place where he had put his finger until he realized he was following the system’s marker.

The surface itself had stopped helping him long ago.

“When did the city disappear?”

Olmer looked through the source list.

“This map is about rock.

“Not the city.

“You won’t extract the date of Baku’s disappearance from it.”

“But it’s gone.”

“The city is.”

Asgar straightened.

His lower back had gone stiff from leaning over the table.

“Can we get there?”

“To the marked area?

“Part of it lies near my route.

“You can’t just walk the whole region.”

“I don’t need all of it.”

Olmer looked at the map again.

“Tomorrow I’m going to the upper node.

“You can see part of the slopes from there.

“You can come if your environmental clearance finishes normally.

“But I’ll be working.

“We won’t always be able to stop exactly where you want.”

Asgar agreed.

That evening he returned to the booth and told Mirena he had established local communication.

Explained where he would sleep and why he intended to move away from the apparatus.

She asked about the accessibility of the return path.

He resisted calling the outing “not far.”

Instead he went back to Olmer for an estimate and only then finished the conversation.

He slept in a small room inside the node.

The bag stood beside the sleeping surface.

Through the wall came Olmer’s footsteps, then water.

Before lying down, Asgar opened the map again on the local screen.

The old marker appeared in the center.

He removed the modern layer.

The familiar coast returned.

He left it that way.

---

In the morning Olmer issued him a portable translator from the node and showed him how to check the connection.

Then he looked over Asgar’s boots, made him walk across the platform, and asked whether they rubbed.

Asgar said he had already walked in them before.

“You’ll walk in them today too.

“If they start causing problems, say so immediately.”

On the edge of the table lay the promised kit:

folding sheet.

thin cord.

small metal container.

firesteel.

knife in a closed housing.

Olmer showed only what Asgar could easily get wrong.

The knife opened somewhere other than where he expected.

On the first attempt, Asgar pressed the wrong projection.

Olmer closed it again and handed it back.

“Again.”

This time it worked.

Olmer made him test the firesteel outside on a clear surface.

Asgar produced several useless sparks before finding the angle.

He unfolded the sheet himself and did not immediately find the attachment points around the edge.

“I’ll show you the rest later,” Olmer said.

“If you’re going to carry this onward, you need to know what you have before anything else.”

Asgar packed it again and put the kit into his own bag.

Olmer packed a field bag.

Asgar waited by the door, watching containers, rolled material, and a small instrument disappear inside it.

Olmer checked several items by hand even though the system already showed readiness.

“All of that is for work?”

“And for getting back from work.

“Those aren’t always the same thing.”

They followed the path.

Soon it narrowed and passed beneath trees.

The air was cooler there.

Asgar brushed a branch with one shoulder and dry particles fell across his jacket.

At the first descent, Olmer stopped him and showed where the paved surface vanished beneath leaves and where actual soil began.

After that he moved more slowly than he would alone.

Asgar could tell because every so often Olmer took several quick steps, then shortened them again.

Asgar tried not to fall behind.

The forest did not look ancient.

Young shoots emerged near roots.

Fresh wet wood gleamed inside a broken trunk.

Small creatures moved through the sunlit patches too quickly for him to study.

Eighteen million years did not lie around them as a motionless mass.

This place had its own morning.

At a bend, Olmer raised one hand.

Below them, large animals moved among the trees.

Asgar saw dark backs.

A long neck.

A heavy head that turned toward them for an instant.

He stopped, trying not to make sound.

Olmer checked his instrument and led him upslope.

“Long detour?”

“Longer than I’d like.

“Shorter than explaining to them why we decided to stand in their path.”

They climbed to a rocky projection.

Asgar was breathing through his mouth by then.

When Olmer finally stopped beside a small device fixed into the rock, Asgar lowered himself onto the nearest dry stone.

“Drink,” Olmer said, opening his bag.

“Don’t save all your water for the walk back.”

While he worked, Asgar studied the slope.

Bands of light and dark crossed the exposed rock.

Some ran nearly level.

Others were folded and plunged away at sharp angles.

“That was underground?”

Olmer looked where he was pointing.

“Some of it.

“Uplift and erosion here.

“You can reconstruct several different environments from the layers.”

“Sea?”

“For some of them, yes.”

Asgar followed the rock with his eyes.

He remembered water beside the Baku boulevard.

The straight line of the railing.

Paving heated by the sun.

He tried to fit it onto the tilted bands and could not.

Olmer finished checking the device and went to the edge of the platform.

“Look from here.”

Beyond the trees opened a wide basin.

Distant slopes stood in pale haze.

Closer were bare ledges and bands of vegetation.

No familiar shore existed.

Olmer pointed toward an area on the right.

“Part of the reconstructed region lies there.

“With the uncertainty you saw.”

Asgar stood and joined him.

“So somewhere around there was the city.”

“If you track the same piece of crust, roughly there.”

He looked for a long time.

Nothing below was distinct.

He could choose a ledge.

A dark patch of trees.

A scree slope.

Tell himself: there.

He could just as easily choose the neighboring hill.

Asgar asked for the map.

Olmer opened it on the portable device.

The old point sat inside a broad region.

“Zoom in.”

Olmer did.

“More.”

The image grew.

So did the blur.

“There isn’t better data,” Olmer said.

Asgar nodded without looking away from the screen.

He had meant to find the place.

Not the remains of the apartment—he had stopped expecting that.

Just stand where he had once walked.

Look in the direction the windows had faced.

Now even “there” required a qualification.

He looked at the blurred edge of the region.

Inside it lay everything at once:

the courtyard.

the road to school where he had walked the younger one before the boy began going alone.

the turn before home where Asgar used to call Katya and tell her to put the kettle on.

Those places were twenty minutes apart on foot.

Here they fit inside one smear wider than he could cross in a day.

Olmer put the device away only after Asgar stepped back.

“We need to continue.

“My route isn’t finished.”

“Yes.

“Let’s go.”

On the way back, they stopped beside a stream.

Olmer collected a sample.

For drinking, he took water from his own container.

Asgar asked whether they could refill here.

“I’ll test it first.

“I like the clarity too, but clarity doesn’t get me very far.”

He showed Asgar how he marked the sample and explained the difference between a field check and confidence that water was safe to drink long-term.

“If the next place has no node,” Asgar said, “I’ll need things like this.”

Olmer raised his head and looked at him longer than necessary.

“So you intend to go into that thing again.”

“I do.”

“Knowing that next time there may be no node.

“No water.

“No people.”

“Knowing.”

Olmer sealed the sample and put it away.

“Besides what I gave you this morning, what do you have?”

“Clothes.

“Bag.

“A few personal things.”

“Then before you leave we’ll add to the kit.

“And you’ll try everything yourself.

“A knife inside a bag is worth less than a knife a person can open with frozen hands.”

Asgar wanted to say he knew how to open a knife.

Then remembered the morning latch and said nothing.

---

By evening, his legs ached.

He washed his hands, removed his boots, and sat for a while without turning on the screen.

Olmer was preparing food in the next room.

At one point he called Asgar to help move containers.

Asgar stood, carried two.

He took the third awkwardly and nearly dropped it.

Olmer caught the opposite edge.

“This one takes two people.”

They set it down.

Asgar stayed by the table.

“You said there are old archives here.”

“There’s access.

“Depends what you’re looking for.”

“Not a map.”

Olmer waited.

Asgar told him about Martin and Saima.

How information about the booth entered records.

How some stories turned out to be retellings of other stories.

Then he explained what Mirena had told him:

empty module.

external mass.

the machine’s own return.

Olmer asked questions.

Each time, Asgar said where the answer came from.

Several times he had to say he did not know.

The food had grown cold by the time they finished.

Olmer pushed Asgar’s portion toward him and opened a request.

“I can find where to look,” he said.

“I don’t work with this material myself.

“Deep historical records aren’t always available through ordinary search.”

“I need the history of an anomaly like this.

“What people understood about it.

“Not necessarily about me.”

Olmer added several refinements.

The node suggested different directions.

The translator handled some of the names uncertainly.

Asgar asked it to keep the original terms beside the translation.

They finished eating while the node searched accessible repositories.

Then an answer appeared:

the relevant material required access to the archival core.

Olmer read the conditions, checked his schedule, and said he could reach it after the next few field tasks.

“Can you wait a few days?”

Asgar looked at the screen.

Off to one side, the map with the Baku marker was still rolled open.

“I can wait.”

“Then tomorrow we continue with the kit.

“While there’s time, you learn it instead of just packing it.”

Olmer left to clear the dishes.

Asgar opened the map one last time.

The city name sat over the old coastline.

He remembered the drive home.

The turn where he sometimes lost time.

The familiar call to Katya before reaching the building.

Not one of those things could be seen from today’s slope even if Olmer could point to the exact place without uncertainty.

Asgar closed the map and returned to the archive request.

The time field contained an enormous range.

The location field still held the selected region.

Asgar removed the location restriction.
