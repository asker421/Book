Chapter 24. Where the Shore Used to Be

A branch swayed beyond the glass.

Asgar watched it until it returned to its previous position. The wind came in gusts: leaves turned their pale sides outward, pressed together, then spread apart again. Between them he could see a slope covered with low trees. Higher up was a band of bare stone.

He was still holding the receiver to his ear.

"I can see ground," he said. "Plants. Looks like open air."

Mirena asked him to describe the area in front of the door. Asgar leaned down. A smooth surface ran beneath the booth; farther on began a strip of fine gravel. To the right, behind the branches, the corner of a low building projected outward.

While the check was running, he looked at the band of stone above the trees. After the ring, an ordinary slope felt almost like home. His eyes found up and down without effort; everything heavy lay where it was supposed to.

"The air is suitable, pressure is normal. You can open the door," Mirena said. "Don't drink the water, don't eat anything local, and don't touch living organisms until they're checked on site."

"Understood. Do you have the date?"

She gave the year.

Asgar thought he had missed the boundary between words.

"Repeat that."

Mirena repeated:

"Eighteen million four hundred three thousand two hundred twenty-one."

He took his hand off the handle.

The branch beyond the glass kept moving. One leaf caught its edge on another, came free, turned. Asgar watched that tiny movement and tried to place the number she had named in front of it.

"Millions?"

"Yes."

He did not answer.

The years of waiting they had discussed before the transition still had something to do with him. This number would not attach itself to anything familiar. He remembered drawings of ancient animals, bands of geological eras, tiny captions beneath museum bones. In his memory, all of that belonged to the past. Ahead, he had imagined cities.

"Asgar?"

"I hear you. I'll open it now."

The door moved heavily. He held it and breathed in warm air. It smelled of heated leaves and something sharp and resinous. Beyond the trees, something crackled softly; the sound moved, faded, then appeared somewhere else.

A voice came from the building.

Asgar froze at the threshold. The voice repeated a short sequence of unfamiliar sounds.

"There's a system or a person here. They're speaking, but I don't understand."

"Try to configure local translation. I'll remain available through the telephone while the channel holds."

He said he was putting down the receiver, returned it to the cradle, and lifted the bag.

Outside, beside the path, stood a dark post. When he stepped toward it, a pale symbol appeared at the top. The voice sounded again.

"I don't understand. Russian. Can you speak Russian?"

The symbol changed. For several seconds nothing happened. Then the system pronounced two familiar words with the spaces between syllables in the wrong places.

Asgar repeated his request more slowly.

The reply grew longer. He made out "speech" and "wait," then heard the whole sentence:

"Matching against historical language record. Continue speaking."

He gave his name, said he was beside a red cabin and needed help. The system asked him to repeat the last word, then reported that translation was available, though individual meanings might require clarification.

"Where am I?"

"Ground biosphere node."

A local name followed. Asgar could not repeat it.

"Is this Earth?"

"Yes."

The post asked him not to move away. Several symbols changed across its upper section; then the system reported that direct presence on the site was permissible. Water, food, and contact with local organisms remained restricted until a separate check.

Another line appeared: passage through the site was temporarily closed. Information about an unknown person and object had been forwarded for review; until a response arrived, Asgar was instructed to remain near the node.

Asgar looked at the branches over the path and took half a step away from them.

---

Olmer came out of the building carrying a long case.

He took two steps and stopped.

His gaze moved across the red body, dropped to the surface beneath it, then returned upward. He did not set the case down. He backed toward the wall without turning his back on the booth and said something short toward the post.

The post answered him in its own language. He asked again. Listened. Asked once more, more briefly.

Only then did an understandable voice sound, with a slight delay:

"Move away from the object. Over here, by the wall. Don't rush."

Asgar picked up the bag and moved over.

"Did you open anything there? Touch anything outside?"

"The door. From inside."

"Nothing else."

"Nothing else."

Olmer looked at his hands, then his face.

"Headache? Any taste in your mouth? Burning skin?"

"No."

He addressed the post again. In the translation Asgar made out "radiation," "field," and a word rendered as "biological risk." The answer took a long time. Olmer listened without taking his eyes off the booth and raised one hand when Asgar started to speak.

"The node sees no immediate threat," he said at last. "It isn't supposed to see everything either. But there's nothing there that will put both of us on the ground within the next hour."

He set the case by the wall.

"Then this is what we'll do. I still need to clear the path."

Asgar looked back. The booth occupied part of the surface.

"I can't move it."

"I'm hoping I can move you. We'll leave the object where it is."

Olmer circled the booth in a wide arc, never coming closer than an outstretched arm. He crouched, looked at where the red upright met the surface, and stayed there longer than Asgar expected. Then he asked the post another question, waited for the answer, and carried the case inside the building.

He came back out empty-handed.

He was slightly shorter than Asgar, broad-shouldered, with short hair and a tanned face. A damp patch darkened one sleeve. He no longer lingered near the booth, but he did not turn his back on it either.

"The node reports a measurement mismatch on the path," he said. "Now it also says your biology doesn't match any active registration. And it didn't register your arrival either. Where did you come from?"

Asgar pointed at the booth.

Olmer looked at the door.

"I got that. Before it?"

He had to start the story with August. Asgar kept it short, left out most names, named the last two places, and explained that he never chose the next exit in advance.

Olmer listened, occasionally glancing at the post. Once he asked the system to repeat the translation. When Asgar finished, he rubbed at the damp patch on his sleeve and said:

"The red thing is right in front of me, and its measurements really are a mess. I can't say anything about the rest yet."

"You don't believe me."

"I don't even know yet what here requires belief. You're speaking through a very old language and you arrived in a way the node has never seen. For now, that's enough for me."

He looked at the post.

"The node has already sent everything onward. Until the answer comes back, don't go far."

Asgar wanted to say that he had spoken to his wife in this very old language. He did not.

Olmer gave his name and asked whether Asgar had pain, difficulty breathing, or nausea. Asgar said he felt normal. Explained that the air had been checked before he came out.

"The air here is suitable. Water and food go through the node for now," Olmer said. "Don't try anything off the trees. Even if you see someone else eating it."

"I understand already."

"Good. Then let's go into the shade. I still need to finish with the samples, and you can sit and tell me what you need right now."

The shift to informal address came through the translation without much nuance. Asgar did not try to work out how that distinction functioned in Olmer's language.

They moved under the canopy. Outside, beyond the open edge of the site, grew trees with long narrow leaves. Something moved below between the trunks, but he could not make it out.

Olmer brought water in a sealed container. Asgar set the bag by his feet. Under the jacket, his T-shirt was stuck to his back.

"Do people live here?" he asked, taking the jacket off.

"I do."

"I mean... a lot of people?"

"On the surface—not many compared with other places. There are settlements, stations, temporary groups. Most people have lived off the surface for a long time."

"In space."

"Among other places. You just came from there yourself."

Asgar nodded. Olmer opened the case and began taking out small sealed containers one by one. He placed each in its own position, waited for a marker on the work surface, then took the next.

"Why are you here?"

"I work with these territories. And I like living here. Less so right now: I have to detour around one area because the animals changed their route. It's made me come back later than planned for the third day in a row."

He raised one container to the light, frowned, and set it aside.

"I'll have to repeat this one."

Asgar drank water and watched Olmer finish his work. Somewhere under the canopy, cooling material clicked. The forest made uneven noise with long pauses.

"Baku used to be here," Asgar said.

Olmer stopped.

"Are you asking or stating?"

"Asking."

"Give the system the name. And the time it belongs to. The name alone may not be enough."

---

At first, the map looked almost familiar.

It appeared on the work surface inside the node after Olmer finished with the containers. Asgar leaned in, found the large continental mass, shifted outlines of seas, a long chain of mountains. He had expected an alien planet. He had not expected familiar shapes.

"Here," he said.

Olmer did not enlarge the selected area.

"Show it on a map from your time."

The node found a historical reconstruction. The image changed. Now Asgar recognized the Caspian immediately, touched the western shore with a finger, then asked for a closer view.

A peninsula appeared.

He involuntarily moved closer to the table.

"Here. Baku."

The node confirmed the name. Old labels were translated through the same historical layer. Asgar recognized several neighboring names and began looking for city streets.

The map would not zoom any farther.

"Can it show more detail?"

The answer referred to the resolution of the selected source. For neighborhoods and individual buildings, other data would be required.

"For now I at least need the shoreline."

Olmer enabled the comparison. Another line overlaid the old outline, then the image became three-dimensional. Asgar lost the peninsula, found it again on the old layer, and held his finger on it.

"And where is this now?"

"Depends what you mean by the same place," Olmer said. "The old coordinates or the same piece of crust? After this much time, those are different things."

Asgar took his hand away.

"The site of the city."

Olmer opened a reconstruction of crustal movement. Several areas were marked by broad blurred boundaries.

"Accuracy is worse here. We can talk about a region. No one is going to show you the courtyard."

The relief changed slowly on the image. A depression narrowed and re-formed; water occupied different regions, disappeared from some and appeared in others. Asgar asked to stop the motion, go back, run it forward again. He followed the place where he had put his finger until he realized he was following the system's marker now. The surface itself had long since stopped helping him.

"When did the city disappear?"

Olmer looked at the list of sources.

"This map is about rock, not a city. You can't pull a date for Baku's disappearance from it."

"But it isn't there."

"The city isn't."

Asgar straightened. His lower back ached from the awkward position.

"Can we get there?"

"To the marked area? Part of it lies near my route. You can't simply walk across the whole region."

"I don't need the whole region."

Olmer looked at the map again.

"Tomorrow I'm going to the upper node. From there you'll be able to see part of the slopes. You can come with me if your environmental clearance comes back normal. But I'll be working. We won't always be able to stop where you want."

Asgar agreed.

That evening he returned to the booth and told Mirena he had established local contact. He explained where he would spend the night and why he intended to move away from the telephone. She clarified whether he would still have a route back. He did not call the trip "not far"; he went back to Olmer for an estimate and only then finished the conversation.

He spent the night in a small room at the node. The bag stood beside the sleeping area. Through the wall came Olmer's footsteps, then the sound of water.

Before lying down, Asgar opened the map again on the local screen. The old marker appeared in the center.

He removed the modern layer. The familiar shoreline returned.

He left it that way.

---

In the morning, Olmer gave him a portable translator from the node and showed him how to check the connection. Then he looked over Asgar's boots, asked him to walk around the site, and asked whether they rubbed.

Asgar said he had walked in them before.

"You're going to walk in them today too. If they start bothering you, say so immediately."

On the edge of the table lay the promised kit: a foldable sheet, thin cord, a small metal container, a fire striker, and a knife in a closed housing. Olmer showed him only what Asgar might otherwise get wrong. The knife did not open where Asgar expected; on his first try he pressed the wrong projection. Olmer closed it again and handed it back.

"Again."

This time it worked.

Olmer made him try the striker outside on a clear part of the site. Asgar threw several useless sparks before he found the angle. He unfolded the sheet himself and did not immediately find the attachment points along the edges.

"I'll show you the rest later," Olmer said. "If you're going to carry this farther, you first need to know exactly what you have."

Asgar folded the items again and packed the kit into his bag.

Olmer packed a field bag. Asgar waited by the door, watching containers, rolled material, a small instrument disappear into it. Olmer checked several things by hand even though the system already showed readiness.

"All of that for work?"

"And to get back from work. Not always the same thing."

They followed the path, which soon narrowed and turned under the trees. The air was cooler there. Asgar brushed a branch with his shoulder, and dry particles fell over his jacket.

At the first descent, Olmer stopped him and showed him where the paving was hidden under leaves and where soil began. After that he walked more slowly than he would alone. It showed in the way he sometimes took several quick steps, then shortened his stride again.

Asgar tried not to fall behind.

The forest did not look ancient. Young shoots pushed up near roots; fresh wet wood gleamed on a broken trunk. Small creatures moved in the sunlight too quickly for him to see clearly. Eighteen million years did not lie around them as a motionless thickness. This place had its own morning.

At a bend, Olmer raised a hand.

Large animals were moving below between the trees. Asgar made out dark backs, a long neck, a heavy head that turned toward them for a moment. He stopped, trying not to make noise.

Olmer checked his instrument and led him uphill.

"Long detour?"

"Longer than I'd like. Faster than finding out why we've stopped across their path."

They climbed to a rocky outcrop. Asgar was already breathing through his mouth. When Olmer finally stopped beside a small device fixed into the rock, Asgar lowered himself onto the nearest dry stone.

"Drink water," Olmer said, opening his bag. "Don't save all of it for the way back."

While Olmer worked, Asgar studied the slope. Light and dark bands alternated in the exposed cut. Some ran almost straight, others were crumpled and dipped sharply downward.

"Was this underground?"

Olmer looked where he was pointing.

"Part of it. Uplift and erosion here. You can reconstruct several different conditions from the layers."

"Sea?"

"For some of them, yes."

Asgar followed the stone with his eyes. He remembered the water along Baku Boulevard, the straight line of the railing, pavement warmed by the sun. He tried to combine it with the tilted bands and could not.

Olmer finished checking the device and walked to the edge of the site.

"Look from here."

Beyond the trees opened a broad depression. Distant slopes stood in pale haze; nearer were bare ledges and bands of vegetation. There was no familiar shore.

Olmer pointed to an area on the right.

"Part of the reconstructed region is there. With the uncertainty you saw."

Asgar stood and went over to him.

"So the city was somewhere around there."

"If you follow the same piece of crust—roughly there."

He looked for a long time.

Nothing below stood out. He could choose an outcrop, a dark patch of trees, a band of scree and tell himself: there. He could just as easily choose the neighboring slope.

Asgar asked for the map. Olmer unfolded it on the portable device. The old point sat inside a broad area.

"Zoom in."

Olmer zoomed.

"More."

The image grew, but the boundary blurred along with it.

"There isn't more precise data," Olmer said.

Asgar nodded, still looking at the screen.

He had intended to find the place. Not the remains of the apartment—he no longer expected that. Just stand where he once walked, look in the direction the windows had faced. Now even "there" required a qualification.

He looked at the blurred edge of the region. Inside it fit everything at once: the courtyard, the road to school along which he had walked the younger boy until he started going alone, the turn before the house where Asgar always called Katya so she could put the kettle on. Those places were twenty minutes apart on foot. Here they lay inside one patch, and the patch was wider than he could walk in a day.

Olmer put away the device only after Asgar stepped back.

"We need to keep going. I haven't finished the route."

"Yes. Let's go."

On the way back, they stopped by a stream. Olmer took a sample but drank from his own container. Asgar asked whether they could refill.

"I'll check first. I like how clear it is too, but that won't get me very far."

He showed how he labeled the sample and explained the difference between a field check and confidence that water was safe to drink over time.

"If the next stop doesn't have a node," Asgar said, "I'll need things like this."

Olmer raised his head and looked at him longer than necessary.

"So you're planning to go back into it."

"I am."

"Knowing next time there may be no node, no water, no people."

"Knowing that."

Olmer closed the sample and put it into his bag.

"Besides what I gave you this morning, do you have anything?"

"Clothes. The bag. A few personal things."

"Then before you leave we'll add to the kit. And you'll try everything yourself. A knife in a bag is less useful than a knife a person can open with frozen hands."

Asgar wanted to say he knew how to open a knife but remembered the latch that morning and kept quiet.

---

By evening his legs were humming. He washed his hands, took off his boots, and sat for a while without turning on the screen.

Olmer was dealing with food in the next room. At one point he called Asgar to help carry containers. Asgar stood, carried two, took the third awkwardly and nearly dropped it. Olmer caught the other side.

"This one takes two."

They set it down. Asgar stayed by the table.

"You said there are old archives here."

"There's access. Depends what you're looking for."

"Not a map."

Olmer waited.

Asgar told him about Martin and Saima. About how information on the booth had entered records, how some accounts became retellings of others. Then he explained what he had learned from Mirena: an empty module, external mass, the machine's own return. Olmer asked questions, and each time Asgar said where he knew the answer from. Several times he had to say that he did not have an answer.

The food had time to cool. Olmer pushed a portion toward him and opened a query.

"I can find where to look," he said. "I don't work with this myself. Deep historical material isn't always accessible through ordinary search."

"I need the history of an anomaly like this. What people understood about it. It doesn't have to be about me."

Olmer entered several more clarifications. The node suggested different directions; the translator rendered some of the names uncertainly.

Asgar asked it to keep the original labels beside the translation.

They finished eating while the node searched for accessible repositories. Then an answer appeared: relevant materials required access to the archival core. Olmer read the conditions, checked his schedule, and said he could get there after the nearest field tasks.

"Can you wait a few days?"

Asgar looked at the screen. Off to the side, the folded map with the Baku marker remained.

"I can wait."

"Then tomorrow we'll continue with the kit. While there's time, learn it instead of just packing things."

Olmer left to clear the dishes.

Asgar opened the map one last time. The city's name sat on the old shoreline. He remembered the road home, the turn where he sometimes lost time, the habitual call to Katya before reaching the building. None of those things could be seen from today's slope even if Olmer had been able to point to the place without uncertainty.

He closed the map and returned to the archive request.

The time field contained an enormous range. The location field still held the selected region.

Asgar removed the location restriction.
