Chapter 22. What, Exactly?

Tessa left both models open.

Asgar sat in front of them until the differences stopped leaping out at him. At first he still tracked regions that vanished when his mass was removed, followed others that appeared in their place, returned the image to its previous position.

Then he began looking only at what stayed.

The overall direction.

He tried naming it differently in his head.

Displacement.

Sequence.

Process development.

Changing the word changed nothing: without him, the machine was still going somewhere.

Tessa worked on her side of the table.

Several times she paused the image and checked a value against a separate record.

Asgar waited for her to discover another dependency they had forgotten to remove.

Like the bag.

“What if some part of me is still in there?” he asked.

“That is exactly what I’m checking.”

He wanted her to say models like this were often wrong.

After a while, Tessa took her hands off the table.

“Everything I could verify has been removed.

“The direction remains.”

“Then I’ll ask them.”

She turned one of the records toward him.

“Say this exactly:

“‘We removed your direct contribution and the direction remained. We do not have an endpoint. We did not reproduce the machine’s initial state. If you have design data, answer from those.’”

Asgar repeated the wording.

Tessa corrected one word.

On the way to the booth, he kept close to the inner wall.

Beyond the broad glazing, the ring curved upward, and where his eyes still insisted there ought to be a ceiling, distant occupied sections glowed.

He could cross this part without stopping now.

He still could not look for long.

The booth stood in the isolated section of the passage with its door open.

They had kept the space around it clear.

The local instruments were outside the boundary.

Inside, everything was unchanged:

glass.

cramped floor.

telephone on the right.

Asgar stepped in, left the door open, and lifted the receiver.

Mirena answered after a pause.

“I’m listening.”

“She asked me to repeat this word for word.”

Asgar recited what he had learned at the table.

“We removed your direct contribution and the direction remained. We do not have an endpoint. We did not reproduce the machine’s initial state. If you have design data, answer from those.”

Mirena said nothing.

“Mirena.”

“I’m here.

“Repeat the first part.”

He did.

“Again. More slowly.”

Asgar repeated it a third time, separating the words the way the registration officer had once separated the digits of his year.

Mirena had never asked him to repeat the same sentence three times before.

Something moved on the other end of the line.

“What does ‘removed your contribution’ mean?

“What, exactly, did they remove from the calculation?”

He explained as best he could.

For several days they had measured what changed with him and what changed on its own.

Then they built a second calculation with nothing tied directly to his body.

Some lines disappeared.

Others connected differently.

The overall progression remained.

“Mass and position? Both?”

“Position was measured.

“Mass was calculated.”

“Those are different.”

“I know.

“She said that too.”

Mirena asked about repeated checks.

Asgar listed the series he remembered and added the detail about the bag: one whole sequence had been rerun because its position had been entered incorrectly.

“Who caught that?”

“She did.

“I wouldn’t have.”

The next pause was longer.

Only steady line noise remained.

“Now I need one thing exactly,” Mirena said at last.

“Did this direction appear after they removed you from the model?

“Or was it already there before?”

Asgar opened his mouth and said nothing.

He had seen both diagrams side by side.

Seen lines disappear.

He had never once asked what the first one had looked like before any of this.

Before him.

“I don’t know.”

“I can bring her onto the line.”

“Not yet.

“This is enough for the engineers to know what to look for.”

“You didn’t have this calculation yourselves?”

“We have a different model.

“I’m not going to pretend they’re the same.”

Asgar ran a thumb along the edge of the black telephone housing.

“All right.

“Forget the models.

“What is returning, Mirena?”

The line went quiet.

“I’ll send that exact question.

“I need the original project file. I don’t have it in front of me.”

“You’re supervising the return.”

“Yes.”

“And you don’t know what you’re returning?”

“I know what I’m supposed to do during the return.

“You’re asking why the module was built in the first place.

“I don’t know that right now, and I’m not going to invent an answer.”

He wanted to say he had lived long enough on guesses.

Instead he asked when to call back.

They agreed he would return after he had rested.

Mirena did not promise the answer would be ready.

---

Back in his room, Asgar removed his jacket and hung it over the chair.

The bag lay beside the bed.

Tobin’s wooden piece stood on the table where he had left it that morning.

He went to get food and ate without noticing much of the taste.

Then he lay down on top of the blanket.

The two models stayed in his head.

He tried to remember which one had a branch that bent more sharply.

As though the exact curve might change the question.

Then he began inventing the answer.

It happened by itself.

The engineers opened the old project.

They found a date.

Or not a date—a way to specify one.

Mirena said:

Now we know where it’s going.

Now we can change it.

Asgar would reach that point in the imagined conversation and start again from the beginning, each time with slightly different words, as though the right phrasing might make it more likely to come true.

Once he reached the part where he would tell Katya.

He could go no farther.

He did not know how old she would be in that conversation.

Asgar sat up, put his feet on the floor, and stayed like that for a while.

The floor was warm.

Someone walked past beyond the wall, going about their business.

The sound was completely ordinary.

He lay down again.

He did not sleep.

When he returned to the booth, people were using the passage.

Two stopped at the marked detour, talked briefly, and went another way.

One carried a long flat object beneath an arm.

Asgar watched them leave.

The booth cost them a few extra steps.

Nothing more.

He entered and lifted the receiver again.

“Asgar,” Mirena said.

“I have an answer.”

He leaned a shoulder against the wall.

“Go ahead.”

“The engineers retrieved the old project and compared it with the first failure.

“The module was sent empty.

“There was never supposed to be a person inside.”

Asgar looked through the open doorway.

“There’s room to stand in here.”

“The space was left for maintenance.

“Not for a passenger.”

He shifted one foot.

There was very little space between the toe of his boot and the opposite wall.

After all this time, the cramped interior had become familiar.

He knew how to turn his shoulder to reach the receiver.

Where to put the bag.

How to hold the door with his free hand.

“So I walked into a place I was never supposed to enter at all.”

“The door was accessible.

“But the return calculation did not account for a person inside.”

“Then why did it let the door close?”

“Because the system checks total mass after closure.

“That is when it detected the excess.

“Before that, no emergency had been registered.”

He remembered the first ring.

First the door.

Then the telephone.

At the time, that had seemed like the natural order:

he entered.

someone saw him inside.

someone called.

“So it rang because it detected extra weight.”

Mirena did not answer immediately.

“The system raised an emergency.

“We opened the voice channel.”

“To me.”

“To the person who happened to be inside.”

Asgar lowered his head.

The water he had carried in from the riverbank had long since dried.

A faint mark remained near the edge of his boot.

He rubbed at it with the sole.

“Keep going.”

“After that, the direct return failed.

“The system went into emergency mode.

“Your ‘windows’ are moments when it briefly manages to hold a stable state and make contact with an external environment.”

“For me to get out.”

“You can get out if the location is safe.

“But the window does not appear for your sake.

“It appears because the module is still returning.”

Asgar looked at the door again.

On August twentieth, he had stepped outside.

The booth had remained behind him.

At the time, simply being outside again had seemed like the important event.

“That’s why nothing ended then.”

“Because your exit was not the completion of its task.”

“And afterward?

“All those stops…”

“It kept trying to return with you inside it.”

Asgar gripped the receiver harder.

Individual words still begged to be challenged.

He wanted Mirena to replace them with something more precise.

Wanted another check.

Another qualification.

“It isn’t returning you, Asgar,” Mirena said.

“It’s returning itself.”

He pulled the receiver away from his ear.

A faint steady hiss came through it.

Asgar stared at the black perforations near the bottom until they blurred, then brought it back.

Mirena stayed silent.

Same glass.

Same screw near the lower edge of the telephone.

Same handle worn pale where hands had touched it.

Asgar knew every part of this place and until this moment had believed he understood why he kept entering it.

He pictured the house in Ar.

The morning he had chosen not to wake there again.

Bertram going to the lower passage with another person.

The shirt Tyra had removed from the bag still sitting on the shelf.

He had left it because he had never worn it.

He remembered the fabric in her hands.

His own nod.

How easily he had agreed not to take something unnecessary.

“The source,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The point it was sent from.”

“Yes.

“The point the module was sent from.”

“Not August.”

“There is no reason to think the source is August 2014.”

He waited.

Mirena added nothing.

“And not my home.”

“No.

“Your home is not part of the module’s task at all.”

Asgar closed his eyes.

Until now, uncertainty had always left room for luck.

The machine was returning.

He was inside it.

If he held on, understood enough, reached whoever had built it, maybe he could get home.

He had heard the caveats.

Repeated them to other people himself.

But beneath every caveat had remained one certainty:

the movement had something to do with his return.

He could no longer find that certainty in anything Mirena had said.

“How long have you known?”

“The full task was confirmed after your question.”

“Today?”

“While you were resting.”

“And before they checked?”

“We knew about the excess mass and the emergency return.

“That was enough to support the process.

“The full old project isn’t kept in the active work package.

“After your question, the engineers went into the archive and compared the original design with the first failure.”

“Your engineers? Or the service?”

“This is the answer that was assembled for you now.

“I don’t know which employees had seen the old project before.

“Before this review, I didn’t have the full picture.”

“Convenient.”

“What part?”

“Everyone knows one piece.

“One person sees mass.

“One person manages the process.

“Another retrieves the old layer.

“And I walk through door after door thinking I’m the one being returned.”

“I understand that for you this changes—”

“Don’t say you understand.”

She stopped.

Someone passed outside the open doorway along the far side of the corridor.

Asgar turned his back to the glass.

“You used the word ‘return’ the whole time.

“From the beginning.

“And now it turns out the word was about a metal box.”

“I used ‘return’ for the module because that is what we call the process.

“Every time you asked about your home, I told you we could not choose the date.”

“But you didn’t tell me this.”

“I didn’t know this.”

“You could have found out.”

The last words came out louder than he intended.

Mirena let the silence sit.

“Yes.

“We should have asked earlier.

“I could have asked too.

“But I didn’t know the answer, and I wasn’t hiding it from you.”

He remembered river water.

The bag between his boots.

Her voice before the door closed.

“In Ar you let me choose.”

“Yes.”

“Stay or continue.

“With what I knew at the time.”

“Yes.

“With what we knew at the time.

“I did not promise you 2014.

“But we did not know this about the original task either.”

“I was standing in front of the door, Mirena.

“You could have said: wait. First let’s find out who this whole thing is even working for.”

“I could have.

“I didn’t.”

“And there was a safe way to end it.

“And I left.”

“You chose to continue.

“I cannot pretend I made that choice for you.

“But I’m not going to pretend you had the full picture either.”

Asgar straightened sharply.

“Of course.

“My choice.

“That’s a very good arrangement you have.

“Everything that happens to the machine is ‘the process.’

“Everything that happens to me is ‘my choice.’”

“I didn’t say that.”

But he no longer wanted to parse what she had said.

He wanted someone on the other end to stop answering evenly.

Wanted some part of what had happened to feel heavy there too.

“Your shift will end,” he said.

“You’ll put down the headset and go home.

“Someone else sits down in your place.

“You can even tell them it was a difficult conversation.

“And I don’t leave this.

“Not really.

“I have to keep living inside it while you rest.”

The line went completely quiet.

Asgar realized his thumbnail was digging into the seam of the receiver.

He relaxed his grip but did not lower it.

“Are you finished?” Mirena asked.

He said nothing.

“You can be angry with me.

“You can demand explanations.

“But my shift and my life outside this line have nothing to do with what happened to you.”

“I was only—”

“No.

“You are trying to hurt me right now.

“I’m not continuing that part of the conversation.”

He stared at the door hinge.

“I will answer questions about the module and about what we confirmed,” Mirena added.

“If you still have them.”

He ran a palm over his face and touched the pale scar above his eyebrow.

There were many questions.

Why the windows looked like this.

Whether the task could be altered.

What the people at the source could do.

Who had left the door accessible.

Who had decided that “supporting” a person trapped inside was enough.

He could not ask any of them without returning to her shift.

“Give me whoever decides,” Asgar said.

Mirena did not answer immediately.

“Wait.”

He stood with the receiver pressed between shoulder and ear.

Something moved on the other end.

Then the background changed.

The faint steady noise he had long ago stopped noticing disappeared, and he noticed it by its absence.

“Asgar.”

A man’s voice.

Even.

No pressure in it.

“My name is Corwin.

“I’m responsible for what is permissible on this line.”

Asgar straightened against the wall.

“So the refusals are yours.”

“Some of them.”

“Then explain why people have been saying ‘return’ to me for this long and no one added that the word had nothing to do with me.”

“Because no one retrieved the original project until your question.”

Corwin did not leave room for interruption.

“That is not an excuse.

“It is the sequence of events.”

“I don’t care what you call it.”

“I can see that.”

Asgar waited.

Corwin did not continue.

“Do you understand what happened to me?”

“We have unplanned living mass inside an unstable return,” Corwin said.

“So far that is the only description that does not promise more than we know.”

Asgar pulled the receiver away and put it back.

“Mass.”

“Yes.”

“I’m a person.”

“I know.

“That does not change what I am responsible for.”

Two people passed outside the open door.

Asgar turned toward the wall.

“You were there when I got trapped,” he said.

“At the beginning.”

“I was.”

“And you ordered them not to open the door.”

“Yes.”

“I was inside.”

“There was a moving boundary inside.

“If the operator had opened the door at that moment, you would have stepped into an unfinished state.

“I do not know what would have been left of you.

“No one does.

“That is why the door stayed closed.”

Asgar said nothing.

“You want me to say that decision was difficult,” Corwin continued.

“It was.

“That did not change the decision then and it does not change it now.”

He paused briefly.

The first pause of the conversation.

“Since you asked for me, I’ll tell you something else.

“Everything you request beyond ordinary support costs someone.

“Not you.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means Mirena paid for your recent requests with her clearance.

“I marked it twice.”

Asgar looked through the open door.

“She never told me.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Ask her.

“I’m not telling you this on her behalf.

“It is cheaper for me if you know the rules than if you break them without knowing they exist.”

Asgar wanted to say something that would remain with the man on the other end.

He considered two sentences.

Discarded both.

Corwin had already accounted for them.

They would change nothing.

“I heard you,” Asgar said.

“Good.”

The line clicked.

The faint steady background returned.

Asgar did not move the receiver.

He remembered Mirena saying they could wait a little when he had been afraid to step onto the ring.

Asking whether anything was pulling him.

Giving him the year without adding commentary.

He had no idea which of those moments had cost her anything.

“I’m here,” Mirena said.

Asgar held the receiver and did not know where to begin.

She did not ask what Corwin had told him.

“Does the source know I’m here?” he asked at last.

“The service knows there is a person inside the module.

“That does not create a ready way to send you home.”

“So there is no solution.”

“No.

“Not now.”

Asgar looked for a long time at the corridor outside—

what he still thought of as a street even though beyond the glass lay an enclosed passage inside a structure around a planet.

“I’ll call again.”

“All right.”

He put the receiver back.

It landed crooked the first time.

He corrected it and kept one hand on it for several seconds.

---

The worktable was not far.

Asgar knew the route by heart now:

out of the isolated section.

two turns.

interior passage where he could not see the black.

He walked more slowly than usual.

Three people passed him.

One carried a rolled sheet over one shoulder.

Two were talking and turned into a side passage halfway through a sentence.

A person stood by the wall looking into a device, shifting from foot to foot.

All of it was happening today exactly as it had yesterday.

It would happen again tomorrow.

Asgar would have to say aloud what he had learned.

He tried the sentence in his head.

It always came out too short.

And wrong.

It made it sound as though he had simply been mistaken all this time.

Not as though no one had told him.

At the interior passage, he stopped briefly beside the glass.

The ring curved upward.

Looking was still difficult.

Today it was easier than continuing.

Then he kept walking.

Tessa was at the worktable.

One of the models was gone.

The other had been reduced and moved aside.

Records Asgar had not seen before occupied the main area.

She noticed him, finished what she was doing, and turned.

“They answered?”

He nodded.

“Can you tell me exactly what they confirmed?”

Asgar remained standing.

“It was sent empty.

“The interior space is for maintenance.

“No people were ever intended to ride in it.”

Tessa looked at the model, then back at him.

He told her about the mass check after the door closed.

The failed direct return.

The temporary stable states.

The more he repeated the facts, the easier they became to say.

They fit into a sequence.

There were ordinary words for them.

“That came from a review of the original design documentation?” Tessa asked.

“Yes.

“Mirena said the engineers pulled the old project layer and compared it with the first event.”

Tessa made a note.

“Then the picture fits better.

“Our model by itself could not tell us why the module was returning.

“The design can.”

Asgar watched her hands.

“You aren’t surprised.”

“I am.

“I had several possibilities in mind, and this was one of them.

“Now there is evidence for it.

“I still need to see what it changes in our calculations.”

He almost asked whether she understood what it meant for him.

Almost in the same words he had just thrown at Mirena.

Tessa cleared a space at the edge of the table.

“Do you want to sit?”

“No.”

She did not insist.

“That’s enough for today.

“I can run the next calculation without you.”

Asgar nodded and walked toward the exit.

At the door he stopped.

“Show me both again.”

Tessa restored the two models side by side.

Now he looked at them differently.

Yesterday, one model had contained him and one had not.

Today, both belonged to the machine.

“That’s enough,” he said.

---

His room was exactly as he had left it.

The jacket hung over the chair.

The bag lay beside the bed.

Asgar took out the bundle containing the phone, unwrapped the cloth, and pressed the button even though he already knew nothing would happen.

The dark glass reflected part of his face.

He turned the phone sideways.

The pale scar was more visible.

Asgar stopped looking and placed the device on the table beside the wooden piece.

He needed to eat.

Or wash.

He could send Tessa a question he had failed to formulate.

He could go back to the booth and tell Mirena that what he had said about her shift had been wrong.

Several times he returned to that thought:

go back.

tell her the part about her shift had been wrong.

Each time he postponed it.

The argument continued in his head without Mirena.

And inside that argument she always answered a little worse than she actually had.

Asgar sat on the edge of the bed.

People walked past beyond the wall.

A door closed.

Someone returned to the neighboring room.

He remembered telling Tyra he wanted to reach the source.

Back then the word could contain everything he did not know.

He could know nothing about the date, the place, the world—

and still say it as though it pointed toward Katya.

Now the source meant something else.

Asgar lifted the bag onto the bed and opened it.

He took out the top change of clothes.

Beneath it lay the T-shirt he had brought from home.

He pulled one edge free but did not remove it.

For a while he sat holding the fabric.

Then he carefully tucked it back.

Until this day, he had been waiting for the journey to end.

For the first time, he no longer knew what the end of the journey would mean for him.
