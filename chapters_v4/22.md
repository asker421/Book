Chapter 22. What Exactly

Tessa left both models up.

Asgar sat in front of them until the difference stopped jumping out at him. At first he still found the sections that had disappeared after his mass was excluded, followed others that had appeared in their place, returned the image to its previous position. Then he began looking only at what remained.

The overall direction.

He tried calling it something else in his head. Shift. Sequence. Development of the process. Changing the word changed nothing: without him, the machine was still going somewhere.

Tessa worked on her side of the table. Several times she froze the image and checked a value against a separate record. Asgar waited for another dependency to appear, one they had forgotten to remove. Like the bag.

"What if we left something of mine in there?" he asked.

"That's exactly what I'm checking."

He wanted to hear that models like this were often wrong.

After a while, Tessa took her hands off the table.

"We removed everything I was able to verify. The direction remained."

"Then I'll ask them."

She turned one of the records toward him.

"Say it like this: we removed your direct contribution, and the direction remained. We don't have an endpoint, and we did not reproduce the machine's initial state. If they have design data, they should answer from that."

Asgar repeated the wording. Tessa corrected one word.

On the way to the booth, he stayed by the inner wall. Beyond the broad glazing, the ring curved upward, and distant rooms glowed where his eyes still insisted there should be a ceiling. He could walk this section now without stopping. He still could not look for long.

The booth stood in the cordoned-off part of the passage with its door open. Free space had been kept around it. The local instruments were outside; inside, everything was the same as before: glass, cramped floor, telephone on the right.

Asgar stepped in, left the door open, and lifted the receiver.

Mirena answered after a pause.

"I'm listening."

"She asked me to repeat this word for word."

Asgar repeated what he had memorized at the table:

"We removed your direct contribution, and the direction remained. We don't have an endpoint, and we did not reproduce the machine's initial state. If you have design data, answer from that."

Mirena did not answer.

"Mirena."

"I'm here. Repeat the first part."

He repeated it.

"Again. More slowly."

Asgar repeated it a third time, separating the words the same way the registration woman had separated his year.

She had never asked him to repeat the same thing three times before.

Something shifted on the other end of the line.

"What does 'removed the contribution' mean? What exactly did they take out of the calculation?"

He explained as best he could. For several days they had watched what changed with him and what changed by itself. Then they built a second calculation with nothing tied to his body. Some lines disappeared, some connected differently. The overall course remained.

"Mass and position—both?"

"Position was measured. Mass was calculated."

"Those are different things."

"I know. That's exactly what she said."

Mirena asked about repeated checks. Asgar listed the series he remembered and added the one about the bag: one had to be redone completely because the data had recorded the wrong position for it.

"Who found that?"

"She did. I wouldn't have."

The pause was longer than the previous ones. Only the steady background noise remained in the receiver.

"Now the part I need exactly," Mirena finally said. "Did that direction appear after they removed you? Or was it there before that too?"

Asgar opened his mouth and said nothing.

He could see the two diagrams side by side. Saw the lines disappearing. He had never once asked what had been in the first one before any of it. Before him.

"I don't know," he said.

"I can call her over."

"Not yet. This is enough for the engineers to understand what to look for."

"You didn't have a calculation like that?"

"We have a different model. I'm not going to pretend it's the same thing."

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the black housing.

"Fine. Then no calculations. What is returning, Mirena?"

The line went quiet.

"I'll pass on that exact question. I need to pull up the original project; I don't have it here right now."

"But you're supporting the return."

"Yes."

"And you don't know what you're returning?"

"I know what I'm supposed to do during the return. You're asking why the module was created in the first place. I don't know that right now, and I'm not going to invent an answer."

He wanted to say he had lived through enough guesses already. Instead he asked when to call back.

They agreed he would return after resting. Mirena did not promise the answer would be ready.

---

Back in the room, Asgar took off his jacket and hung it over the back of a chair. The bag lay by the bed. Tobin's wooden figurine stood on the table where he had left it that morning.

He went to get food and ate almost without noticing the taste. Then he lay down on top of the blanket.

The two models stayed in his head. He tried to remember which one had a section that bent more sharply. As if the shape of the bend could change the question.

Then he began inventing the answer.

It happened by itself. The engineers pull up an old project and find a date in it. Or not a date, but a way to set one. Mirena says: now we know where it's going, and we can change that. Asgar brought the conversation to that point and started over, each time a little differently, as though choosing the right words would make it more likely to come true.

Once he got as far as imagining how he would tell Katya.

He could not go farther. He did not know how old she would be in that conversation.

Asgar sat up, lowered his feet to the floor, and stayed like that for a while. The floor was warm. Someone walked by outside the wall on their own business, and the sound was completely ordinary.

He lay down again.

He could not sleep.

When he returned to the booth, people were moving through the passage. Two stopped at the marked detour, discussed something, and took another route. One carried a long flat object under his arm. Asgar watched them go. They had to take a few extra steps, and that was all the booth demanded of them.

He stepped inside again and lifted the receiver.

"Asgar," Mirena said. "We have an answer."

He leaned his shoulder against the wall.

"Tell me."

"The engineers pulled up the old project and compared it against the first failure. The module was sent empty. There was not supposed to be a person inside."

Asgar looked at the open doorway.

"But there's room to stand in here."

"Service space was left inside. Not passenger space."

He shifted one foot slightly. There was very little room between the toe of his boot and the opposite wall. Over all this time, the cramped space had become familiar. He knew how to turn his shoulder to pick up the receiver, where to put the bag, how to hold the door with his free hand.

"So I went into something I was never supposed to enter at all."

"The door was accessible. But the return calculation did not account for a person inside."

"Then why did it let the door close?"

"Because the system checks total mass only after the door closes. That's when it detected the excess. Before that, the failure had not yet been registered."

He remembered the first ring.

First the door. Then the telephone.

At the time it had seemed like an ordinary sequence: he went in, someone noticed him inside and spoke.

"So it rang because it detected extra weight."

Mirena did not answer immediately.

"The system raised an emergency. We opened the voice channel."

"With me."

"With the person who had ended up inside."

He lowered his head. The water he had brought from the riverbank had long since dried on the floor. A faint mark remained near the edge of his boot, and Asgar rubbed at it with his sole.

"Go on."

"After that, the direct return failed. The system went into emergency mode. Your 'windows' are moments when it manages to hold a stable state for a short time and make contact with the external environment."

"For me to get out."

"You can exit if the location is safe. But the window doesn't appear for the purpose of letting you out. It appears because the module is continuing to return."

He looked at the door again.

On August twentieth, he had come out. Stood outside. The booth had remained behind him. Back then, the fact that he was outside again had seemed like the main thing.

"That's why nothing ended then."

"Because your exit wasn't the completion of its task."

"And after that? All those stops..."

"It kept trying to return with you already inside."

Asgar tightened his grip on the receiver.

Part of him still wanted to challenge individual words, make Mirena replace them with more precise ones, demand another verification.

"It isn't returning you, Asgar," Mirena said. "It's returning itself."

He took the receiver away from his ear.

A faint, steady noise came from it. Asgar stared at the black holes along the lower edge until they blurred, then brought the receiver back.

Mirena was silent.

The glass was the same. The same screw near the lower edge of the telephone, the same metal worn pale on the door handle. Asgar knew every object in here and, until this minute, had thought he knew why he kept entering.

He pictured the house in Ar. The morning he had chosen not to meet there. Bertram walking toward the lower passage with another person; the shirt Tyra had taken from the bag still lying in the house.

He had left it because he had never worn it.

He remembered the fabric in her hands, his own nod, how easily he had agreed not to take anything unnecessary.

"The source," he said.

"Yes."

"The point it was sent from."

"Yes. The point the module was sent from."

"Not August."

"There is no reason to think it's August two thousand fourteen."

He waited. Mirena added nothing.

"And not my home."

"No. Your home isn't part of the module's task at all."

Asgar closed his eyes.

Before, the lack of a guarantee had left room for luck. The machine was returning—he was inside—if he held on, figured it out, reached the people who made it, maybe he could get home. He had heard the qualifications. Repeated them to others himself.

But all that time, one certainty had remained in the middle: the movement had something to do with his return.

Now he could not find that certainty in a single thing Mirena had said.

"How long have you known this?"

"The full task was confirmed after your question."

"Today?"

"While you were resting."

"And before the check?"

"We knew about the excess mass and the emergency return. That was enough for support. The full old project isn't kept in the working package. After your question, the engineers went into the archive and compared it with the first failure."

"Your engineers or the service?"

"This is the answer that was assembled for you now. I don't know which employees saw the old project and when. Before this check, I didn't have the full picture."

"Very convenient."

"What exactly?"

"Everyone knows their piece. One sees the mass, another supports the process, a third digs up the old layer. And I keep walking through door after door thinking they're returning me."

"I understand that for you this changes—"

"Don't say you understand."

She fell silent.

Someone passed on the far side of the passage beyond the open door. Asgar turned his back to the glass.

"You kept saying 'return.' From the beginning. And now it turns out the word was about a metal box."

"I used 'return' for the module because that's what we call this process. Every time you asked about your home, I told you we couldn't select the date."

"But you didn't tell me this either."

"I didn't know this."

"You could have found out."

The last words came out louder than he intended.

Mirena let the pause stand.

"Yes. We should have asked that earlier. I could have asked too. But I didn't know the answer, and I wasn't hiding it from you."

He remembered river water, the bag between his boots, her voice before the door closed.

"In Ar you let me choose."

"Yes."

"Stay or continue. With what I knew then."

"Yes. With what we knew then. I didn't promise you two thousand fourteen, but we didn't know this about the original task at the time either."

"I was standing in front of the door, Mirena. You could have said: wait, first let's find out who this whole thing is even working for."

"I could have. I didn't."

"But there was a safe chance to stop. And I left."

"You decided to continue. I can't pretend I made that decision for you. But I also won't pretend you had the whole picture then."

He straightened sharply.

"Of course. My decision. You've built a very good system: everything that happens to the machine is a process, and everything that happens to me is my choice."

"I didn't say that."

But he no longer wanted to sort out what she had said.

He wanted someone on the other end to stop answering evenly. Wanted at least some part of what had happened to be heavy there too.

"Your shift will end," he said. "You'll put down the receiver and leave. Someone else will sit in your place. You can even say it was a difficult conversation. And I won't get out of this. Out of this—I don't get out. I have to keep living in here while you rest."

The line went completely quiet.

Asgar felt the nail of his thumb digging into the plastic seam. He relaxed his hand but did not lower the receiver.

"Are you finished?" Mirena asked.

He did not answer.

"You can be angry with me and demand explanations. But my shift and my life outside this line have nothing to do with that."

"I was only—"

"No. Right now you're trying to hurt me. I'm not continuing that part of the conversation."

He stared at the door hinge.

"I'll answer about the module and about what we've confirmed," Mirena added. "If you still have questions."

Asgar ran a hand over his face and touched the pale scar above his eyebrow.

There were many questions. Why the windows worked the way they did. Whether the task could be changed. What they could do at the place it had been sent from. Who had allowed the door to remain accessible. Who had decided that simply supporting a person who had accidentally ended up inside was enough.

He could not ask a single one right now without returning to her shift.

"Give me the person who decides," Asgar said.

Mirena did not answer immediately.

"Wait."

He waited with the receiver pressed to his shoulder. Something shifted on the other end. Then the background changed: the faint steady noise Asgar had long ago stopped noticing disappeared, and he heard its absence.

"Asgar."

The voice was male, level, without pressure.

"My name is Korvin. I'm responsible for what is permissible on this line."

Asgar straightened against the wall.

"So the refusals are yours."

"Some of them."

"Then explain why I've been hearing the word 'return' all this time and not one person added that it wasn't about me at all."

"Because no one pulled the original project until your question." Korvin did not leave a pause for him to interrupt. "That isn't an excuse. It's the sequence."

"I don't care what you call it."

"I can see that."

Asgar waited for more. There was no more.

"Do you understand what happened to me?"

"We have unplanned living mass inside an unstable return," Korvin said. "So far that's the only description that doesn't promise more than we know."

Asgar pulled the receiver away from his ear and pressed it back.

"Mass."

"Yes."

"I'm a person."

"I know. That doesn't change what I'm responsible for."

Two people passed through the open doorway. Asgar turned toward the wall.

"Were you there when I got locked in?" he asked. "At the very beginning?"

"I was. I prohibited opening the door."

"I was in there."

"There was a boundary moving away. If the operator had opened the door at that moment, you would have stepped out into an incomplete state. What would have been left of you, I don't know. No one knows. That's why the door wasn't opened."

Asgar said nothing.

"You want me to say that decision was hard for me," Korvin continued. "It was. That changed nothing in the decision then and changes nothing now."

He made a short pause—the first in the conversation.

"Since you called me onto the line, I'll tell you one more thing. Everything you ask for beyond support costs someone. Not you."

"What does that mean?"

"It means Mirena has been paying for your latest requests. With her authorization. I've noted it twice."

Asgar looked at the open door.

"She didn't tell me."

"No."

"Why?"

"Ask her. I'm telling you this not for her sake. It's cheaper for me if you know the rules than if you break them through ignorance."

Asgar wanted to say something that would stay on the other end. He ran through two phrases and rejected both: the man on the line had already accounted for them, and they would not change the decision.

"I heard you," Asgar said.

"Good."

Something clicked in the receiver. The faint steady noise returned.

Asgar did not shift the receiver. He remembered Mirena saying, "We can wait a little," when he had not wanted to step out onto the surface. How she had asked whether anything was pulling him. How she had given the date and added nothing. He did not know which of those minutes had cost her anything.

"I'm here," Mirena said.

Asgar held the receiver and did not know where to begin.

She did not ask what they had discussed.

"Does the source know I'm here?" he finally asked.

"I don't know. We don't have a direct channel to the source. The service knows there's a person inside, but we can't send a message there right now."

"So there is no solution."

"No. Not right now."

Asgar looked for a long time at the street—at what he still called a street even though beyond the glass was a closed passage in a structure around a planet.

"I'll call again."

"All right."

He put the receiver back on the cradle. It landed crooked the first time; he adjusted it and kept his hand on it for several seconds.

---

The worktable was not far. Asgar knew the route by heart: out of the cordoned section, two turns, the inner passage where the blackness was hidden.

He walked more slowly than usual.

Three people came toward him. One carried a rolled-up covering over one shoulder; two were talking and turned into a side passage in the middle of a sentence. A person stood by the wall looking at a device, shifting from foot to foot. All of it was happening today exactly as it had yesterday, and tomorrow it would happen again.

Asgar had to say aloud what he had heard. He tried it in his head. The words formed one short sentence, and the sentence came out wrong: it sounded as though he had been mistaken all this time, not that no one had told him.

At the passage he stopped briefly by the glass. The ring curved upward. It was still hard to look at, but today that was easier than continuing on.

Then he kept walking.

Tessa was at the worktable. She had already removed one of the models; the second was reduced and pushed to the side. In front of her were records Asgar had not seen before.

She noticed him, finished what she was doing, and turned.

"They answered?"

He nodded.

"Can you tell me exactly what they confirmed?"

Asgar remained standing.

"It was sent empty. The space inside was for servicing. People were never intended to be in there."

Tessa looked at the model, then back at him.

He told her about the mass check after the door closed, the failed direct return, and the stable intermediate states. As he retold it, speaking became easier. The facts settled into a sequence. There were ordinary words for them.

"They told you this after checking the original documentation?" Tessa asked.

"Yes. Mirena said the engineers pulled up the old project layer and compared it with the first event."

Tessa made a note.

"Then things fit better. Our calculation on its own couldn't tell us why the module is returning. The project can."

Asgar watched her hands.

"You're not surprised?"

"I am. I had several possibilities in mind, and this was one of them. Now it has evidence behind it. I still need to check what it changes in our calculations."

He wanted to ask whether she understood what that meant for him. Almost in the same words he had just used against Mirena.

Tessa cleared the edge of the table.

"Do you want to sit?"

"No."

She did not insist.

"That's enough for today. I can run the next calculation without you."

He nodded and headed for the exit. At the door he stopped.

"Show me both again."

Tessa brought the two images back side by side.

Now he looked at them differently. Yesterday, one model had been with him and one without him. Today, both belonged to the machine.

"That's enough," Asgar said.

---

The room was exactly as he had left it.

His jacket hung on the chair. The bag lay by the bed. He took out the bundle with the phone, unwound the cloth, and pressed the button even though he already knew nothing would happen.

The dark glass reflected part of his face.

He turned the phone sideways. The pale scar stood out more clearly. Asgar stopped looking and placed the device on the table beside the wooden figurine.

He needed to eat. Or wash. He could send Tessa the question he had failed to formulate. He could go back to the booth and tell Mirena he should not have said what he did about her shift.

Asgar returned several times to the thought of going back to the booth and telling Mirena he had been wrong about her shift, and each time put it off. The argument kept going in his head without Mirena, and in that argument she answered a little worse every time than she really had.

Asgar sat on the edge of the bed.

People passed outside the wall. A door closed with a short sound. Someone had returned to the neighboring room.

He remembered telling Tyra he wanted to reach the source. Back then, the word had carried everything he did not know. He could know neither the date nor the place nor how the world worked and still say it as though it were a direction toward Katya.

Now the source had a different purpose.

Asgar picked the bag up from the floor and set it on the bed. He opened it and took out the top change of clothes. Beneath them was the T-shirt he had brought from home. He pulled out the edge but did not take it any farther.

For a while he sat there holding the fabric.

Then he carefully put it back.

Until that day he had been waiting for the journey to end. Now, for the first time, he did not understand what the end of the journey would mean for him.
