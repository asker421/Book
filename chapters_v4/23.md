Chapter 23. After What Was Said

In the morning, Asgar zipped the bag even though he was not planning to leave yet.

The phone and the wooden figurine remained on the table. He looked at them, unzipped the bag again, and sat down to breakfast. The food from the night before had not disappeared anywhere; he had to remove yesterday's portion and order another. The system asked whether to save his previous choice. He agreed just to make it stop asking questions.

Tessa's message arrived while he was finishing.

She could meet now or later, after her work. Asgar chose now. It was hard to stay in the room: everything he needed was within reach, no one disturbed him, and he could not find anything in that calm to hold on to.

He washed, put on his jacket, and took the local translator. He left the bag by the bed.

Tessa was waiting in the room where they had worked the day before. There were no images above the table. She invited him to sit and spent some time finding the record she wanted to show him. Asgar watched her hands, already preparing himself for one more clarification that would make yesterday's answer worse.

"We can continue working on separation," she said.

He looked up.

"Here?"

"Yes. The measurements give us something to work with. We're a long way from a finished separation procedure: first we have to understand what happens when we remove your contribution little by little. But this is no longer a 'maybe someday' conversation. I'm prepared to work on it seriously."

"How far away?"

"I don't know. Definitely not a few tests. It may take a long time."

"And the booth will stay here the whole time?"

"We don't have its schedule. We'll work while it's available and wait for the next states if we have to. I won't promise a quick result. But staying here is a real possibility. Otherwise I wouldn't suggest that you build a life around it."

Asgar ran a hand along the edge of the table.

"Will I have to stay beside it all the time?"

"No. You'll come in for measurements. The rest of the time—you live. They'll help with a room; if this one has to be cleared, they'll find another."

She said it matter-of-factly, like moving a workplace. He looked at the empty space above the table where the two models had moved yesterday.

"You already checked?"

"This morning. Before I called you."

Tessa opened the record. This time it contained a list of required observations and several blank fields. She explained which data could be obtained without him present and which would require repeat meetings. Asgar listened and gradually began to see the length of the life being offered to him: not today's decision at a door, but a schedule he could get used to. Come in, return to the room, one day stop thinking of it as temporary.

"And you still can't send me back."

"To your own past—no."

"Even though you can change bodies."

"Those are different tasks."

He nodded. Ilar had already explained enough for him not to argue as if refusal meant a lack of effort.

"If I stay, what happens to the body? Mine," Asgar clarified. "Will they suggest replacing it?"

"You'll be offered examinations and options for assistance. You do not need a replacement body for ordinary life here."

Tessa moved the research record aside.

"If you decide to continue the journey, it's worth checking the condition of your body too. You've already spent several months in it."

Asgar wanted to correct her: not several, almost... He had to calculate. The days before the first major transition. Nineteen days with Martin. The road with Damir. Ar.

One hundred and three days in Ar.

"About five months," Tessa said, checking the sequence they had assembled. "By your elapsed time. Approximately."

He looked at his hands.

Five months fit into a familiar calendar. In that much time back home, projects ran their course, vacation plans changed, children grew out of clothes. Here he had crossed tens of thousands of years and still carried a T-shirt that could be washed and worn.

"All right," he said. "We can check."

---

The examination took place in a neighboring sector. Asgar asked several times whether anything was going to be left inside his body, and on the third time the woman conducting the examination stopped.

"Right now we're only measuring," she said. "Any intervention would be a separate decision."

He apologized and let her finish.

They found no urgent need for deep intervention. For long-term health maintenance, Asgar asked her to separate what required permanently operating internal devices from what would be completed before he left. The woman rearranged the list and added that there was no medical need to choose anything today.

That suited him. He took the results for Tessa and spent a long time looking at his own measurements translated into familiar units. Some of the values meant nothing to him. Weight did.

He remembered Mirena's words the day before about the load and, for the first time, thought of it as a quantity that could not be measured once and treated as constant. He ate, drank, sweated, lost weight; took off his jacket, put it on again, carried water. Over months, neither his body nor the load could have stayed the same.

When he returned to Tessa, he asked about it before she had time to open the results.

"Do I need to maintain exactly the same weight now?"

"You won't be able to, and you don't need to. We'll just account for your current weight each time."

"So if I lose weight..."

"Your contribution changes. Let the service calculate how important that is for the next window. Report changes. And don't try to restore the number with objects."

Asgar remembered Bertram's tool in the inner pocket of the bag.

"I wasn't going to."

Tessa looked at him. He held her gaze, then added:

"I just thought of it."

She nodded and went back to the results.

---

He came to the booth without the bag.

He stood inside by the open door, lifted the receiver, and waited for an answer. When he heard Mirena, he suddenly realized he had not prepared even the first sentence. Everything he had rehearsed on the way began with her words from yesterday.

"Tessa is offering to work on separation," he said.

Mirena asked what exactly had been confirmed. He relayed Tessa's assessment, separately making clear that there was no finished procedure yet. He told her about the examination and asked how to report changes in weight.

She answered in detail. Questions about the body were easier than the rest: he could name a measurement, a source, a time, and admit what he did not know.

Then the conversation stopped.

Asgar looked at the empty passage beyond the door. He could put down the receiver, go back to Tessa, and discuss the research. Here they took him seriously. Staying did not require him to prove that he deserved help.

"I want to understand what happens if I continue," he said.

"What exactly?"

"The source. I kept picturing a place where the people who made it are sitting. A laboratory. They look at what broke and tell me how to fix it."

Mirena was silent for a moment.

"We don't have confirmation of that picture. The point of origin may not be a laboratory and may not even be a place in the usual sense. It may be a system state."

"But there has to be a system that sent it."

"Yes. The return is tied to the infrastructure from which the module was sent. I don't know what form that infrastructure has survived in, or whether there are people there."

"The creators may already be gone."

"Yes."

He pressed his shoulder against the wall. Yesterday that possibility would have felt like one more injustice being hidden from him. Now he said it himself and waited to see what changed after the answer.

Nothing changed. He only had to let go of the people in the laboratory—the strangers he had spent all this time imagining as waiting for him.

"And you're not from there?"

"No. We handle the emergency from this side. We don't have a direct channel to the origin point."

"I see."

He said it too quickly. After a pause, he returned to the question.

"No, I don't understand everything. But I heard you."

Mirena did not fill the silence.

"How much longer?" Asgar asked. "To the source."

"There is no exact duration. By our calculations, more lies ahead than has already passed."

He moved away from the wall.

"Longer by dates or for me?"

"By calendar, it makes no sense to count this as your life at all. But you may have to wait at stable stops. So assume we're talking about years of your time."

People moved through the passage. Asgar watched them without making out faces.

"Years."

"Yes."

"One? Ten?"

"I don't know. And I don't want to give you a range you'll later treat as a promise."

He exhaled slowly through his nose.

Five months could still be called an absence, even a terrible, impossible one. Years required another life. There would have to be habits, new things, illnesses, people he did not yet know. He tried to picture himself at the end and saw only today's jacket, as though everything else could somehow be preserved.

"Will you be on the line?"

"I can't promise that. Over years, there may already be someone else on the line."

He lowered his gaze to the cord.

"You'll be replaced?"

"Maybe. I live between these conversations too. I work, rest, may change jobs—I don't know. For me, your next window doesn't arrive instantly either."

It was exactly that life of hers he had used against her yesterday. Being able to leave a shift, put down someone else's voice, sleep. Everything he considered necessary in his own life had suddenly become evidence of indifference in hers.

"About yesterday," he said. "After an answer like that... You understand, I left Ar, and then found out this whole thing wasn't even..."

He stopped.

The sentence continued on its own: anyone would lose control in that state. He only had to explain it thoroughly enough for Mirena to agree.

Asgar ran his free hand over the back of his head and stopped himself.

"No. It doesn't matter why."

She did not interrupt.

"You didn't know. You told me you didn't know, and I kept talking as if you had known and hidden it. Then I went after your life. Your shift. I wanted you to feel bad too."

The last words were harder. He looked toward the passage, though no one there could help him finish.

"I'm sorry."

Mirena answered after a pause.

"I heard you."

He waited for something more. Familiar softening in her voice, permission to stop being ashamed. He had already opened his mouth to ask whether they could talk as before now, then closed it.

"But the question of why the project wasn't pulled up earlier is still there. It still has to be addressed."

"Yes. But that's a different question."

"Yes."

They were silent for a while.

"Korvin said you've been paying for my recent requests," Asgar said.

"He did."

"Is that true?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because then you would have started choosing what to ask me. People in that position choose badly."

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the receiver.

"And now?"

"Now you know. Ask what you need. I'll do the counting."

Asgar adjusted the receiver against his ear.

"Mirena."

"Yes?"

"Where are you sitting right now?"

He prepared for her not to answer.

"At the second console from the passage. There's a window on the left; it looks into an inner courtyard where nothing is happening. Someone else's cup is on the sill. They forgot it a week ago, and every day I think about taking it away."

Asgar laughed—briefly, unexpectedly.

"Then take it away."

"Then I'll stop thinking about it. During a shift, it's useful for me to think about something besides the door."

He waited to see whether she would add anything else. She did not.

"I haven't decided yet," he said. "I need to think."

"All right."

He put down the receiver and stepped out.

---

On the way back to the room, Asgar reached the broad glazing and stopped by the wall.

The planet filled an enormous part of the view. A band of clouds covered the edge of a continent; above, beyond the blue curve, blackness began. He made himself look a little longer than usual. The floor stayed beneath his feet. People passing by did not cling to the wall.

He could learn to live here.

He had already learned to order food and find his way. He could get used to the sky being off to the side, figure out the translation, occupy himself with work. Separation would take time, but there was time in both options now.

Asgar returned to the room and took off his jacket. He sat at the table and placed the phone and the figurine beside him. He turned the wooden object flat-side down several times until it stood securely.

He tried to understand what he wanted after yesterday's answer.

He still wanted home. The new knowledge had not made that desire smaller or given it another name. He wanted to hear Katya without someone else's device between them. See the children. The apartment door opened in his memory: Katya barefoot in the hallway, him with the key he could not get into the lock. He remembered his own "we were just sitting there talking" word for word. He waited for her answer but could only remember how, in the morning, he had started justifying himself again.

But neither Tessa nor Mirena had a way to bring him there.

He could stay and live with that.

He could go to the origin system. For an unknown amount of time. Find infrastructure there that might be unable to do anything for him except confirm that it was impossible.

Asgar lay down, then sat up again. Spend years, reach the end, and hear no. He still could not honestly say he was ready for that. He could only refuse to remove that answer from the calculation.

Toward evening he asked Tessa to meet.

She came after work, with the same translator and the research record. Asgar cleared the chair for her.

"I want to continue," he said.

Tessa set the record on the table.

"What changed?"

He told her about the conversation with Mirena: the origin infrastructure, the possible duration, the possibility that the people who had built the machine might no longer exist. This time he did not shorten the unpleasant parts.

"Here you can study the connection," he finished. "But you can't answer whether there is a way back for me. I want to reach the thing this all started from."

"There may not be an available answer there either."

"I know."

Tessa looked at him, and Asgar corrected himself.

"I've been told. I still don't know how to think about it calmly."

She lowered her eyes to the record.

"You don't have to calm down first in order to decide."

He gave a slight smile, but she was already opening the results.

"Then I'll assemble a final package for you: separately, what we measured; separately, where the assumptions begin. You can pass that to Mirena."

Asgar looked at the research record she had brought.

"You prepared this in the morning."

"Yes."

"And you asked people?"

"Two. One of them was going to free up part of their time for this."

Tessa said it without pressure and did not wait for an answer.

"I'll let him know today. Here it happens more often than the opposite."

Asgar nodded and for a while could not think of the next question.

They went through the record together. Asgar hurried to say the connection could be closed here.

"You mean we can begin testing separation?" Tessa asked.

He corrected himself.

"Yes. There is no finished procedure. I said that wrong."

Tessa left that point in front of him until he finished the retelling.

---

Before leaving, he wrapped the phone in cloth. He put the wooden figurine into the bag beside the clothes, making sure it was not pressing against Bertram's tool. The T-shirt from home lay lower down. Asgar did not take it out.

At the booth, Tessa waited while he relayed the results to Mirena. To clarify one point, he asked Mirena to wait, hung up, stepped out, and asked Tessa. Then he went back inside and corrected his own retelling.

Mirena accepted the clarification.

Asgar stepped out again, took the bag, and returned the local translator. Tessa put it away. Without the device, the final words of farewell were incomprehensible, but she held out a hand, and he shook it.

Inside, he set the bag between his boots. The door remained open. He lifted the receiver, confirmed to Mirena that the bag was now with him, and only then did they finish checking the conditions for continuing.

"I've decided to continue," Asgar said into the receiver.

"Before you close the door: we still do not have a way to return you home. That hasn't changed."

"Yes. And there may not be one at the source either."

He looked at Tessa. She stood beyond the clear area, holding the returned translator.

"I still want to reach it," he added. "If a physical return is impossible, I want to learn that where there is a basis for the answer."

Mirena confirmed readiness to support the transition. Her voice remained restrained.

Asgar held the bag in place with his foot and took hold of the door. For a second he paused, looking down the passage he could still use to return to the room.

Then he pulled the heavy door toward himself.

The lock closed. He kept his hand on the handle and waited as the familiar room beyond the glass began to lose depth. Tessa raised a hand. Asgar managed to answer with a nod.

When the passage disappeared, he brought the receiver closer.

"I'm here," he said.
