Chapter 23. After What Was Said

In the morning, Asgar zipped the bag even though he was not leaving yet.

The phone and the wooden piece stayed on the table.

He looked at them, opened the bag again, and sat down to eat.

The food had not vanished overnight. He had to clear yesterday’s portion and order another. The system asked whether it should remember the previous selection.

He agreed just to make it stop asking questions.

Tessa’s message arrived while he was finishing.

She could meet now or later, after her work.

Asgar chose now.

It was difficult to remain in the room.

Everything he needed was within reach.

No one disturbed him.

He could not find anything solid in that calm.

He washed, put on the jacket, and took the local translator.

The bag remained beside the bed.

Tessa was waiting in the room where they had worked the previous day.

No images were open above the table.

She offered him a chair and spent a while locating the record she wanted to show him.

Asgar watched her hands, already bracing for another clarification that would somehow make yesterday’s answer worse.

“We can continue working on separation,” she said.

He looked up.

“Here?”

“Yes.

“The measurements give us something to work with.

“A finished separation procedure is still far away. First we need to understand what happens as we remove your contribution in small steps.

“But this is no longer a conversation about ‘maybe someday.’

“I’m prepared to work on it seriously.”

“How far away?”

“I don’t know.

“Definitely more than a few trials.

“It could take a long time.”

“And the booth stays here the whole time?”

“We don’t have its schedule.

“We work while it’s accessible and wait for the next stable state if we have to.

“I won’t promise a quick result.

“But the possibility of staying here is real.

“Otherwise I wouldn’t suggest building a life around it.”

Asgar ran a palm along the edge of the table.

“Would I have to sit beside it all the time?”

“No.

“You come for measurements.

“The rest of the time, you live.

“They’ll help with accommodation.

“If this room has to be freed, they’ll find you another.”

She said it matter-of-factly, the way someone might talk about moving a desk.

Asgar looked at the empty space above the table where the two models had moved the day before.

“You already checked that?”

“This morning.

“Before I called you.”

Tessa opened a record.

This one contained a list of required observations and several empty fields.

She explained which data could be collected without him and which would require repeated sessions.

Asgar listened, and gradually he began to see the length of the life she was offering:

not a decision at one door today.

A schedule.

Something he could get used to.

Come in.

Go back to the room.

One day stop calling it temporary.

“And you still can’t send me backward.”

“Into your own past—no.”

“Even though you can change bodies.”

“Those are different problems.”

He nodded.

Ilar had explained enough for him not to argue as though the inability meant a lack of effort.

“If I stay, what happens to my body?

“Mine,” Asgar clarified.

“Would they offer to replace it?”

“You’d be offered an examination and options for care.

“You do not need another body for ordinary life here.”

Tessa moved the research record aside.

“If you decide to continue, it would still be worth checking your physical condition.

“You’ve already spent several months in that body.”

Asgar almost corrected her:

not several, almost—

He had to count.

The days before the first major transition.

Nineteen days with Martin.

The journey with Damir.

Ar.

One hundred and three days in Ar.

“About five months,” Tessa said after checking the sequence they had built.

“By your elapsed time.

“Approximately.”

Asgar looked at his hands.

Five months fit inside a familiar calendar.

At home, projects could begin and end in five months.

Vacation plans changed.

Children outgrew clothes.

Here he had crossed tens of thousands of years and was still carrying a T-shirt that could be washed and worn again.

“All right,” he said.

“An examination is fine.”

---

The examination took place in a neighboring sector.

Asgar asked several times whether anything would be left inside his body.

On the third repetition, the woman conducting the examination stopped.

“Right now we are only measuring,” she said.

“Any intervention would be a separate decision.”

He apologized and let her finish.

They found no urgent reason for deep intervention.

When long-term health support came up, Asgar asked her to separate options that required permanently operating internal devices from treatments that would be complete before he left.

She rearranged the list and added that there was no medical reason to choose anything today.

That was enough for him.

He took the results back for Tessa and spent a long time looking at the values converted into units he recognized.

Some meant nothing to him.

His weight did.

He remembered Mirena’s words about the load and for the first time thought of it as something that could never be measured once and assumed constant.

He ate.

Drank.

Sweated.

Lost weight.

Took off the jacket, put it back on, carried water.

Over months, neither his body nor the load could possibly stay the same.

When he returned to Tessa, he asked before she had even opened the results.

“Do I need to keep the same weight now?”

“You can’t.

“And you don’t need to.

“We just account for your current weight each time.”

“So if I lose weight…”

“Your contribution changes.

“How much that matters for the next window is for the service to calculate.

“Report changes.

“And do not try to restore a number by adding objects.”

Asgar remembered Bertram’s tool inside the bag.

“I wasn’t planning to.”

Tessa looked at him.

He held her gaze, then added:

“I just thought of it.”

She nodded and returned to the results.

---

He went to the booth without the bag.

Stood inside with the door open, lifted the receiver, and waited.

When Mirena answered, he realized he had not prepared even the first sentence.

Everything he had rehearsed on the way over had begun with her words from the day before.

“Tessa is offering to work on separation,” he said.

Mirena asked what had actually been confirmed.

He repeated Tessa’s assessment and made a point of saying there was no finished procedure yet.

He told her about the examination and asked how to report weight changes.

Mirena answered in detail.

Questions about the body were easier than the others.

There were measurements.

Sources.

Times.

Things that could be named.

Things she could admit she did not know.

Then the conversation stopped.

Asgar looked into the empty passage outside the door.

He could hang up.

Go back to Tessa.

Discuss the research.

Here, people took him seriously.

To stay, he did not have to prove that he deserved help.

“I want to understand what happens if I continue,” he said.

“What exactly?”

“The source.

“I kept imagining a place with people who built it.

“A laboratory.

“They look at what broke and tell me how to fix it.”

Mirena paused.

“We have no confirmation of that picture.

“The source may not be a laboratory.

“It may not even be a place in the ordinary sense.

“It may be a system state.”

“But the system that sent it has to be there.”

“Yes.

“The return is tied to the infrastructure from which the module was dispatched.

“I don’t know what form that infrastructure is in now.

“I don’t know whether there are people there.”

“The creators may be gone.”

“Yes.”

He pressed his shoulder against the wall.

Yesterday, that possibility would have felt like one more unfair thing no one had told him.

Now he had said it himself and waited to see what changed after the answer.

Nothing.

He only had to give up the people in the laboratory—

strangers he had been imagining all this time, waiting for him.

“You aren’t at the source yourselves?”

“No.

“We’re managing the emergency from this side.

“We have no direct channel to the source.”

“Understood.”

He said it too quickly.

Then stopped and corrected himself.

“No.

“Not everything.

“But I heard you.”

Mirena did not fill the silence.

“How much farther?” Asgar asked.

“To the source.”

“There is no exact duration.

“According to our calculations, more remains ahead than has already passed.”

He moved away from the wall.

“Longer in calendar time or for me?”

“In calendar terms, it’s meaningless to measure it as your life.

“But at stable stops, you may wait.

“So assume it could take years of your own time.”

People moved through the corridor.

Asgar watched them without seeing faces.

“Years.”

“Yes.”

“One? Ten?”

“I don’t know.

“And I’m not going to give you a range that you’ll later hear as a promise.”

He breathed out slowly through his nose.

Five months could still be called an absence, even a monstrous impossible one.

Years demanded another life.

Habits.

New possessions.

Illnesses.

People he did not know yet.

He tried to imagine himself at the end and saw only the jacket he was wearing now, as though everything else could somehow remain unchanged.

“Will you still be on the line?”

“I won’t promise that.

“Over years, someone else may be handling it.”

“You’ll be replaced?”

“Maybe.

“I live between these conversations too.

“I work.

“I rest.

“I may change jobs.

“I don’t know.

“Your next window is not instantaneous for me either.”

That was the life he had used against her yesterday.

The ability to finish a shift.

Put down someone else’s voice.

Sleep.

Everything he considered necessary in his own life had suddenly become evidence of indifference when it belonged to her.

“About yesterday,” he said.

“After that answer, I…

“You understand, I left Ar and then found out all of this was never even…”

He stopped.

The sentence offered its own continuation:

anyone would have snapped.

All he had to do was explain enough for Mirena to agree.

Asgar rubbed the back of his head with his free hand and cut himself off.

“No.

“Doesn’t matter why.”

She did not interrupt.

“You didn’t know.

“You told me you didn’t know.

“And I kept talking as if you did and hid it from me.

“Then I went after your life.

“Your shift.

“I wanted you to feel bad too.”

The last sentence was harder.

Asgar looked toward the passage, though no one there could help him finish it.

“I’m sorry.”

Mirena answered after a pause.

“I heard you.”

He waited for more.

Some familiar softening in her voice.

Permission to stop being ashamed.

He almost asked whether they could talk the way they had before.

Then closed his mouth.

“But the question of why the project wasn’t retrieved earlier still remains,” she said.

“It still needs to be examined.”

“Yes.

“But that is a different question.”

“Yes.”

They were quiet.

“Corwin said you paid for some of my recent requests,” Asgar said.

“He told you.”

“Is it true?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you say anything?”

“Because then you would have started choosing what you were allowed to ask me.

“People make bad choices in your position when they start doing that.”

Asgar ran a thumb along the edge of the receiver.

“And now?”

“Now you know.

“Ask what you need.

“I’ll do the counting.”

He adjusted the receiver against his ear.

“Mirena.”

“Yes?”

“Where are you sitting right now?”

He expected her not to answer.

“Second console from the passage.

“There’s a window on my left.

“It faces an inner courtyard.

“Nothing is happening there.

“Someone’s cup is on the sill.

“They forgot it a week ago, and every day I plan to take it away.”

Asgar laughed.

Short.

Unexpectedly.

“Then take it away.”

“If I do, I’ll stop thinking about it.

“And during a shift it’s useful to think about something besides the door.”

He waited to see whether she would add anything.

She did not.

“I still haven’t decided,” he said.

“I need to think.”

“All right.”

He replaced the receiver and stepped outside.

---

On the way back to his room, Asgar reached the broad glazing and stopped beside the wall.

The planet filled an enormous part of his view.

A band of cloud covered the edge of a continent.

Beyond the blue curve began blackness.

He forced himself to look a little longer than usual.

The floor stayed under his feet.

The people passing by did not hold the wall.

A person could learn to live here.

He had already learned how to order food.

Find his way.

He could get used to the sideways sky.

Learn the translation system.

Find work.

Separation would take time, but now both choices contained time.

Asgar returned to the room and removed the jacket.

Sat at the table.

Placed the phone and the wooden piece beside each other.

He turned the wooden piece several times until the flat side rested steadily on the surface.

He tried to understand what he wanted after yesterday’s answer.

He still wanted home.

The new knowledge had not reduced that desire or renamed it.

He wanted to hear Katya without someone else’s device between them.

See the children.

The apartment door opened in memory:

Katya barefoot in the hall.

Asgar with the key he could not fit into the lock.

He remembered his own words exactly:

“We were just sitting there talking.”

He waited for her answer.

Could only remember himself starting to justify it again the next morning.

But neither Tessa nor Mirena possessed a way to bring him there.

He could stay and live with that.

Or he could continue toward the source system.

For an unknown number of years.

Reach infrastructure that might do nothing for him except confirm that what he wanted was impossible.

Asgar lay down.

Then sat up again.

Spend years.

Reach the end.

Hear no.

He could not honestly say yet that he was ready for that.

He could only stop excluding that answer from the calculation.

Near evening, he asked Tessa to meet.

She came after work with the same translator and the research record.

Asgar cleared the chair for her.

“I want to continue,” he said.

Tessa put the record on the table.

“What changed?”

He told her about the conversation with Mirena:

the source infrastructure.

the possible duration.

the possibility that no people who had built the machine remained.

This time he did not shorten the unpleasant parts.

“You can study the connection here,” he finished.

“But you can’t answer whether a route back exists for me.

“I want to reach the thing this all started from.”

“There still may be no usable answer there.”

“I know.”

Tessa looked at him.

Asgar corrected himself.

“I was told.

“I still don’t know how to think about it calmly.”

She lowered her eyes to the record.

“You don’t have to be calm before you decide.”

He gave a small smile, but she was already opening the results.

“Then I’ll prepare a final package for you.

“Separately: what we measured.

“Separately: where the assumptions begin.

“You can give that to Mirena.”

Asgar looked at the research record she had brought.

“You were preparing all this this morning.”

“Yes.”

“And you asked people?”

“Two.

“One of them was going to reserve part of his time for it.”

Tessa said it without pressure and did not wait for a response.

“I’ll tell him today.

“It happens more often this way than the other way around.”

Asgar nodded.

For a while he could not think of another question.

They reviewed the record together.

Asgar rushed and said the connection could be closed here.

“You mean we can begin testing separation?” Tessa asked.

He corrected himself.

“Yes.

“There is no finished procedure.

“I said that wrong.”

Tessa left the point in front of him until he finished the explanation correctly.

---

Before leaving, he wrapped the phone in cloth.

He put the wooden piece into the bag beside the clothes, making sure it did not press against Bertram’s tool.

The T-shirt from home lay lower down.

Asgar did not take it out.

At the booth, Tessa waited while he relayed the results to Mirena.

At one point he asked Mirena to wait, hung up, stepped outside, and asked Tessa a question.

Then he went back in and corrected his own summary.

Mirena accepted the correction.

Asgar stepped out once more, picked up the bag, and returned the local translator.

Tessa put it away.

Without the device, the final words of farewell were incomprehensible.

But she held out her hand.

Asgar shook it.

Inside the booth, he set the bag between his boots.

The door remained open.

He lifted the receiver and told Mirena the bag was with him now.

Only then did they complete the checks for continuing.

“I’ve decided to go on,” Asgar said.

“Before you close the door:

“We still have no method to return you home.

“That has not changed.”

“Yes.

“And there may not be one at the source.”

He looked at Tessa.

She stood beyond the clear area holding the returned translator.

“I still want to reach it,” he added.

“If physical return is impossible, I want to learn that where there is enough basis to answer.”

Mirena confirmed that support was ready.

Her voice remained restrained.

Asgar held the bag in place with one foot and took the door handle.

For a second he hesitated, looking into the passage that still led back to his room.

Then he pulled the heavy panel toward himself.

The lock closed.

He kept his palm on the handle and waited as the familiar room beyond the glass began losing depth.

Tessa raised one hand.

Asgar managed to answer with a nod.

When the passage disappeared, he brought the receiver closer.

“I’m here,” he said.
