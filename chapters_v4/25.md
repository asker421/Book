Chapter 25. Through So Many Hands

Olmer set a rod and a small piece of metal in front of him.

Asgar picked them up and looked at the dry fibers in the shallow bowl.

Yesterday it had looked easier.

Olmer had drawn the metal across the rod, sparks had fallen, and an orange point had appeared among the fibers.

Now the metal scraped unpleasantly against the rod, and the sparks died before they reached the bowl.

“Closer,” Olmer said.

Asgar leaned in.

“Hands closer.

“Your head can stay where it is.”

He moved the bowl.

On the third attempt, several sparks landed in the center.

The fibers darkened.

Nothing happened.

Olmer sat opposite him repairing a fastening on his field bag.

He did not take the tools away every time Asgar failed.

Did not demonstrate the whole thing again.

Sometimes he looked up, said one or two words, and returned to the fastening.

When smoke finally appeared, Asgar blew too quickly.

The fibers scattered.

Olmer put the bag on his knees.

“Now gather it again.

“That’s why we’re doing this here.”

There was no wind beneath the canopy.

Water stood nearby.

Food waited inside the building.

Asgar gathered the scattered material, pushing the fibers into the bowl with movements that were too sharp.

Over the past months he had learned things whose ordinary names he did not even know.

A small fire would not start.

On the next attempt, it did.

He held the bowl with both hands and watched the flame slowly catch the thin pieces of prepared wood.

Olmer waited until it was stable, then showed him how to put it out and check the remains.

“Again tomorrow,” he said.

“I understood.”

“We’ll see tomorrow.”

Asgar set the bowl aside.

“Why are you doing this?

“You have routes to cover.”

Olmer tightened the repaired fastening and tugged it.

“Yesterday you said you’re going into that thing again.

“And the next place may have no node.”

“I did.”

“Half your story I can’t verify.

“This I can.”

He nodded toward the bowl of fibers.

“Every year someone goes into the field assuming there’ll be a node.

“Then people search from the last place they were seen.”

Olmer set the bag aside.

“This costs me a few evenings.

“For you it may be the first twenty-four hours.”

---

They went to the archive several days later, after Olmer finished his rounds and handed over the samples he had collected.

Until then Asgar helped around the node, learned how to use the filter, and practiced tying a knot that would not lock solid after bearing weight.

In the evenings they returned to the search requests.

The booth remained beside the path.

Asgar checked the telephone regularly.

Before leaving for the archive, he told Mirena where he was going and when he expected to return.

She confirmed that the current state remained stable.

She did not promise how long it would last.

Olmer waited by the transport with two cases.

“Those for the archive?” Asgar asked.

“Neighboring sector.

“I have work there too.

“You work, I drop off the material, and then we see who ends up waiting for whom.”

On the way, Asgar watched the forest.

Bare rock opened between stands of trees, then disappeared beneath green again.

After the trip to the old location of the city, he had stopped searching for a familiar coastline.

Still, sometimes he caught himself looking for roads where no road could possibly exist.

From outside, the archive repository barely differed from the other low buildings.

Inside, the air was cooler.

Olmer left him at a work station, helped connect the node translator to the local system, then walked away carrying the cases.

The first thing the archive offered was an overview.

A red rectangle appeared before the text.

Asgar recognized the proportions immediately even though the image was schematic:

tall narrow booth.

divided windows.

door in front.

A chain of events ran beneath it.

Asgar leaned closer, then stopped himself.

“Where did that image come from?”

“Overview reconstruction.

“Prepared for an educational summary.”

The voice had none of the delays he had grown used to at the outdoor node.

Asgar moved the reconstruction aside.

“Then let’s agree on something now.

“Tell me what’s confirmed and what somebody reconstructed or embellished later.

“If something isn’t there, say it isn’t there.

“I don’t need a good story.”

“Accepted.

“Source status will be identified in each answer.”

Martin would have needed a week of messages and two arguments to get a system to behave that way.

“What is known about the origin of an object with this form?”

“No confirmed information about the origin exists.

“There is a sequence of recorded appearances beginning in the twenty-first century.

“There is also a separate chain of later images derived from one another.”

“Remove the later ones.

“Keep what existed independently.”

The list visibly shrank.

When Olmer returned, Asgar was still working through what remained.

“Find anything?”

“I’m still figuring out what they mean when they say they ‘found’ something.”

Olmer sat beside him and pulled the translation toward himself.

“Reasonable.

“You’re still going to have to eat.”

---

“Earliest reliable record.”

The archive gave the year 2014.

Asgar asked it to show what had been rejected before that year:

disappearances.

strange structures.

people seen in the wrong time.

Some collapsed as soon as their basis was opened.

Others had been connected to the red booth only by later authors.

“Remove every supposed match.”

Only August remained.

August 16, 2014 appeared large in the familiar notation.

Asgar placed both palms on the table.

That number had a morning.

He remembered getting ready.

Talking to Katya.

What he had planned to do after the stadium.

The archive began the anomaly’s history on a day that had not yet had anything unusual in it for him.

“Nothing reliable before that?”

“No confirmed material concerning this object predates the specified date.

“This is an absence of data, not a statement that no earlier events occurred.”

Asgar read the answer to the end and did not broaden the search backward again.

Olmer looked at him, then at the date.

“Your day?”

“Mine.”

He asked nothing else.

What followed were conflicting accounts and consequences that could not be dismissed as ordinary observational error.

Asgar knew much of it from his own life.

Here it existed outside him, broken into records and reliability assessments.

He found the mention of the man near the booth and stopped there.

There was no new reliable information.

Asgar closed the fragment before he could read every link.

“What happened to this form afterward?”

---

The red profile appeared many times:

illustration.

symbol for a branch of research.

outer shell of a demonstration device.

Then a technical diagram appeared.

The booth looked similar enough that Asgar enlarged the image with both hands.

“There.”

Olmer leaned toward the screen.

“What is it?”

“Standard exterior design for an experimental volume,” the archive answered.

“The document references the historic red profile as a recognizable shape.

“Confirmed.”

“So they saw it,” Asgar said.

“The authors of the standard knew the historical image.”

“And built the same thing.”

Olmer pointed to the neighboring field.

“What’s inside it?”

Asgar opened it and immediately asked the next question himself.

“Does the internal device match my machine?”

“No.

“The internal architecture does not match the characteristics you provided.

“The confirmed correspondence is limited to exterior form.”

And yet the red walls, door, and narrow volume were already arranging themselves into a simple story in his head:

the booth appeared in the past.

people remembered it.

later they built things that looked like it.

eventually one such shell became the skin around his machine.

Asgar pushed the diagram away.

“Is this the origin of my machine?”

“That conclusion does not follow from the available data,” the archive replied.

“The similarity of exterior appearance is confirmed.

“Similarity of physical principle is not confirmed.

“The research lineage that produced the effect is traceable separately from this form.”

Asgar looked at the table for a while.

“Then show me that.

“How did the physics itself develop?

“No booth.”

Olmer leaned back in the chair.

“Now I want to see too.”

---

The display changed almost completely.

What remained were studies whose titles Asgar could not pronounce.

Measurement systems.

Arguments over results.

The archive translated the term *retroprojection* consistently; most of the rest it unfolded into explanations.

Asgar did not try to follow the equations.

He asked one thing over and over:

what did this work demonstrate?

what did the next work depend on?

The first convincing results came much later than his own time, and behind them stretched an independent history of measurement and failed experiments.

One early design looked like coupled volumes and external measurement nodes.

Nothing like a telephone booth.

“What did this line take from the red booth?”

“No calculations.

“No device.

“No method for producing the effect.

“Influence on research questions is confirmed: the historical event appears in discussions and review literature.

“No technical derivation from it is documented.”

Olmer read the explanation beside him.

“They may have been interested because of the story,” he said.

“But they still had to do the work.”

Asgar asked to bring back the projects with similar shells anyway.

Opened one.

Then another.

By the third, he noticed he was choosing them by resemblance of the picture.

“I’m chasing the shape again.”

“I noticed on the first one,” Olmer said without looking up.

“I was waiting for you to notice too.”

Asgar rubbed his eyes and closed the unnecessary windows.

The external image descended from the booth.

The physical effect did not.

---

The next day he stopped asking about appearance.

Olmer began at the neighboring table, working through his own material.

Asgar phrased the problem he had been living inside for months.

“The system is designed to return.

“Something gets inside that the calculation didn’t include.

“Extra mass.

“What did people write about that?”

The answer did not come immediately.

The archive separated work on unrelated processes and showed the earliest of what remained.

The old document contained no red image.

Its summary discussed the return of a system after unplanned attachment of external mass.

Asgar called Olmer over.

Olmer finished the record he was reading before he came.

“What is it?”

Asgar showed him the line.

Olmer read it, returned to the beginning, and asked about provenance.

An old stage of research.

The custody chain was traceable.

Some appendices were unavailable.

The main text survived.

They opened the explanation.

The system had been calculated for a defined composition.

Additional mass altered the return conditions.

Then came constraints and a discussion of why a solution valid for the initial state could not automatically be applied to the altered state.

Asgar pulled his eyes from the sentence and inhaled.

“They already knew.”

Olmer kept looking at the text.

“They had already studied this class of problem.”

“Is my name in there?”

“No.”

“Does the paper describe my machine?”

“No.

“No connection to a specific device is confirmed.

“The available material is insufficient to establish such a connection.”

But the problem was here.

Asgar reread the first paragraph, more slowly.

Altered system composition.

Unplanned mass.

The impossibility of simply continuing the original calculation.

He remembered the floor beneath his boots and the door he had closed because he wanted quiet.

“Show me where this was used afterward.”

Several continuations existed.

Some refined special cases.

Others cited the formulation of the problem itself.

The chain did not reach a specific machine.

Asgar began asking in different words.

Olmer waited for another answer, then moved his chair closer.

“Are you looking for the continuation now, or do you need there to be one?”

“Wouldn’t you?”

“I would.

“Especially after two days at this table.

“But I don’t see how wanting it helps us read what isn’t here.”

Asgar turned back toward the screen.

“If I ask it differently, does the answer change?”

“No.

“In the available records there is no established link between this work and your device.”

That did not make it feel better.

People had studied this type of difficulty long before his machine.

The booth’s own origin remained somewhere farther away.

When they left the repository, Asgar stood in the light with his eyes closed for several seconds.

“You hungry?” Olmer asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.

“Today we start there before you find another reason to go back inside.”

---

At the booth, Asgar relayed what they had found to Mirena.

He stood inside with the door open.

Olmer had gone to unload the cases and could not hear the conversation.

Asgar separated archive fact from the connections he himself had wanted to make.

When he reached the red profile, he hesitated.

“I wanted to decide that this was already the history of my machine.

“But there’s no chain from those things to it.”

“Did the archive preserve sources and transmission chains?”

“Yes.

“And the limitations.

“I can give you the wording.”

She asked him to start with the old work on external mass.

Asgar read the prepared translation, stopped at a disputed term, and plainly admitted he did not understand it well enough to paraphrase it.

Mirena asked him to repeat the explanation in full.

He did.

This time he did not want to hurry her toward a useful answer.

When she asked about the source, he opened the relevant record on the local device lying near the door and read while holding the receiver at the limit of its cord.

“All right.

“That’s enough.

“I’ll pass it to the engineers.”

Asgar put the device down.

“I didn’t find who built it.”

“Understood.”

He was quiet.

“That option in Ar.

“Separation.

“Is it still available from your side?”

“Yes.

“We need a stable window and preparation time.

“If you say the word, we start calculating.”

Asgar looked out through the open door at the path where Olmer was unloading cases.

“I’m not saying it yet.”

“I’ve recorded that you asked.”

“But now I know people studied this kind of problem long before the machine I entered existed.

“How that line reached the machine is still missing.”

Mirena did not turn that into a promise.

They agreed on the next contact.

---

By the twelfth day, Olmer had stopped reminding him where the water was or which exit closed in the evening.

Asgar learned to pack field gear so the thing he needed would not be buried underneath everything else.

The fire still did not work every time, but he could now tell when the material was too damp and when the problem was simply his own impatience.

He assembled the filter without help.

Before Asgar left, Olmer placed a soft belt pouch on the table.

Inside were a folding knife, firesteel, mechanical filter, thin metal container, and cord.

Beside them lay a small packet of salt and ten sealed test strips.

Asgar counted them before packing them.

“You already ruined the training ones at my expense,” Olmer said.

“Take care of these.”

He went over the limits one more time.

The strips could warn about some hazards.

They were not permission to eat everything.

The filter had limited service life too and could not turn arbitrary water into safe drinking water.

Asgar closed the pouch and put it around his waist.

“Pinching anywhere?”

“No.”

“Sit.”

When he sat, the edge of the container pressed into his stomach.

He had to rearrange the contents and shift the pouch slightly.

Olmer waited for him to stand and sit again.

“Now?”

“Fine.”

Asgar ran a palm over the clasp.

“Thank you.”

Olmer nodded, already putting away the remaining training gear.

They walked to the booth together.

Asgar carried the bag from Ar in one hand.

The phone, wooden piece, and Bertram’s tool remained inside among the clothes.

He returned the local translator to Olmer after his last conversation with Mirena.

Asgar had already reported the contents of the new survival kit.

The mass check would happen once the door was closed.

Asgar paused on the path.

Twelve days earlier Olmer had asked him to clear this same passage.

Now Olmer stood to the side of the door holding the returned device and waited for Asgar to finish.

Asgar touched the belt pouch, then shook his hand.

Inside the booth, he had to place the larger bag differently.

He checked that it would not interfere with the door, lifted the receiver, and told Mirena he was ready.

Beyond the glass, Olmer picked up a fallen branch from the path and carried it to the edge of the paved area.

Then he returned to where he had been standing.

Asgar looked at him.

At the canopy where he had scattered the dry fibers again and again.

At the dark entrance to the node.

Then he closed the door.
