Chapter 25. Through So Many Hands

Olmer placed a rod and a small piece of metal in front of him.

Asgar picked them up and looked at the dry fibers in the shallow bowl. Yesterday everything had looked easier. Olmer had swept his hand once—sparks fell, an orange point appeared among the fibers. Now the metal scraped unpleasantly along the rod, and the sparks died before reaching the bowl.

"Closer," Olmer said.

Asgar leaned in.

"Hands closer. Your head isn't needed yet."

He moved the bowl. On the third attempt, several sparks landed in the middle. The fibers darkened, but nothing happened.

Olmer sat opposite, repairing a fastening on his field bag. He did not take the items away from Asgar or demonstrate again after every failure. Sometimes he looked up, said one or two words, and went back to the fastening.

When smoke finally appeared, Asgar blew too quickly. The fibers scattered.

Olmer set the bag on his knees.

"Now gather it back up. That's why we're doing this here."

There was no wind under the canopy. Water stood nearby; food waited inside the building. Asgar gathered the scattered material, pushing the fibers back into the bowl with movements that were too sharp. In recent months he had had to figure out things for which he did not even know the ordinary names. A small fire would not work.

The next attempt worked.

He held the bowl in both hands and watched the flame gradually catch the thin prepared splinters. Olmer waited until it was stable, then explained how to extinguish it and check what remained.

"Again tomorrow," he said.

"I understand it now."

"We'll see tomorrow."

Asgar set the bowl aside.

"Why are you doing this? You have routes."

Olmer tightened the repaired fastening and tugged it.

"You said yesterday that you're going back into that thing. And that next time there may be no node."

"I did."

"I can't verify half your story. This I can."

He nodded toward the bowl of fibers.

"Every year someone goes into the field counting on a node. Then people look for them from their last stop."

Olmer put the bag aside.

"This costs me a few evenings. For you, it could be the first twenty-four hours."

---

They went to the archive several days later, after Olmer finished his rounds and handed over the collected samples. Before that, Asgar helped him around the node, learned to use the filter and tie a knot that did not cinch permanently under load. In the evenings they returned to the queries.

The booth remained by the path. Asgar went to the telephone regularly; before leaving, he told Mirena where he was going and when he expected to return. She confirmed the current state was holding but would not promise how long it would last.

Olmer waited by the transport with two cases.

"Those are for the archive?" Asgar asked.

"For the neighboring sector. I have my own work there. You work, I turn in materials, then we'll see who ends up waiting for whom."

On the way, Asgar looked at the forest. Bare rock opened between the trees, then green began again. After the trip to the city's former location, he stopped looking for a familiar curve of shoreline, but from time to time caught himself searching for a road where none could have existed.

From outside, the archival repository looked almost no different from the other low structures. Inside, the air was cooler. Olmer left Asgar at a work station, helped connect the node translator to the local system, and went away with the cases.

The first thing the archive offered Asgar was an overview.

A red rectangle appeared before the text.

He recognized the proportions immediately, though the image was schematic: a tall narrow cabin, divided windows, door at the front. Beneath it ran a chain of events. Asgar leaned forward, then stopped.

"Where did this image come from?"

"Overview reconstruction. Prepared for an educational summary."

The voice came without the delays Asgar had become used to at the post on the site. He pushed the reconstruction aside.

"Then let's agree right away. Tell me what's confirmed and what someone reconstructed or filled in later. If something isn't there, call it what it is: not there. I don't need it made pretty."

"Accepted. The distinction will be stated with each answer."

With Martin, that caveat would have taken a week of correspondence and two arguments.

"What is known about the origin of an object of this form?"

"There is no confirmed information about its origin. There is a sequence of recorded events beginning in the twenty-first century. There is a separate line of later images derived from one another."

"Remove the later ones. Leave only what existed independently."

The list visibly shrank.

When Olmer returned, Asgar was still sorting through what remained.

"Find anything?"

"I'm still figuring out what exactly they mean by 'found.'"

Olmer sat beside him and pulled the translation closer.

"Sensible. But you still have to eat."

---

"The earliest reliable record."

The archive named the year two thousand fourteen.

Asgar asked to see what had been filtered out before that year: disappearances, strange structures, people seen in the wrong time. Some of it fell apart as soon as the basis was opened. The rest had been connected to the red cabin by later authors.

"Remove all presumed matches."

August remained.

August 16, 2014 appeared large in the familiar format. Asgar placed both palms on the table.

That date had a morning. He remembered getting ready, talking to Katya, what he planned to do after the stadium. The archive began the history of the anomaly at a point when, for him, the day had not yet distinguished itself in any way.

"Nothing reliable before that?"

"There are no confirmed materials on this object before the stated date. This is an absence of data, not a claim that no events occurred."

Asgar read the answer to the end and did not widen the search backward.

Olmer looked at him, then at the date.

"Your day?"

"Mine."

He asked nothing else.

What followed were contradictory witness accounts and consequences that could not be reduced to ordinary observational error. Asgar knew much of it from his own life; here it existed outside him, broken into records and confidence assessments.

He found the reference to the man near the cabin and stopped there. There was no new reliable information.

Asgar closed the fragment before he could examine every link.

"What happened to this form later?"

---

The red profile appeared many times: illustration, emblem of a research direction, outer shell of a demonstration device. Then a technical diagram appeared.

The cabin resembled it so closely that Asgar enlarged the image with both hands.

"There."

Olmer leaned toward the screen.

"What is it?"

"Standard external design for an experimental volume," the archive answered. "The document cites the historical red profile as a recognizable form. Confirmed."

"So they saw it," Asgar said.

"The authors of the standard knew the historical image."

"And built the same thing."

Olmer pointed to the neighboring field.

"What's inside it?"

Asgar opened it and immediately asked his own question:

"Does its internal design match my machine?"

"No. The internal diagram does not match the features you described. The match applies only to the external profile."

And yet the red walls, the door, the narrow volume were already assembling into too simple a story in his head: the booth appeared in the past, people remembered it, later built something similar—and one day that shell would end up around his machine.

Asgar pushed the diagram away.

"Is this the origin of my machine?"

"That conclusion does not follow from the available data," the archive replied. "The visual match is confirmed. A match in physical principle is not confirmed. The early research line in which the effect was first produced can be traced separately from this form. That chain does not reach the specific configuration of your machine."

Asgar looked at the table for a while.

"Then show me that. How the physics itself developed. Without the booth."

Olmer leaned back in his chair.

"Now I want to see that too."

---

The image changed almost completely.

What remained were studies with names Asgar could not pronounce, measurement installations, disputes over results. The archive consistently rendered the term "retroprojection"; the rest it unfolded into explanations.

He did not try to understand the formulas and asked one thing: what result was obtained and what the next work relied on. The first convincing results appeared much later than his own time, and before them stretched an independent history of measurements and failures. One of the early diagrams looked like a system of coupled volumes and external measurement nodes. Nothing like a telephone booth.

"What did this line take from the red cabin?"

"In the early stages, no direct borrowing of calculations, device structure, or method of producing the effect has been confirmed. The historical event influenced the questions being asked and the criteria used for verification. Later project layers are less well preserved. These materials do not support either a claim of direct technical continuity or a claim that there was none."

Olmer read the explanation beside him.

"They could have been interested because of the story," he said. "But they still had to do the work."

Asgar nevertheless asked to bring back the projects with similar shells. He opened one, then another, and on the third realized he was selecting by visual similarity again.

"I'm back to chasing the shape."

"I noticed on the first one," Olmer said without looking up. "I was waiting for you to notice."

Asgar rubbed his eyes and closed the unnecessary windows.

The outer image clearly came from the booth. The physics had started down its own road. Where and when those two lines met again, the archive still did not show.

---

The next day he stopped asking about appearance.

Olmer was first working with his own materials at the neighboring table. Asgar formulated the problem he had been living with for the last few months:

"The system is designed to return. Something entered it that wasn't in the calculation. Extra mass. What did people write about that?"

The answer did not come immediately. The archive separated papers about completely different processes and showed the earliest of what remained.

The old document had no red image.

Its abstract discussed the return of a system with an unplanned attachment of external mass.

Asgar called Olmer over.

Olmer finished reading his own record and only then came across.

"What is it?"

Asgar showed him the line.

Olmer read it, went back to the beginning, and asked about the material's provenance. An old stage of research; the chain of custody could be traced, some appendices were unavailable, the main text survived.

They opened the explanation.

The system had been calculated for a defined composition. Additional mass changed the return conditions. Then came variants of constraints and an analysis of why a solution for the original state could not automatically be applied to the altered one.

Asgar looked away from the line and took a breath.

"They already knew."

Olmer looked at the text.

"They had already considered that kind of problem."

"Is my name in here?"

"No."

"Does this work describe my machine?"

"No. A connection to a specific device isn't confirmed. The available materials don't contain data for that link."

But the problem was here.

Asgar read the first paragraph again, more slowly this time. A changed system composition. Unplanned mass. The impossibility of simply continuing the old calculation.

He remembered the floor under his boots and the door he had closed just to get some quiet.

"Show me where this was used afterward."

Several continuations appeared. Some refined particular conditions, others cited the problem formulation itself. The chain still did not reach a specific machine.

Asgar began asking in a different way.

Olmer waited for the answer and pulled his chair closer.

"Are you looking for a continuation right now, or do you want there to have to be one?"

"Wouldn't you?"

"I would. Especially after two days at this table. But I don't see how wanting it helps us read something that isn't here."

Asgar turned toward the screen.

"If I ask differently, does the answer change?"

"No. The available materials do not trace a connection between this work and your device."

That did not make it more pleasant. People had studied this class of difficulty long before his machine, while the origin of the cabin itself remained farther away.

When they left the repository, Asgar stood in the light with his eyes closed for several seconds.

"Hungry?" Olmer asked.

"Yes."

"Good. Today we'll start with that before you find another reason to go back."

---

At the booth, Asgar relayed the findings to Mirena.

He stood inside with the door open. Olmer had gone to unload the cases and could not hear the conversation. Asgar separately explained what was an archival fact and where he himself saw a possible connection.

When he reached the red profile, he hesitated.

"I wanted to decide that this was already the history of my machine. But the chain doesn't reach it."

"Do you have the sources and transmission chains?"

"In the local archive. And the limitations too. I can give you the wording."

She asked him to start with the old work on external mass. Asgar read the prepared translation, stopped at a disputed term, and honestly said he did not understand it well enough to paraphrase.

Mirena asked him to repeat the explanation in full.

He did.

This time he did not want to push her toward a convenient answer. When she clarified a source, he opened the relevant record on the local device lying by the door and read from it without moving away with the cord in his hand.

"Good. That's enough. I'll pass it to the engineers."

Asgar put the device down.

"I didn't find who made it."

"Understood."

He was silent for a moment.

"That option from Ar. Separation. Is it still available on your side?"

"Yes. We need a stable window and preparation time. Say the word and we'll start calculating."

Asgar looked through the open door at the path where Olmer was unloading cases.

"I'm not saying it yet."

"I've recorded that you asked."

"But now I know that people were considering this kind of problem long before the machine I ended up in. How they got from there to it—this archive still doesn't have that."

Mirena did not turn that into a promise. They agreed on the next contact.

---

By the twelfth day, Olmer had stopped reminding him where the water was and which exit closed in the evening.

Asgar learned to pack the field items so the thing he needed was not buried beneath everything else. The fire still did not always work, but he could already tell when wet material was the problem and when it was his own hurry. He assembled the filter without prompting.

Before he left, Olmer placed a soft waist pack on the table.

Inside were a folding knife, fire rod, mechanical filter, thin metal container, and cord. Separately lay a small packet of salt and ten sealed test strips. Asgar counted them before putting them away.

"You already ruined the practice ones at my expense," Olmer said. "Save these."

He reminded him again of the limits of testing. The strips could warn of certain dangers; they were not permission to eat everything. The filter also had a limited capacity and did not make any water safe.

Asgar fastened the pack and tried it around his waist.

"Does it press anywhere?"

"No."

"Sit down."

When he sat, the edge of the container pressed into his stomach. He had to rearrange the items and shift the pack slightly.

Olmer waited until he stood and sat again.

"Better."

Asgar ran a hand over the clasp.

"Thank you."

Olmer nodded, already putting away the remaining training equipment.

They walked to the booth together. Asgar carried the bag from Ar in his hand; the phone, figurine, and tool remained inside among the clothes. He returned the local translator to Olmer after the final conversation with Mirena. Asgar had reported the composition of the new items to Mirena in advance; the mass check would be left until the moment the door closed.

On the path, Asgar paused.

Twelve days earlier, Olmer had asked him to clear the passage. Now he stood beside the door holding the returned device, waiting for Asgar to finish getting ready.

Asgar indicated the waist pack, then shook his hand.

Inside, he had to position the large bag a little differently. He checked that it did not block the door, lifted the receiver, and told Mirena he was ready.

Beyond the glass, Olmer picked up a fallen branch from the path and carried it to the edge of the surface. He returned to the same place.

Asgar looked at him, at the canopy beneath which he had scattered dry fibers several times, at the dark entrance to the node.

Then he closed the door.
