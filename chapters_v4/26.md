Chapter 26. While the Fire Holds

The first thing he saw was a rock right by the door.

The rock lay on its side, pressed into the slope; beyond it ran a strip of grass, farther on the ground dropped out of sight. Asgar leaned toward the glass, trying to understand how steep it was. Water gleamed below. Trees grew on the opposite slope, with pale ribs of rock projecting between them.

Mirena gave the year: sixty-one million eighty-four thousand seven hundred seventy-three.

He repeated the number to remember it. Then asked about the air.

While the check was running, he looked along the river. No road, no building, no even opening between the trees. Several times he shifted his gaze from one bank to the other, hoping to notice at least a support, a railing, anything straight.

"I can't see anyone here," he said.

"Describe the area in front of the door."

Asgar described the rock, the grass, and the slope. The check allowed him to open the door. He pulled it toward himself, held it, and stuck his head out. The river was much louder than it had seemed through the glass. It smelled of wet earth.

"I can get down. It's gentler on the left."

"Take your things. Stability of this state is decreasing."

He nudged the bag closer with his foot and checked the clasp of the waist pack.

"Next window?"

"No time has been established."

He already knew that answer. Still, he waited before putting down the receiver.

Outside, he had to set the bag on the ground first, then climb down himself. Asgar went around the rock and picked up his things. When he looked back, the red upright was becoming transparent. The slope was visible through it.

He stepped back toward it.

The booth disappeared.

Asgar remained with one hand outstretched. The grass that had been pressed down at the threshold slowly straightened.

"Mirena."

The river drowned out his voice.

He walked to where the door had been, looked down, then up the slope. Shifted the bag from one hand to the other.

"All right," he said. "All right."

He stood a little longer, then began descending toward the water.

---

The first evening, he could not get a fire going.

Asgar gathered branches that looked dry and scraped at the rod for a long time until his fingers hurt. The sparks landed where they should, but the material darkened without catching. He broke one branch. It was damp inside.

Under Olmer's canopy, everything had been prepared.

Asgar swore, put the rod away, and sat beside the bag. Light was leaving the slope. There was still sun on the other side of the river, but he had no intention of crossing: the water ran fast, the stones beneath it vanished in foam.

He used one test strip on the water. It did not show the dangers it was capable of detecting. Asgar filtered a little water and drank several swallows.

Then he began looking for somewhere to spend the night.

A recess beneath overhanging rock was higher up the slope. He had to duck to enter. Farther in, there was room to sit and stretch out his legs; a dark crack ran under the ceiling, but he did not crawl into it. The floor was dry. Asgar examined the ground, found no fresh droppings, listened, and only then brought his things inside.

He kept the jacket on. Put spare clothes from the bag under his side and lay down.

Something rustled outside.

He propped himself on one elbow, peering toward the entrance. The rustling stopped. After a while it started again, closer to the ground.

Asgar took out the knife and opened it, then realized he would fall asleep with an exposed blade in his hand. He closed it and placed it beside him.

The night was long. Several times he thought dawn was coming, but only the darkness at the entrance changed. He wanted to check the time. His hand automatically reached toward the bag where the phone lay.

He did not take it out.

By morning everything hurt from the awkward position. Asgar climbed out, urinated behind the rocks, and drank for a long time in small swallows. Then he climbed to the place where the booth had appeared.

The slope was empty.

He left a noticeable pile of stones there and returned to the river.

---

On the third day, he got a fire from the dry core of a broken trunk.

His fingers were shaking from hunger. When the thin shavings caught, Asgar did not believe it at first. For too long, smoke had meant only another failure. He added the prepared twigs, shielded the flame from the wind, and sat over it until the heat began to burn his face.

"There," he said. "See?"

The word had nowhere to go.

He put the water container near the fire. Then carried some of the coals to the shelter and set up a hearth outside the entrance, where smoke would not be pulled inside. He spent a long time finding a stable position for the metal vessel. Once he knocked it over and scalded his fingers with hot water. The burn was minor.

By evening he had two separate piles of branches: dry for the fire and those still needing to dry.

Food was harder.

He watched small animals, but remembered Olmer's words. What they ate did not become safe for him because of that. The test strips helped him reject one sample; on another they showed none of the dangers they could detect. Asgar stared at the result for a long time, understanding how little he had learned in exchange for one of ten strips.

Small silver bodies moved in the river. He tried catching them with his hands, then driving them between rocks. Almost all escaped. His first catch was smaller than his palm. Asgar cleaned it, lost part of the meat, and held it over the fire longer than he wanted, afraid to eat it raw.

He ate slowly. Put the bones in a pile beside him.

At night he woke and listened to his stomach.

Gradually there were foods after which he did not become ill. He had to find them again in the same places without substituting similar-looking things. Once he substituted anyway. He spent the night vomiting by the entrance; the next day he could only lie down and sip water. He threw the remainder far from the camp.

He stopped trying new food in the evening.

He spent hours in the shallows moving stones. Water washed the barrier apart, fish found exits, his hands went numb even on a warm day. When he managed to build a trap that was not destroyed immediately, he began checking it in the morning and before dark. Sometimes there was nothing. Sometimes there was enough not to go to bed with an empty stomach.

This still was not a secure life. It was the next day.

---

He twisted his left ankle while returning with water.

A stone rolled under his foot. Asgar tried to catch himself, shifted his weight the wrong way, and fell onto his side. The container hit the ground, water spilling across the grass.

At first he lay there with one hand pressed to his ankle. Then he sat up and tried to put weight on it.

Pain made him remove the weight immediately.

"Not this."

The shelter was not far. Normally he crossed that section without noticing the climb. Now he moved sitting down, helping himself with his hands and his good leg. He dragged the container by the rim. Twice he stopped to catch his breath, and each time looked downhill: the water was still there.

Inside the shelter, he carefully removed the boot. The ankle was swelling.

There was no one to show it to.

He remembered Olmer talking about keeping supplies near the sleeping place and punched the ground. He still had boiled water. Some food too. He had always thought of it as excessive caution because the river was close.

Now "close" meant a descent he could not make.

For the first few days Asgar barely moved away from the entrance. He pulled dry branches closer, rationed water, rested the leg on rolled clothes. To reach one needed thing, he had to move everything else. He finally assigned the knife, rod, and container each its own place.

At night he woke from pain whenever he turned in his sleep.

Before going down, Asgar chose a straight dry branch near the entrance, shaved off the side twigs with the knife, and pressed it into the ground several times. He descended to the water with that stick, choosing a route where he could brace himself with his free hand. He climbed back with a partly filled container. At the entrance he sat down and cried from exhaustion. Briefly, almost soundlessly. Then he saw he had set the water on a sloping rock and moved it to a flat one.

The swelling gradually went down. He began walking farther, first along the wall, then to the river. The leg hurt with a bad movement but held. After several weeks, an ordinary step no longer required effort.

On wet stones, he still stopped and tested his footing.

---

At first Asgar marked every day with a separate line on a flat stone by the entrance.

Then he ran out of space. He began combining the marks into groups and counting long intervals as approximate months. Sometimes he woke after a bad night and could not remember whether he had made yesterday's mark or only intended to. He marked such places separately.

There was no exact date anymore. The count remained.

In the morning he checked water, fire, and the trap. Then chose work according to the weather. On dry days he stored fuel, expanded storage space, brought in bedding to replace damp material. After rains he repaired what had washed out. By evening he tried to leave himself enough strength for one more trip down to the river.

It did not always work.

Once he spent the whole day rebuilding the barrier in the shallows, and at night rain raised the river and carried it away with the catch. Asgar stood in the downpour and shouted at the river. In the morning he did not want to go outside for a long time.

By midday he became thirsty.

He got up.

He checked the place where the booth had appeared every day until his leg forced him to skip the climb. Then he returned every other day, and later combined the visit with other tasks. The pile of stones settled; he rebuilt it.

There was no sign of the machine.

For the first few weeks he prepared a report for Mirena. Memorized where the shelter was, what he had managed to find, what had happened to his leg. Formed complete sentences, sometimes speaking them aloud.

Later he began forgetting the beginning of the report.

"The leg is better," he would say by the hearth. "I'm walking. Only downhill for now..."

He stopped because no one asked how exactly the descent was going.

---

When it became cooler, Asgar realized the fire alone was not enough.

The wind changed direction and reached him deep inside the shelter. He built a screen of branches at the entrance, leaving a passage and room for smoke to escape, then rebuilt it: the first screen went down in a strong gust. The new work took several days.

Clothes dried badly. He wore one set while the other lay near the heat. The edge of an old patch on his trousers was coming apart. He could not repair it neatly; he had to pull the fabric together more crudely. The boots still held, but the soles were wearing down.

His body became lighter.

He noticed when the waist pack began slipping. Tightened the strap, then some time later tightened it again. He rarely saw his face: the reflection broke in moving water, and the black phone glass showed only separate features.

His hair fell into his eyes. He tried cutting it with the knife, spent a long time on it, and left it uneven. He was more afraid to touch the beard: too easy to cut himself where he could not see.

The salt ran out. He had long used the filter sparingly; when it began working worse, he stopped relying on it and spent more time on water and fuel. A few test strips remained. He kept them dry, but now more often avoided new foods entirely.

There were good weeks. The trap held prey, tested fruit ripened in a familiar area, the weather allowed him to dry a reserve. Asgar ate more, became more talkative, sometimes hummed.

The words of songs disappeared in the simplest places.

He started again, reached the same gap, and stopped singing.

One morning he said Katya's name and fell silent: in his own voice it sounded unfamiliar.

After that, he said the names every day.

The sons were harder. He called Katya the way he always had. With the boys he had to choose: say the name—and immediately decide what age of person he meant. The one who had fallen asleep against his side at the table and the grown man whose voice once called him Dad through the partition answered to the same name.

He still said both. He just stayed silent longer afterward.

---

Olmer's container darkened from the fire. One side had a dent from the fall by the river, and Asgar always set it down the same way so it would not rock. The rod grew thinner. The knife needed care; he spent time on it even when he wanted only to lie down.

In the evenings Asgar laid the items out in front of him, wiped them, checked them, put them away.

Sometimes he said:

"This one will still work."

Or:

"I'll deal with it tomorrow."

He knew he was talking to himself. But after a whole day of silence, it let him finish the work as though someone had accepted it.

He placed Tobin's figurine on a ledge deep inside the shelter. At first he put it back in the bag; then he left it there. Bertram's tool lay beside it, rarely useful here. Asgar still checked it, turned the head, returned it to place.

Once he caught more than usual. He cooked the food and divided it into two roughly equal portions.

He put one in front of himself.

He pushed the other toward the empty place by the wall.

His hand was still extended when he realized what he had done.

Asgar looked at the second portion, then at the entrance. His clothes hung there, thin branches were drying, the stick lay nearby. No one was supposed to come back for dinner.

He covered the second portion and left it for morning.

That evening he ate without appetite even though he had been hungry all day.

---

The cold retreated. New leaves appeared on familiar branches. The river changed color, flooded, then settled lower. Asgar moved the trap and spent several days finding again the place where it worked.

He counted the marks.

One day he counted everything from the beginning and got a number he did not like. He went through them again, checked the groups and uncertain marks.

The difference could have been several days.

It no longer mattered.

That evening he climbed to the booth site and stayed until dark. The wind moved along the slope. Asgar sat on the rock where the threshold had once been and for the first time allowed himself to finish the thought he had always cut off: the booth might fail to remain for another week or fail to appear after the next rain. It might never return at all.

He tried to remember Mirena's exact words. No time established. Stability decreasing. She had made no promise about another window.

Asgar ran a hand over his face. The beard felt coarse and foreign.

Below waited water that had to be boiled. If he kept sitting, he would still have to fetch it tomorrow.

He went down in the dusk.

The next day he reinforced the shelf for dry fuel. Then cleared the drain by the entrance that he had neglected for a long time. He worked slowly, rested more often than he wanted.

In the evening he made another mark.

---

He stopped checking his reflection every time he washed. It took too much effort to make out a face in the water, and it told him too little that was new.

But one day the river was calm by the bank.

Asgar leaned down to fill the container and saw hollow cheeks, a dark tangled beard streaked with gray, graying hair at his temples; a thin pale line showed above his left eyebrow. He held the container above the surface, trying not to move, until the reflection trembled and the face he had not yet managed to examine as a whole broke into ripples.

The memory of the stadium came all at once: the red door, the man coming out, the brief movement aside. Asgar had tried for a long time to reconstruct his face and had always run into missing detail. Now memory eagerly supplied what he had just seen in the river.

He straightened too quickly, his foot slipped, and he had to catch his balance.

The same caution on the next step.

Asgar sat on the bank.

For the first time he could not dismiss the possibility: the man at the stadium might have been him. The thought had occurred after the scar above his eyebrow healed, but then it had remained an absurd coincidence.

He lifted a hand to the scar. Tried to remember the man at the stadium as he had actually seen him then, not as he imagined him now. What remained in memory was a tired face, a beard, an approximate age, and an uneven gait. Whether there had been the same mark above the eyebrow, Asgar no longer knew.

He looked at his boots. It was too easy to rebuild an old memory from what stood in front of him now.

He wanted to talk to Mirena—not to report a discovery, but to hear his own first description while it still existed separately from his current memory. He even turned toward the slope, then remembered the empty place above.

Asgar filled the container and returned to the shelter.

At night he woke to his own voice. He had dreamed he was trying to call out to the man at the stadium but did not know what name to use.

---

He met the second cool period better prepared.

Dry fuel lay farther inside. There were fewer gaps at the entrance. He knew which areas would flood first and moved things higher in advance. He still lost food, got cold, woke to rustling, but spent less strength on useless attempts.

He spoke little.

Before, words had helped hold order. Now order lived in his hands. In the morning he found the knife by touch in the same place, checked the coals, took out the container. He could work for hours without saying anything.

When he forgot why he had gone outside, he stopped and said aloud:

"For branches."

After saying it, he continued.

Sometimes he forced himself to tell stories about home. Not the booth and not the disappearance. Ordinary incidents that had once not seemed worth retelling: how they looked for a lost thing, were late, argued over something trivial. He discovered that he remembered his own explanations better than other people's words.

Asgar returned to the beginning of the story and tried not to make himself look better in it.

By his count, the months reached about twenty-two. He was not sure where the boundaries fell, but the overall duration left almost no doubt.

By then the climb to the booth site had become part of a longer circuit. Asgar looked there, rebuilt the settling pile of stones, and continued. He had stopped waiting in place.

---

That day, a shadow stopped him.

It crossed the grass where nothing should have been casting it. Asgar took another step and saw a red edge.

At first he did not move.

The bag of gathered branches slipped from his shoulder. He adjusted it automatically, still staring. The cabin stood slightly differently than before. In the rear glass, the slope began higher than outside.

Asgar set the branches down.

He walked slowly. At an uneven place, he set his left foot carefully and tested the rock with the stick. Near the booth he stopped again.

Touched the frame with his hand.

It was solid.

He went around the front corner, checking the ground and the position of the threshold, then pulled the door. It opened with the same resistance as before.

The telephone hung inside.

Asgar stepped in without closing the door, lifted the receiver, and pressed it to his ear.

"I..."

Nothing more came.

He swallowed. There was noise in the receiver, then a click.

"Asgar? Can you hear me?"

He squeezed his eyes shut.

"Yes."

"This is Mirena. Can you speak?"

Asgar nodded. Then remembered she could not see him.

"I can."

He stood with his forehead against the cool wall beside the telephone. Tears were running down his face. He wiped one cheek with his shoulder, but it only became worse.

Mirena waited.

"Are you injured now?"

"No. My leg... was. I can walk now."

"All right. Don't rush."

He tried to tell everything at once and lost the words. The shelter. The river. Food. It had been a long time. She asked short questions, and those he could answer.

"How much time have you lived since the last contact?"

Asgar looked through the doorway at the slope.

"Almost two years. By my marks. Around twenty-two months."

After a pause, Mirena asked him to confirm.

He repeated it. Then managed to explain how he had counted days and where errors could have entered.

She asked about weight, nutrition, the injury. Asgar said he had lost a great deal of weight, that he had twisted his left ankle long ago, that pain now appeared only rarely. He did not know his exact weight. He had no medical instruments.

As he answered, he gradually stopped pressing himself against the wall.

"I thought..." he began and stopped.

"What?"

"That there wouldn't be another one."

"The connection exists now," she answered. "I hear you."

Asgar lowered his head.

The river roared outside. It had been that loud the entire time, and he had almost stopped noticing it.

---

He did not close the door immediately.

After the check, it turned out he had time to collect his things. Asgar told her where the shelter was, clarified the state of the window, and put down the receiver.

On the slope he had to sit down.

He sat beside the abandoned bundle of branches until his hands stopped shaking. The booth remained nearby. Several times he raised his head to check.

Then he stood and carried the branches home.

The word came by itself. Asgar heard it in his head and did not correct it.

Everything in the shelter was in its place. Tobin's figurine stood on the shelf, the tool beside it. The phone was under clothes in the bottom of the bag. Asgar took it out, wiped it with cloth, and wrapped it again.

He wanted to leave everything as it was and run back.

Instead he drank water and ate. While chewing, he looked at the shelves he had made, the darkened stone by the entrance, the rows of marks. He could live another day here. And the next.

What pulled him to the machine was a human voice.

He remembered the question he had left Tessa with. There was still no answer. Even the man at the stadium remained a memory he wanted to trust more than it allowed.

Asgar packed the knife, the worn rod, the filter, and the remaining strips. He threw away the empty salt packet. Washed the metal container and secured it so it would not strike the other things. Packed the clothes, the figurine, the tool.

He extinguished the fire last.

He checked the coals, went back into the shelter for the stick, and saw the bare ledge where the wooden figurine had stood.

For a while he looked at it.

Then he went outside.

---

At the telephone, he spoke more steadily, though long sentences still required effort.

"I'm packed."

Mirena clarified the contents of his things. Asgar named what remained and separately mentioned what had been lost or consumed. When she asked whether he was ready to continue, he did not answer at once.

"I want to reach the source," he said. "That question remains."

"There is still no confirmed solution for returning you."

"I remember."

He looked at the open slope.

"One more thing... There was a man at the stadium. I told you about him."

"Yes."

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the receiver.

"Do you still have my first description? The one I gave back then?"

"We do."

"Don't repeat it now. Just keep it separately."

After a short pause, Mirena answered:

"All right."

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge of the receiver once more and lowered his hand.

When the check was complete, Asgar placed the large bag on the floor. He adjusted the waist pack on himself. Positioned the stick so it would not interfere with the door.

"I'm putting the receiver down," he said.

"I hear you."

He kept it to his ear for a few more seconds.

Then returned it to the cradle.

His hand settled on the door handle. Beyond the threshold were grass, scree, and the familiar turn toward the river. Asgar looked toward the place where the shelter entrance was hidden behind the rock.

He closed the door himself.
