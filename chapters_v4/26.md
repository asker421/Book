Chapter 26. While the Fire Holds

The first thing he saw was a rock beside the door.

It lay on its side, pressed into the slope. Beyond it ran a strip of grass, and farther on the ground dropped out of sight.

Asgar leaned toward the glass, trying to judge the angle.

Water flashed below.

Trees covered the opposite slope, with pale ribs of exposed rock between them.

Mirena gave him the year:

sixty-one million eighty-four thousand seven hundred seventy-three.

He repeated the number so he would remember it.

Then asked about the air.

While the checks ran, he looked along the river.

No road.

No building.

No straight opening between the trees.

Several times he moved his gaze from one bank to the other, hoping to catch some support, a barrier line, anything made straight.

“I can’t see anyone here,” he said.

“Describe the area in front of the door.”

Asgar described the rock, the grass, and the slope.

The checks allowed him to open the door.

He pulled it toward himself, held it, and leaned his head outside.

The river was much louder than it had sounded through the glass.

The air smelled of wet soil.

“I can get down.

“Left side is easier.”

“Take your things.

“The stability of this state is decreasing.”

He nudged the bag closer with his foot and checked the belt pouch clasp.

“The next window?”

“No interval established.”

He already knew the answer.

He still waited before replacing the receiver.

Outside, he had to put the bag on the ground first, then climb down himself.

Asgar went around the rock and picked up his things.

When he looked back, the red upright was translucent.

The slope showed through it.

He stepped back toward it.

The booth vanished.

Asgar remained with one hand extended.

Grass that had been flattened beside the threshold slowly lifted again.

“Mirena.”

The river drowned out his voice.

He went to the place where the door had been.

Looked down.

Then up the slope.

Moved the bag from one hand to the other.

“All right,” he said.

“All right.”

He stood there a little longer.

Then started down toward the water.

---

The first evening, he failed to make a fire.

Asgar collected branches that looked dry and scraped at the firesteel until his fingers hurt.

The sparks landed where they should.

The material darkened.

Nothing caught.

He snapped one branch.

It was damp inside.

Under Olmer’s canopy, everything had been prepared.

Asgar swore, put away the firesteel, and sat beside the bag.

Light was leaving the slope.

The opposite bank still caught the sun, but he was not going to cross.

The water ran fast.

Stones vanished beneath white foam.

He spent one test strip on the river.

It detected none of the dangers it was designed to recognize.

Asgar filtered a little water and drank several mouthfuls.

Then began looking for somewhere to sleep.

He found a hollow beneath an overhang higher up the slope.

He had to crouch to enter.

Farther in, he could sit and stretch out his legs.

A dark crack ran across the ceiling, but he did not crawl into it.

The ground was dry.

Asgar inspected the floor, found no fresh droppings, listened for a while, and only then moved his things inside.

He kept the jacket on.

Used spare clothing beneath his side and lay down.

Something rustled outside.

Asgar pushed himself onto one elbow and stared at the entrance.

The sound stopped.

After a while it came again, lower to the ground.

He took out the knife and opened it.

Then realized he would fall asleep holding an exposed blade.

He closed it and left it beside him.

The night was long.

Several times he thought dawn had come, but the darkness at the entrance had only changed shape.

He wanted to check the time.

His hand automatically moved toward the bag where the phone lay.

He did not take it out.

In the morning, everything hurt from the position he had slept in.

Asgar crawled out, urinated behind some rocks, and drank for a long time in small mouthfuls.

Then he climbed to where the booth had appeared.

The slope was empty.

He made a visible pile of stones and returned to the river.

---

On the third day, he got fire from the dry heartwood of a broken trunk.

His hands were shaking from hunger.

When the thin shavings caught, Asgar did not believe it at first.

For too long, smoke had meant another failure.

He added the twigs he had prepared, shielded the flame from the wind, and stayed over it until the heat began burning his face.

“There,” he said.

“See?”

There was no one to finish the sentence to.

He placed the metal container near the fire.

Later he carried some of the coals to the shelter and built a small hearth outside the entrance where smoke would not be drawn inside.

He spent a long time finding a stable position for the metal pot.

Once he knocked it over and scalded his fingers with hot water.

The burn was minor.

By evening he had two separate piles of branches:

dry ones for burning.

and those that still needed to dry.

Food was harder.

He watched small animals but remembered Olmer’s warning.

The fact that they ate something did not make it safe for him.

A test strip rejected one sample.

Another showed none of the dangers it could detect.

Asgar stared at the result for a long time, understanding how little one of his ten strips had actually bought him.

Small silver bodies moved in the river.

He tried catching them by hand.

Then tried driving them between stones.

Almost all escaped.

The first thing he caught was smaller than his palm.

Asgar cleaned it badly, lost some of the meat, and held the rest over the fire longer than he wanted because he was afraid of leaving it raw.

He ate slowly.

Placed the bones beside him.

Woke in the night and listened to his stomach.

Gradually he found foods that did not make him sick.

He had to find the same things again in the same places and not substitute something similar.

Once he did substitute.

He spent the night vomiting near the entrance.

The next day he could do little besides lie still and drink slowly.

He threw the remains far from camp.

After that, he stopped trying new foods late in the day.

He spent hours in the shallows moving stones.

The water tore down the barriers.

Fish found exits.

His hands went numb even on warm days.

When he finally built a trap that did not wash away immediately, he began checking it in the morning and again before dark.

Sometimes it was empty.

Sometimes there was enough not to go to sleep hungry.

It was not a secure life yet.

It was the next day.

---

He twisted his left ankle while coming back with water.

A stone rolled beneath his foot.

Asgar tried to catch himself, shifted his weight wrong, and fell sideways.

The container struck the ground.

Water spilled into the grass.

At first he lay there with one palm over the ankle.

Then he sat and tried to put weight on it.

The pain made him take the weight away immediately.

“Not this.

“Please not this.”

The shelter was not far.

Usually he covered the distance without noticing the climb.

Now he moved sitting down, helping himself with both hands and the good leg.

He dragged the container by one edge.

Twice he stopped to catch his breath.

Each time he looked down the slope.

The water remained there.

Inside the shelter, he carefully removed the boot.

The ankle was swelling.

There was no one to show it to.

He remembered Olmer saying to keep supplies near the sleeping place and struck the ground with his fist.

He still had some boiled water.

Some food.

He had always thought of those reserves as excessive caution because the river was close.

Now “close” meant a descent he could not make.

For the first few days, Asgar barely left the entrance.

He pulled dry branches within reach.

Rationed water.

Rested the leg on rolled clothing.

To reach one needed object, he had to move everything else.

He finally began placing the knife, firesteel, and container in fixed positions.

At night he woke in pain when he turned in his sleep.

Before attempting the descent, Asgar chose a straight dry branch, cut the side twigs away with the knife, and tested it against the ground.

He went down to the water using the stick, choosing a route where one hand could remain free for support.

He came back with the container only partly filled.

At the entrance he sat down and cried from exhaustion.

Not long.

Almost without sound.

Then he saw he had placed the water on a sloping rock and moved it to a flat one.

The swelling gradually went down.

He began walking farther.

At first along the wall.

Then to the river.

The ankle hurt when he stepped badly, but it held.

After several weeks an ordinary step no longer required thought.

On wet stones, he still stopped and tested his footing.

---

At first Asgar marked every day with a separate line on a flat stone beside the entrance.

Then he ran out of space.

He began grouping marks and counting longer spans as approximate months.

Sometimes he woke after a bad night and could not remember whether he had made yesterday’s mark or only intended to.

He marked those places differently.

There was no exact date anymore.

There was still a count.

In the mornings he checked water, fire, and the trap.

Then chose work according to the weather.

On dry days he gathered fuel, enlarged storage, replaced damp bedding.

After rain he repaired whatever had washed out.

By evening he tried to leave enough energy for one more trip down to the river.

It did not always work.

Once he spent an entire day rebuilding the barrier in the shallows.

That night rain raised the river and carried it away with the catch still inside.

Asgar stood in the downpour shouting at the water.

In the morning he did not want to leave the shelter.

By midday he was thirsty.

He got up.

He checked the booth site every day until the ankle forced him to skip the climb.

Then he went every other day.

Eventually he combined the check with other tasks.

The stone pile subsided.

He rebuilt it.

There was no sign of the machine.

For the first few weeks, he prepared a report for Mirena.

He memorized where the shelter was, what he had found, what had happened to the ankle.

Formulated it in complete sentences.

Sometimes spoke it aloud.

Later he began forgetting the beginning of the report.

“The leg is better,” he would say beside the fire.

“I can walk.

“Only downhill I still…”

He would stop because no one was asking exactly how downhill felt.

---

When the weather grew cooler, Asgar realized fire alone was not enough.

The wind changed direction and reached him deep inside the shelter.

He built a screen of branches at the entrance, leaving a passage and enough space for smoke to escape.

Then rebuilt it after the first one collapsed in a strong gust.

That took several days.

Clothes dried badly.

He wore one set while another lay near the heat.

The old patch on his trousers began separating.

He could not repair it neatly.

He pulled the fabric together more crudely.

The boots still held, but the soles were wearing thin.

His body became lighter.

He noticed when the belt pouch began sliding down.

He tightened it.

Later tightened it again.

He rarely saw his face.

The river broke his reflection.

The black glass of the phone showed only pieces.

His hair fell into his eyes.

He tried cutting it with the knife, worked at it for a long time, and left it uneven.

He was more afraid to cut the beard.

Too easy to slice skin he could not see clearly.

The salt ran out.

He had long been using the filter sparingly.

When it began performing worse, he stopped relying on it and spent more time on water and fuel.

Only a few test strips remained.

He kept them dry.

He now avoided new foods more often.

There were good weeks.

The trap produced food.

Known plants ripened in familiar places.

The weather let him dry reserves.

Asgar ate more, became more talkative, sometimes sang.

Lyrics disappeared in the simplest places.

He would start again.

Reach the same blank.

Stop.

One morning he said Katya’s name aloud and fell silent.

In his own voice, it sounded unfamiliar.

After that, he said names every day.

His sons were harder.

Katya was always Katya.

With the boys, he had to choose:

say a name—

and immediately decide which age of that person he meant.

The child who had fallen asleep against him at the table.

The adult whose face he had seen on someone else’s screen among fields of dates.

Both answered to the same name.

Asgar said both anyway.

Only stayed silent longer afterward.

---

Olmer’s metal container darkened over the fire.

A dent appeared in one side after a fall near the river, and Asgar always set it down the same way so it would not rock.

The firesteel grew thinner.

The knife needed maintenance.

He spent time on it even when all he wanted was to lie down.

In the evenings Asgar laid his things out, cleaned them, checked them, put them away.

Sometimes he said:

“This one will last.”

Or:

“Tomorrow.”

He knew he was talking to himself.

After a full day of silence, it allowed him to finish the work as though someone had accepted it.

He placed Tobin’s wooden piece on a ledge deep inside the shelter.

At first he packed it away every time.

Later he left it out.

Bertram’s tool lay beside it and rarely had any use here.

Asgar checked it anyway.

Turned the head.

Put it back.

One day he caught more than usual.

He cooked the food and divided it into two roughly equal portions.

One he placed in front of himself.

The second he slid toward the empty place near the wall.

His arm was still extended when he understood what he had done.

Asgar looked at the second portion.

Then at the entrance.

His clothes hung there.

Thin branches dried.

The walking stick lay nearby.

No one was expected home for dinner.

He covered the second portion and left it for morning.

That evening he ate without appetite despite having been hungry all day.

---

The cold receded.

New leaves appeared on familiar branches.

The river changed color, rose, then dropped again.

Asgar moved the trap and spent several days learning where it worked now.

He counted the marks.

One day he counted everything from the beginning and got a number he disliked.

He went through again.

Checked the groups.

Checked the uncertain marks.

The difference might have been several days.

It no longer mattered.

That evening he climbed to the booth site and stayed there until dark.

The wind moved along the slope.

Asgar sat on the rock where the threshold had once been and for the first time allowed himself to finish the thought he always stopped:

the booth might fail to return next week.

or after the next rain.

or ever.

He tried to remember Mirena’s exact wording.

No interval established.

Stability decreasing.

She had never promised another window.

Asgar ran a palm across his face.

The beard felt coarse and foreign.

Below waited water that needed boiling.

If he kept sitting here, tomorrow he would still have to go down and fetch it.

He descended in twilight.

The next day he strengthened the shelf where he stored dry fuel.

Then cleared the drainage channel by the entrance, a job he had kept postponing.

He worked slowly.

Rested more often than he wanted.

In the evening he made another mark.

---

He stopped checking his reflection every time he washed.

It took too much effort to find a face in the water and told him too little.

But one day the river was calm near the bank.

Asgar bent to fill the container and saw cheeks, tangled dark beard, hair at his temples.

Above the left eyebrow, a thin pale line.

He held the container above the surface without moving, trying to see the whole face before a ripple broke it apart.

The memory of the stadium arrived complete:

red door.

man stepping out.

short movement to one side.

Asgar had been trying for years to reconstruct that face and always ran into missing detail.

Now memory eagerly supplied what he had just seen in the river.

He straightened too quickly.

His foot slipped.

He had to catch himself.

The same cautious correction on the next step.

Asgar sat on the bank.

For the first time, he could not dismiss the possibility that the man at the stadium had been him.

The thought had occurred after the scar over his eyebrow healed, but back then it had remained an absurd coincidence.

He touched the scar.

Tried to remember the stadium man as he had actually seen him then, not as he imagined him now.

What remained was a tired face.

Beard.

Approximate age.

Uneven gait.

Whether there had been the same mark above the eyebrow, Asgar could no longer tell.

He looked at his boots.

It was too easy to rebuild an old memory from whatever was in front of him now.

He wanted to speak to Mirena—

not to report a discovery.

To hear his own first description while it still existed separately from the memory he had now.

He even turned toward the slope.

Then remembered the empty place above.

Asgar filled the container and returned to the shelter.

That night he woke to the sound of his own voice.

He had been dreaming that he was trying to call out to the man at the stadium but did not know what name to use.

---

He met the second cold season better prepared.

Dry fuel stayed farther inside.

There were fewer gaps at the entrance.

He knew which sections would flood first and moved things higher in advance.

He still lost food.

Still froze.

Still woke at noises.

But he wasted less strength on things that would not work.

He spoke less.

Words had once helped preserve order.

Now order lived in his hands.

In the morning he found the knife by touch in the same place.

Checked the coals.

Reached for the container.

He could work for hours without speaking.

When he forgot why he had gone outside, he stopped and said aloud:

“Branches.”

Once he said it, he kept walking.

Sometimes he forced himself to tell stories about home.

Not the booth.

Not the disappearance.

Ordinary events that would never have been worth retelling before:

looking for something that was lost.

being late.

arguing over something stupid.

He discovered he remembered his own explanations better than other people’s words.

Asgar returned to the start of each story and tried not to make himself look better inside it.

By his count, roughly twenty-two months had passed.

He was not certain of the boundaries.

The overall duration left little doubt.

By then the climb to the booth site was part of a longer circuit.

Asgar looked there, rebuilt the settling stone pile, and continued.

He no longer waited on the spot.

---

That day, a shadow stopped him.

It crossed the grass where nothing should have been casting one.

Asgar took another step and saw a red edge.

At first he did not move.

The bag of gathered branches slipped off his shoulder.

He adjusted it automatically without taking his eyes away.

The booth stood slightly differently than before.

Through the rear glass, the slope began higher than it did outside.

Asgar lowered the branches.

He walked slowly.

At an uneven patch he placed the left foot carefully and tested the stone with the stick.

Near the booth he stopped again.

Touched the frame with one hand.

Solid.

He went around the front corner, checked the ground and the threshold, then pulled the door.

It opened with the same resistance.

The telephone hung inside.

Asgar stepped in without closing the door, lifted the receiver, pressed it to his ear.

“I…”

Nothing else came.

He swallowed.

There was static.

Then a click.

“Asgar?

“Can you hear me?”

He squeezed his eyes shut.

“Yes.”

“This is Mirena.

“Can you speak?”

Asgar nodded.

Then remembered she could not see him.

“I can.”

He stood with his forehead pressed against the cool wall beside the telephone.

Something ran down his face.

He wiped one cheek with his shoulder.

That made it worse.

Mirena waited.

“Are you injured now?”

“No.

“The leg… was.

“I can walk now.”

“All right.

“Don’t rush.”

He tried to tell everything at once and lost words.

Shelter.

River.

Food.

Long time.

She asked short questions.

Those he could answer.

“How much time have you lived since our last contact?”

Asgar looked through the doorway at the slope.

“Almost two years.

“By my marks.

“About twenty-two months.”

After a pause, Mirena asked him to confirm.

He repeated it.

Then explained how he had counted and where the error might be.

She asked about weight, food, injury.

Asgar said he had lost a lot of weight.

That the left ankle had been injured a long time ago.

That now it only hurt occasionally.

He did not know his weight.

He had no medical instruments.

As he answered, he gradually stopped leaning against the wall.

“I thought…” he began.

Stopped.

“What?”

“That there wouldn’t be another one.”

“The connection exists now,” she said.

“I can hear you.”

Asgar lowered his head.

Outside, the river roared.

It had been that loud the entire time.

He had almost stopped noticing it.

---

He did not close the door immediately.

After the checks, they determined there was time for him to collect his belongings.

Asgar told Mirena where the shelter was, confirmed the state of the window, then hung up.

On the slope he had to sit.

He stayed beside the abandoned bundle of branches until his hands stopped shaking.

The booth remained there.

Several times he looked up to check.

Then he stood and carried the branches home.

The word came by itself.

Home.

Asgar heard it in his own head and did not correct it.

Inside the shelter, everything remained in place.

Tobin’s piece on the shelf.

Bertram’s tool beside it.

The phone wrapped beneath clothing inside the bag.

Asgar took it out, wiped the cloth, wrapped it again.

He wanted to leave everything and run back.

Instead he drank water and ate.

While chewing, he looked at the shelves he had built.

The darkened stone by the entrance.

The rows of marks.

A person could live one more day here.

And another.

What pulled him toward the machine was a human voice.

He remembered the question he had left Tessa with.

There was still no answer.

Even the man at the stadium remained a memory he wanted to trust more than the memory allowed.

Asgar packed the knife, worn firesteel, filter, remaining strips.

Threw away the empty salt packet.

Washed the metal container and secured it so it would not strike the other things.

Packed the clothes.

The wooden piece.

The tool.

Last, he put out the fire.

He checked the coals, went back inside for the walking stick, and saw the bare ledge where the wooden piece had stood.

For a while he looked at it.

Then he left.

---

At the telephone, his voice was steadier, though long sentences still required effort.

“I’m packed.”

Mirena asked about the contents.

Asgar listed what remained and separately reported what had been lost or used up.

When she asked whether he was ready to continue, he did not answer immediately.

“I want to reach the source,” he said.

“That question is still there.”

“There is still no confirmed solution for returning you home.”

“I remember.”

He looked at the open slope.

“One more thing.

“The man at the stadium.

“I told you about him.”

“Yes.”

Asgar ran a finger along the edge of the receiver.

“Do you still have my first description?

“The one I gave back then?”

“We do.”

“Don’t read it to me now.

“Just preserve it separately.”

After a short pause, Mirena answered:

“All right.”

Asgar ran his thumb along the edge once more and took his hand away.

When the check was complete, he placed the larger bag on the floor.

Adjusted the belt pouch.

Positioned the stick so it would not interfere with the door.

“I’m putting the receiver down,” he said.

“I hear you.”

He kept it to his ear for several more seconds.

Then returned it to the cradle.

His hand found the door handle.

Beyond the threshold were grass, scree, and the familiar bend leading down to the river.

Asgar looked toward the place where the shelter entrance hid behind the rock.

Then he closed the door himself.
