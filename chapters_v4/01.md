Chapter 1. Away from the Noise

By evening, the concrete in the stands was still holding the day’s heat. Asgar could feel it through his soles as he drew his feet in to let a man carrying cups squeeze past. The man edged sideways along the row, elbows raised, and every time someone jumped to their feet in front of him, he stopped with an expression that suggested the outcome of the match depended on whether he got his beer through safely.

An attack was developing on the field. Asgar craned his neck between other people’s shoulders, caught a glimpse of the ball right on the touchline—and the whole row rose. He stood with everyone else. The shot struck a defender; the stand exhaled, and someone nearby slapped the back of a seat in frustration. The man with the cups took advantage of the lull.

“Thanks, thanks… Your leg, please.”

Asgar pulled his knee back toward the seat. A cold drop landed on his hand. He shook it off and suddenly wanted a beer with painful clarity: that first long swallow, wet glass, the bitterness that tasted better than anything else in heat like this. But his phone was already vibrating in the front pocket of his jeans.

Katya’s name was on the screen.

Asgar answered, pressing his free palm over his other ear.

“Yeah, I can hear you.”

“Well, I can’t hear you. Are you still there?”

“At the stadium. Hang on, I’ll get out of the row.”

He turned toward the aisle. People were sitting down again, and he had to ask the same men to move their legs. One held his cup against his chest; another stood all the way up. Asgar squeezed past, clipped someone’s knee with his thigh, and turned to apologize. Katya was saying something into the phone. The only word that reached him was “home.”

“What?”

She repeated herself, but the roar behind him swelled so suddenly that he pulled the phone away and automatically looked back at the field. A player was down near the penalty area. The referee was running toward him with one arm raised.

“Asgar, are you talking to me?”

“Yeah. Everyone here just decided the referee needed help. I’ll find somewhere quieter.”

He went down the steps more slowly than he wanted to. Spectators were coming up against him, and someone had stopped in the middle of the stairs to film the field. Asgar stepped around a phone held out at arm’s length and ducked beneath the stands. A broad rectangle of evening light remained behind him for a few steps. Then the crowd blocked it out.

The concrete concourse smelled of hot oil and spilled beer. The floor stuck under his shoes. Asgar pressed himself to the wall to let the oncoming stream pass, then brought the phone back to his ear.

“All right. Go ahead.”

“Get water on your way. A big bottle. And tell me what time I should expect you so I know what to do with the cake.”

“What do you do with a cake? Eat it.”

“Wonderful. I’ll tell your son his father said he can start without him.”

“Kat, I’ll make it.”

“I asked what time.”

He looked back toward the shifting patch of light above the crowd. He could go back, watch a few more minutes, then leave before everyone else poured out. The attacks had finally started getting good. It felt stupid to sit through the whole match and miss the goal because you were already on your way out.

“Ten more minutes, and then…”

He stopped before she could answer.

A week ago he had told her exactly the same thing. He had been standing outside the office after work, holding the phone between shoulder and ear, explaining that they were only going in for a few minutes to talk something over. Ten minutes, tops. Then someone set beer on the table. Finger marks gathered on the misted side of his glass; he turned it in his hand, listened, joined in. When the next one arrived, he still remembered the promise. By then, though, keeping it required an unpleasant action: standing up, interrupting everyone, leaving.

He got home around two. His key kept striking the metal around the keyhole, and Asgar steadied his wrist with his other hand, angry at the lock. Katya opened the door from inside. He remembered the light from the hall, her bare feet, and his own careful, useless explanation: they had only been sitting there talking; they had not gone anywhere else.

In the morning, he had begun by explaining why he had not noticed the time.

Even then.

“Asgar?”

He rubbed a palm over the hot back of his neck.

“I’m listening. I’m leaving now. I’m already out of the stands.”

“You can say you want to finish watching. Just say it properly. Then I’ll sort everything out here myself.”

“You don’t need to. I’m coming.”

“Okay. Then don’t drag it out. And don’t forget the water, please.”

He already had a joke ready about water being taken more seriously in that house than husbands. He kept it to himself. Near one of the kiosks, a man lifted a cup over the line to signal a friend. Asgar turned toward a side passage.

“I remember. Big bottle. Home.”

“Yes. Preferably with the water still in it when it gets here.”

He smiled. Katya was smiling too; he could hear it in the last word. He wanted suddenly to be in the kitchen already, to put the water down and come up behind her while she fussed with the cake. Their son had turned ten today. Asgar had remembered August sixteenth since morning. He meant to be back in time for the cake.

Feet pounded overhead. A heavy, unified sound rolled through the concrete, and Katya’s voice vanished into it again.

“Wait. I missed the last part.”

Asgar turned into a less crowded passage. Two men stood against the wall here with their heads bent over one phone. Farther on, a service door stood open. Beyond it stretched a corridor with ceiling lights; no one was coming from that direction. He stepped through, intending to finish the call and head back toward the concourse exit.

The roar from the stands became duller, though every individual shout that reached the corridor through the open door went on echoing between the walls. Asgar walked a few more paces. Bare concrete ran along his left, marked by traces of old paint. Ahead, roughly halfway down the corridor, stood something red.

At first he took it for a cabinet. Then he made out the glass panes, the handle, and the rounded roof of a telephone booth.

Asgar stopped.

“Where did you go?” Katya asked.

“I’m here.”

The booth stood flush against the wall. From where he was, its rear was hidden by concrete, and Asgar looked for the edge of a recess it might have been pushed into. He could not see one. The gray wall ran straight, while the booth’s red edge seemed to end in the middle of it.

He shifted one step sideways. The lower corner looked wrong too: the line of the floor met the body of the booth higher than it should have. Asgar blinked and looked at the nearest lamp. It trembled faintly. In light like this, even an ordinary step could look like a hole.

“What is it?” Katya had heard his attention drift.

“A phone booth. Red. Old-fashioned. Right here in the corridor.”

“At the stadium?”

“That’s what I’m wondering.”

Something moved inside.

The door opened toward the concrete wall and stopped, leaving only a narrow gap. A shoulder in dark clothing appeared through it. The person paused as he turned, then put one leg over the threshold. Asgar stepped back to make room.

The man squeezed out and straightened in the corridor. Thin, gray hair stuck to one temple, gray threaded through his beard as well. He still had one hand on the edge of the door. He shifted his weight onto the leg he had put out, released the panel, and it began to swing slowly back. Their eyes met for a second. Asgar’s gaze lingered on his face, but the man had already looked away.

Asgar moved his phone a little farther from his cheek. The man looked exhausted: mouth slightly open, shoulders sagging. He passed Asgar with his eyes already fixed ahead, and Asgar noticed the uneven way he walked. One foot came down more carefully than the other. The light above them flickered; his face fell into shadow for a second.

Asgar watched him go. Near the far turn, the man stopped and looked back toward the booth. Asgar looked at the red door too. It had already closed. When he raised his eyes again, the man was disappearing around the corner.

“Asgar, I’m going to hang up.”

“No, wait. A man just came out of this thing. He looked completely wiped out.”

“Is he sick?”

“I don’t know. He left.”

Asgar kept watching the turn, expecting footsteps coming back or some sound from beyond it. The corridor stayed empty. Behind him, the door to the concourse slammed, opened again, and a fresh roar rolled into the service passage. Asgar covered his free ear.

“It’s quieter here, but still… What were you saying about the cake?”

“I asked whether you’re coming straight home or if you need to go somewhere else first.”

“Straight home.”

He walked up to the booth. Through the front glass he could see a black telephone mounted on the right-hand wall. Dust in the corners of the panes and the heavy metal handle made it look like some old thing that had been preserved for no obvious reason. Holding his mobile in his right hand, Asgar gripped the handle with his left and pulled.

The door opened reluctantly. Almost at once, its edge touched the concrete. He had to turn sideways.

“I’ll step into the booth for a second,” he said. “We’ll finish talking, then I’ll go.”

“Fine. Except now I’ve forgotten what I wanted to say.”

“Then we covered the important parts. Water and my disgraceful behavior.”

“The important part is that you come home. I can tell you the rest there.”

Asgar chuckled and squeezed through the gap. He put one foot over the threshold, turned his shoulder with the phone after it; the red frame passed close in front of his face. Inside, he turned around, planted both feet more comfortably, and took the inner handle.

“Are you stuck in there?” Katya asked.

“No. Heavy door.”

He pulled it toward him. At the last moment he had to put more force into it; the door settled into the frame with a dull thud.

The noise softened. Individual shouts disappeared into the general roar, but the many-thousand-strong crowd was still audible. Asgar took his hand off the handle and straightened.

Through the side glass, the concrete now looked farther away. From outside, the open door had struck the wall; now there was a strip of space between wall and glass wide enough for another person to stand in. Asgar leaned closer, trying to separate reflection from whatever lay behind it.

“There. Better.”

“Then listen. When you…”

The rest stretched into a brief burst of electrical crackle.

“When I what?”

He pressed the phone closer. The speaker repeated a fragment of sound, this time without the word. Beyond the glass, the pale strip of wall began sliding sideways. Asgar jerked his head back. The strip stopped.

“Kat?”

The call dropped.

He lowered the phone. The screen showed the call had ended. He almost dialed again, then reached for the door first. A metal box inside all this concrete—of course there would be no signal.

The handle would not turn.

Asgar pressed harder, then pulled the door toward him. There was not even any play in it. He braced one palm against the frame and tried again, this time pushing the panel outward. From the concourse came a long, collective shout. The corridor light blinked.

“What the…”

The floor vanished beneath his feet.

Asgar slammed shoulder-first into the red panel and bent his knees, waiting to fall. His stomach lurched into his throat. In the glass, the same ceiling lamp flashed twice—beside itself—and everything snapped back into place with a jolt that clicked his teeth together.

He was still standing, gripping the handle.

The stadium was gone.

Asgar stood with his mouth open. His first breath came short and rough; he heard it so close and clearly that for a moment he thought his ears had blocked. He swallowed, worked his jaw. Something clicked in his right ear.

His own breathing remained.

He rapped his knuckles against the glass.

The sound was clean and dry.

Beyond the door lay the same corridor. The same lamps, the same concrete, the same far turn. But there were no footsteps or voices from the concourse anymore. No one thundered overhead. Asgar waited for the crowd to erupt again, and the waiting became physically unbearable: he wanted to force the familiar noise back into the corridor himself.

“Hey!”

He struck the glass with his palm. The shout bounced inside the cramped space and hurt his ears.

“Open this! There’s someone in here!”

No one appeared at the service entrance.

Asgar yanked the handle, braced his feet harder. He was shaking after the jolt; his sole slipped and he nearly hit his forehead against the door. He stopped, pressed his left palm to the glass, and stared back toward where he had come from.

The match could not have ended that fast. Even if it had, people should have been coming through here, leaving, arguing. He had just been squeezing between them. He could still hear them when he touched this damned handle.

Asgar called Katya.

The call would not start.

He tried again, watching the screen, then raised the phone toward the glass. His hand was trembling. He wanted to hear even a ring tone. Any ordinary response—busy, unavailable, anything he could get angry at the network for.

Nothing.

“Kat, come on…”

He said it aloud and immediately turned back toward the corridor. No one was running to help him. The red frame divided the familiar place into narrow rectangles, and every one of them was empty.

Behind his right shoulder, a bell rang.

Asgar spun around, pressing the mobile to his chest. A black telephone hung on the wall. Beneath the receiver, a round metal dial caught the light. The ringing stopped; in the silence between bursts, Asgar could hear the scrape of his sole as he turned in the tight space between door and wall.

The telephone rang again.

Asgar reached for it with his free hand.
