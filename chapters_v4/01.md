Chapter 1. Away from the Noise

By evening, the concrete in the stands still held the day's heat. Asgar could feel it through the soles of his shoes as he drew his legs in to let a man carrying cups pass. The man edged sideways along the row, elbows raised, and every time someone jumped to their feet in front of him, he stopped with an expression that made it seem as though the outcome of the match depended on whether he got his beer through.

An attack was underway on the field. Asgar craned his neck between other people's shoulders, caught a glimpse of the ball right by the touchline—and the entire row rose. He stood up with everyone else. The shot hit a defender; the stand exhaled, and someone nearby slapped the back of a seat in frustration. The man with the cups took advantage of the pause.

"Thank you, thank you... Could you move your leg, please."

Asgar drew his knee back toward the seat. A cold drop landed on his hand. He shook it off and suddenly wanted a beer with startling clarity: that first big swallow, the wet glass, the bitterness that tasted better than anything else in heat like this. But his phone was already vibrating in the front pocket of his jeans.

Katya was on the screen.

Asgar answered, pressing his free palm over his other ear.

"Yes, I can hear you."

"I can't hear you. Are you still there?"

"At the stadium. Hold on, I'll get out of the row."

He turned toward the aisle. People were sitting down again, and he had to ask the same men to move their legs. One held his cup against his chest, another stood up completely; Asgar squeezed past them, bumped someone's knee with his thigh, and turned back to apologize. Katya was saying something on the phone. The only word from her sentence that reached him was "home."

"What?"

She repeated herself, but behind him the crowd roared so loudly that he pulled the phone away from his ear and automatically looked toward the field. A player was lying near the penalty area. The referee was running toward him with one hand raised.

"Asgar, are you talking to me?"

"Yes. Everyone here has just decided to help the referee. I'll find somewhere quieter."

He went down the steps more slowly than he wanted to: spectators were coming up toward him, and someone had stopped in the middle of the stairs to film the field. Asgar stepped around a phone held out at arm's length and ducked beneath the stands. Behind him, a broad rectangle of evening light was still visible. A few steps later, people blocked it from view.

The concrete concourse smelled of hot oil and spilled beer. The floor stuck underfoot. Asgar moved against the wall to let the oncoming stream pass, then raised the phone to his ear again.

"All right, go ahead."

"Buy water on the way. A big bottle. And tell me when to expect you so I know what to do about the cake."

"What is there to do with it? Eat it."

"Very funny. When am I supposed to get the candles out?"

"Katya, I'll make it."

"I'm asking what time."

He looked back toward the moving patch of light above the crowd. He could go back, watch a few more minutes, then leave before everyone else poured out. The attacks had finally started getting good; it would be annoying to sit through the whole match and then miss a goal while trying to get out.

"Ten more minutes, and..."

He stopped himself before she answered.

A week ago, he had told her the same thing. He had been standing outside after work, holding the phone between his shoulder and ear, explaining that they were only going in for a little while, just to discuss something. Ten minutes, maximum. Then beer appeared on the table. Finger marks remained on the misted side of his glass; he turned the glass in his hand, listened, joined the conversation. When the next one arrived, he still remembered the promise, but keeping it now required an unpleasant action—standing up, interrupting everyone, leaving.

He got home around two. The key kept striking the metal around the keyhole, and Asgar steadied his wrist with his other hand, angry at the lock. Katya opened the door from inside. He remembered the light from the hallway, her bare feet, and his own careful, unnecessary explanation: they had just been sitting there talking, they hadn't gone anywhere else.

In the morning, he started by explaining why he hadn't noticed the time. Even then.

"Asgar?"

He ran a palm over the hot back of his neck.

"I can hear you. I'm leaving now. I'm already out of the stands."

"You can say you want to finish watching. Just say it normally. Then I'll sort everything out here myself."

"Don't do it yourself. I'm coming."

"Okay. Then don't drag it out. And don't forget the water, please."

He already had a joke ready about how water was taken more seriously around here than a husband. He didn't say it. On the concourse, a man by a kiosk lifted a cup above the line to signal to a friend; Asgar turned toward a side passage.

"I remember. A big one, straight home."

"Yes. So it actually makes it home."

He smiled. Katya was smiling too—he could hear it in the last word. He wanted to be back in the kitchen sooner, set the water down, come up behind her while she fussed with the cake. His son turned ten today. Asgar had remembered August sixteenth since morning; he intended to make it back in time for the cake.

Footsteps thundered overhead. A heavy, blended sound rolled through the concrete, and Katya's voice disappeared into it again.

"Hold on, I didn't hear the last few words."

Asgar turned into a less crowded passage. Two men stood by the wall here, heads bent over the same phone. Farther on, a side door stood open. There was no sign and no barrier in front of it. Beyond the door stretched a corridor with lights along the ceiling; no one was coming from it. He stepped inside, planning to finish the conversation and return to the concourse exit.

The roar of the stands became duller, but every individual shout that reached this place through the open door echoed along the walls. Asgar walked a few more steps. On the left ran a concrete wall marked by traces of old paint. Ahead, roughly halfway down the corridor, stood something red.

At first he took it for a cabinet. Then he made out glass panels, a handle, and the rounded top of a telephone booth.

Asgar stopped.

"Where did you go?" Katya asked.

"I'm here."

The booth stood flush against the wall. From a distance, its rear section was hidden by the concrete, and Asgar looked for the edge of a recess it might have been pushed into. No recess was visible. The gray wall ran straight, while the red edge seemed to end in the middle of it.

He took a step sideways. The lower corner of the booth looked strange too: the line of the floor met the body higher than it should have. Asgar blinked and looked at the nearest light. It trembled faintly. In light like that, even an ordinary step could look like a hole.

"What's going on over there?" Katya had heard his attention drift.

"A telephone booth. Red, old. Right here in the corridor."

"At the stadium?"

"I'm surprised too."

Something moved inside.

The door swung open toward the concrete wall and stopped, leaving a narrow gap. A shoulder in dark clothing appeared through it. The person paused while turning, then put one foot over the threshold. Asgar stepped back to make room.

The man squeezed out and straightened up in the corridor. Thin, with gray hair stuck to his temple and a beard streaked with gray as well. With one hand he was still holding the edge of the door; he shifted his weight onto the foot he had put down, then let go of the door. It slowly began to swing back. Their eyes met for a second. Asgar's gaze lingered on his face, but the man had already looked away.

Asgar moved the phone slightly away from his cheek. The man looked exhausted: mouth parted, shoulders lowered. He walked past, now looking straight ahead, and Asgar noticed the uneven way he placed his feet. One foot came down more carefully than the other. The light above them flickered; for a second, the man's face fell into shadow.

Asgar watched him go. At the far turn, the man stopped and turned his head toward the booth. Asgar looked at the red door too. It had already closed. When he raised his eyes again, the man was disappearing around the corner.

"Are you all right?" Asgar called.

The man did not turn around. He only raised a hand—briefly, as if to show that everything was fine—and vanished around the corner.

"Asgar, I'm going to hang up."

"No, wait. A man just came out of here. He looks completely drained."

"Is he sick?"

"I don't know. I called out to him—he seemed to signal that he was okay."

He kept looking at the turn, waiting for footsteps coming back or some sound from beyond it. The corridor remained empty. Behind him, the door to the concourse slammed shut, then opened again, and a fresh roar rolled into the service passage. Asgar covered his free ear.

"It's quieter here, but still... What were you saying about the cake?"

"I was asking whether you're coming straight home or need to go anywhere else first."

"Straight home."

He walked up to the booth. Through the front pane, he could see a black telephone mounted on the right-hand wall. Dust in the corners of the glass panels and the heavy metal handle made it look like some old thing that, for some reason, had been preserved. Asgar took the handle with his left hand, holding his mobile in his right, and pulled.

The door opened with effort. Almost immediately, its edge touched the concrete. He had to turn sideways.

"I'm going to step into the booth for a second," he said. "At least I'll be able to hear you in there."

"Go ahead. I didn't finish what I was saying about the cake anyway."

"There. Now talk."

Katya started a sentence, but another roar rolled into the concourse, and Asgar heard only the beginning.

"Hold on, I'll close the door."

He squeezed through the opening. He slipped one leg over the threshold, then turned the shoulder holding the phone after it; the red frame passed close in front of his face. Inside, he turned around, planted both feet more comfortably, and took hold of the inner handle.

"Are you stuck in there?" Katya asked.

"No. The door's heavy."

He pulled it toward him. At the last moment he had to use more force; the door settled into the frame with a dull thud.

The noise softened. Individual shouts disappeared into the general roar, but the stand with its thousands of people was still audible. Asgar took his hand off the handle and straightened up.

From in here, the concrete beyond the side pane seemed farther away. From outside, the door had hit the wall, but now there was a strip of space between the wall and the glass wide enough for another person to fit. Asgar leaned forward, trying to separate the reflection from what he was seeing beyond it.

"There. That's better."

"Then listen. When you..."

The end of the sentence stretched into a brief electrical crackle.

"When I what?"

He pressed the mobile closer. A fragment of sound repeated in the speaker, no longer a word. Beyond the glass, the pale strip of wall began to slide sideways. Asgar jerked his head back; the strip stopped.

"Katya?"

The call dropped.

He lowered the phone. The call had ended on the screen. He was about to dial again, but first he reached for the door: a metal box, concrete all around—of course there was no signal in here.

The handle would not turn.

Asgar pressed harder, then pulled the door toward himself. There wasn't even any play in it. He braced a palm against the frame and tried again, this time pushing the door outward. A long, collective shout came from the concourse, and the light beneath the corridor ceiling flickered.

"What the..."

The floor dropped away beneath his feet.

Asgar slammed his shoulder into the red panel and bent his knees, expecting to fall. His stomach lurched into his throat. In the glass, the same light flashed twice—next to itself—and everything snapped back into place with a jolt that made his teeth clench.

He remained standing, gripping the handle.

He could no longer hear the stadium.

Asgar stood with his mouth open. His inhale came short and rough; he heard it so close and clearly that for a moment he thought his ears had blocked. He swallowed and worked his jaw. His right ear popped. The sound of his own breathing remained.

He rapped his knuckles against the glass.

The sound was clear and dry.

Beyond the door lay the same corridor. The same lights, concrete, the distant turn. But no footsteps or voices came from the concourse anymore. No one was thundering overhead. Asgar waited for the crowd to roar again, and the waiting became physically painful: he wanted to force the familiar noise back into the corridor himself.

"Hey!"

He struck the glass with his palm. The shout bounced around the cramped interior and hurt his ears.

"Open up! There's someone in here!"

No one appeared at the service entrance.

Asgar yanked the handle and planted his feet harder. He was still shaking from the jolt; his sole slipped on the floor, and he nearly hit his forehead against the door. He stopped, pressed his left palm to the glass, and stared toward where he had come from.

The match could not have ended that quickly. Even if it had, people should have been coming through here, leaving, arguing. He had only just been squeezing between them. He could still hear them when he touched that damned handle.

Asgar called Katya.

The call would not go through.

He tried a second time, watching the screen, then raised the phone higher toward the glass. His hand was shaking. He wanted to hear at least a ring tone. Any ordinary response: busy, unavailable, anything that would let him get angry at the connection.

Nothing.

"Katya, come on..."

He said it aloud and immediately turned back toward the corridor. No one was running from there to help him. The red frame divided the familiar place into narrow rectangles, and every one of them was empty.

A bell rang behind his right shoulder.

Asgar spun around, pressing the mobile to his chest. A black telephone hung on the wall. Beneath the receiver, a round metal dial gleamed. The ringing stopped; in the silence that followed, he could hear his sole scrape as he turned between the door and the wall.

The telephone rang again.

Asgar reached toward it with his free hand.
