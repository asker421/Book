Chapter 14. There Is No Short Way

Damir pulled the map toward himself.

Asgar moved his hands so the man could flatten one curled edge. Buildings, passages, and several crossed-out areas were marked on the dense sheet. Some marks had been drawn over older ones; the lines differed in thickness and color.

Damir traced a route north from the shelter, circled a large blank area, then came back toward the place where the booth stood.

“Could we go this way?”

Radmila answered before he did.

“Long.”

She indicated the northern route. More than a day of travel; the old road was blocked, then they would have to descend to a lower collector, and beyond that began another active sector. The last group had returned from there without getting past the pumping station.

Asgar pointed to the short line between the shelter and the booth.

“And here?”

“Less than two kilometers,” Damir said.

Radmila tapped the endpoint and named it: the old metro entrance.

Then she pointed to three patches on the map.

One belonged to maintenance complexes, another to old defense systems, the third to transport. Red marked active sensors, gray marked places whose condition no one had confirmed, green marked routes people had used recently.

“Green means safe?” Asgar asked.

“It means someone came back,” Radmila said.

She pulled the map toward herself.

“We go nowhere before evening. First we see whether the booth stays.”

A passive camera checked it every hour.

The booth remained.

“Why are you even helping me?” Asgar asked. “You’ve known me less than a day.”

Radmila shifted the map and tapped the route from the settlement to the booth.

“Send you alone?”

“For example.”

“Then you either die, or you run from a machine back here. Neither works for me.”

Asgar looked at her.

“So this isn’t charity.”

“No. I need you not to bring the problem to our entrance.”

Radmila slid the map slightly toward him.

“If you go, someone who knows the surface goes with you.”

“Damir.”

“Yes.”

Asgar studied the route again.

“What if I change my mind?”

“Then you stay here. No one drags you anywhere.”

Radmila reduced the preparation to one principle: give the old systems as little reason as possible to notice them.

Asgar was given clothing with a low thermal signature and shoes without active electronics. Nazar checked the dressing on his right knee and sealed it beneath a thin waterproof covering; part of the tunnel was flooded.

The dead phone stayed in his pocket.

Damir rejected the first outer layer immediately after finding a working tag in the collar.

The translator had to stay behind too.

Nazar would accompany them only as far as the tunnel exit.

Before they left, Radmila stood Asgar in front of the map and made him repeat the route from landmarks.

He got halfway and lost it.

She returned him to the beginning.

“Again.”

The second time, he named everything.

“Now the rules. First.”

“When Damir stops, I stop.”

“Second.”

“Turn nothing on.”

“Not ‘turn nothing on.’ Turn nothing on out of curiosity. Those are different, and the second is the one you’ll break.”

Asgar said nothing.

“Third. A machine that let you pass once did not become safe. It let you pass. That’s all.”

Radmila ran a fingernail along the green line.

“And remember this. Green does not mean ‘safe.’ Green means ‘someone returned alive from here.’”

“What if we lose each other?”

“You do not come back here from memory. Find a quiet place and stay there. Otherwise you bring whatever was following you straight to our entrance.”

She waited until he nodded.

“Last one. If any machine follows you and won’t break off, there is no return route for either of you. Damir draws it toward the industrial sector. You go to your booth or find cover. Not after him.”

Asgar looked at Damir.

He stood by the exit already dressed, looking toward the stairs.

“He knows that?”

“He came up with it,” Radmila said.

“Why are you even coming with me?” Asgar asked.

Damir tightened a strap.

“You don’t know the route.”

“I understand it’s dangerous.”

“Understanding isn’t enough. On the surface, sometimes you notice the mistake after the machine has already noticed you.”

“Very reassuring.”

Damir checked the closure on his bag.

“If you’re changing your mind, say it now. Once we start walking, we walk. We don’t debate.”

“How many people died out there?”

Damir looked toward the red sector.

“I didn’t count. Enough that people stopped using the short way.”

The service tunnel ran beneath the ruined district for almost seven hundred meters.

Cable channels, pumping shafts, and technical rooms still survived here. In several places they had to climb over fallen concrete.

Every few dozen meters, Damir touched a small passive indicator to the wall. When its strip turned yellow, they stopped.

Once, a heavy vibration passed through the ceiling: something large was moving along the street above them.

Only after the trembling faded did Damir signal to continue.

Without the translator, the route became quieter and at the same time easier to understand.

Palm down: stop.

Two fingers toward the wall: don’t touch the equipment.

A short motion back: retreat several steps.

In the tunnel, Asgar did not need to understand the history of the machines.

He only had to put his foot where the person who had walked here before him put his.

At one section, Damir moved onto a metal grating along the wall. The passive indicator showed a weak signal beneath the ordinary floor.

They waited until the signal disappeared, but Damir did not return to the previous route. He pointed to the indicator’s yellow trace, then to the bypass grating.

Asgar followed.

At a pumping shaft, the lower route was partly blocked. They could squeeze through a narrow gap or climb one level higher into an area the map marked unknown.

Damir inspected the concrete, pointed at Asgar’s shoulders, then at the opening and gestured for him to go first.

Asgar removed his outer jacket and squeezed through sideways.

For a second, his shoulder caught.

The automatic urge was to jerk free.

He stopped, exhaled, turned his body, and passed through without bringing down the loose edge.

Damir followed and gave him a silent thumbs-up.

Farther on, the tunnel was flooded almost to the knee.

The water smelled of metal and settled dust.

Asgar pointed toward a nearby technical room.

Damir brought the indicator to the door; its strip turned yellow.

He indicated a cable running behind the wall cladding and shook his head.

They went through the water.

By the end of the section their feet were completely soaked. Asgar walked more carefully, feeling cold water move inside his shoes with every step.

At one branch they stopped before an old service door.

Damir traced the short spur on the map, then the longer detour; by scale, the difference was almost two hundred meters.

Then he tapped the date of the last passage—seven months earlier—and shook his head.

They took the longer route.

Nazar stayed below at the exit.

From there, open surface began.

Asgar and Damir climbed an emergency ladder into the basement of a building missing half its façade.

In the gray evening light several blocks away, a repair machine hovered beside a ruined bridge and printed a new section of support onto a structure that had led nowhere for a very long time.

It worked perfectly and pointlessly at the same time.

Two hundred meters later, an old panel came alive.

Damir dropped to the concrete instantly and dragged Asgar down with him.

A small scanning unit appeared over the street.

A band of light swept across them, lingered for a second, then moved on.

Damir waited a full minute before rising, then signaled for Asgar to stay close to the wall and continued.

At the next intersection, Damir stopped before any light appeared.

He was not looking at the road.

He was watching thin strips of plastic beside a wall.

They lay parallel, as if a recent gust had blown them into alignment, though there was no wind.

Damir pointed out the direction, and they changed streets.

Several minutes later, a white pulse flashed along their old route.

Asgar looked again at the strips of plastic.

Farther on, they encountered a security machine.

It stood at an invisible boundary and ignored small cleaning units.

A heavy maintenance platform crossed the line—

a white pulse cut through its body.

A minute later, another machine was already gathering the fragments and rebuilding the damaged unit.

Damir did not hurry.

For ten minutes they watched which objects passed without a response.

Small devices: yes.

An empty cargo platform made the sensor turn, but no shot followed.

From the far side, another maintenance machine of the same type as the destroyed one emerged.

It gathered some components and tried to push the restored body through the boundary.

The sensor turned again.

The first pulse came after roughly two seconds.

The second, three seconds later.

Damir showed Asgar a route along the dead cargo platform and the next piece of cover.

About forty meters separated them.

Asgar gestured: wait one more cycle?

Damir nodded.

They watched another sequence.

It repeated.

Damir pointed again along the platform.

When the next maintenance machine entered the far edge and the sensor turned toward it, he waved Asgar forward.

Asgar ran.

Twenty meters.

His soles struck concrete, the bag slammed against his back, his breathing broke on the third stride.

Nothing happened.

At thirty meters, something snapped.

Dry.

Short.

To the right.

Damir struck him down across the shoulder from behind.

Asgar dropped, put out his hands, and hit his left hip.

A white line passed above the platform.

His right leg jerked; fire flared beneath the dressing.

He lay there with only his own breathing in his ears.

A straight strip of melted metal marked the top of the platform.

In cover, Damir checked whether the wound had begun bleeding again and gave him two signs:

forward or back.

Asgar stood, took several steps along the wall, crouched, then rose again.

The right knee hurt more, but it held his weight.

Going back meant crossing the same open space again and making the entire return journey to the tunnel.

Less than half the route remained ahead.

He pointed at the leg, then forward, then finally raised his palm: stop if necessary.

Damir watched the way he set his foot down and nodded.

The next hundred meters went more slowly.

Nothing followed them.

The knee gradually stopped occupying all his attention.

The route then entered what had once been an energy district.

Cable recesses smelled of ozone and burned plastic; identical dark marks covered the walls.

Between structures, panels from different systems had been installed one over another.

Dozens of hand-painted symbols began appearing on the walls.

At one, Damir touched his water flask.

At another, he pointed to a pocket and shook his head.

A third, beside a reflective façade, he passed at a wider distance.

Asgar began linking marks to actions.

Beside one symbol stood a line of local characters and a date.

Damir touched them with his fingers and pointed back toward the settlement.

Asgar paused at the wall but asked nothing.

Halfway through the energy district they had to wait almost forty minutes.

A heavy machine moved slowly along an old cable route, stopping at equal intervals and pressing new connection nodes into the ground.

Damir sat in a concrete recess and signaled to wait.

They did not emerge until the machine vanished behind a distant building.

While they waited, Damir took out a small metal token and placed it near the edge of the recess.

Five minutes later a service drone passed along the street.

It did not alter its route.

Damir retrieved the token.

Asgar asked with gestures what that had been.

Damir laid the token on his palm, pointed after the drone, and traced its straight path with one finger.

Then he pointed at the token again, swept his finger sideways as though changing a trajectory, and tapped the next section of the map.

The drone continued straight.

Damir put the token away and signaled to move.

From the final piece of cover, they watched the booth for almost ten minutes.

Two small machines passed on a neighboring street without approaching.

Even from here, the red structure looked wrong: the concrete canopy of the old metro entrance intersected its upper section without pressing against it or crumbling.

Damir indicated that he would go no farther.

Asgar pointed toward the booth, then raised a stopping hand toward Damir: from here, alone.

Damir stayed in cover.

Asgar walked toward the booth.

The closer he came, the less the geometry made sense.

The top of the red structure passed through the concrete canopy. From outside, it looked as though the part of the canopy intersecting the booth did not exist; through the side glass, the same concrete continued inside at a different height.

The lower edge of the door lay slightly below the broken paving, though the interior floor remained level.

The telephone was ringing.

Asgar opened the door, kept both feet outside, and lifted the receiver.

“The booth is holding, but the transition isn’t ready yet.”

“Why?”

“Something outside keeps changing the environment around it. The boundary can’t stabilize.”

“What system?”

Damir called out sharply behind him.

Asgar turned.

Damir had raised one arm high.

Three low dark machines had appeared on the far side of the street.

They moved in synchronization and blocked the road, but did not fire.

One stopped about fifty meters from the booth.

For the first time since Asgar had arrived in this time, a human voice came from its body—old, distorted English.

Asgar knew enough English for work and travel.

He understood the sentence without translation.

“Temporal anomaly. Civilian exclusion protocol active.”

Asgar froze.

The machine repeated the phrase without changing position or coming closer.

“Keep the door open.”

“Why?”

“If you close it, the transition may begin. The boundary is still unstable.”

The first machine repeated its message, then added:

“Civilian route unavailable. Maintain exclusion boundary.”

The machines kept their distance and blocked approaches from the street—almost like Inessa’s service in 2191, except with no people beside them.

The words *temporal anomaly* lodged in Asgar’s mind.

Someone, sometime, had put that category into their protocol.

He could get nothing more from the message.

With every new sweep of the scanning beam, the voice in the receiver reported increasingly divergent boundary readings.

The numbers moved, but always around the same distance—

slightly less than twelve meters from the red structure.

Beyond that, the sensors began agreeing with one another again.

Asgar fixed on the number, but another beam passed over the frame and the readings diverged again.

He raised his voice.

“Can you hear me?”

A white light blinked on the machine’s front panel.

“Civilian voice channel active.”

“Can you disable scanning?”

“Authorization required.”

Then the machine gave a set of codes and an authority domain.

None meant anything to Asgar.

He asked whether the object itself was considered a threat to civilians.

“Threat assessment unavailable without command authority,” the machine replied.

Then it repeated the requirement to maintain the exclusion boundary.

From cover, Damir raised a hand: do not move.

All three machines remained where they were.

Every new pass of the scanning beam coincided with another jump in the readings being called out through the receiver.

Asgar pressed the receiver to his ear.

“They call this a temporal anomaly. Do you know that protocol?”

“No. I’ve never heard the name before.”

“Does that help?”

“Not yet. Their scanning is interfering with the boundary.”

At that same moment, one of the old sensors swept a beam across the red edge again.

Someone on the line sharply demanded that the active interaction be removed.

Without taking the receiver from his ear, Asgar repeated the demand to the machine in English.

It answered with its own warning.

The blue beam crossed the red frame again.

At the same time, multiple voices broke out in the receiver; one was calling parameters, another demanding that the external interaction stop.

Asgar looked from the machine to the telephone.

“Perfect. Both of you are trying to make this safer.”

A short pause on the line.

“Yes.”

The red edge in front of him wavered almost imperceptibly.

“And together you’re making it worse.”

“Right now—yes.”

The red door trembled on its hinges and remained open.

“Do not enter. Keep the door open. While they’re scanning, the transition isn’t ready.”

Asgar remained outside without crossing the threshold and placed his left palm against the edge of the open panel.

From cover, Damir kept his hand raised: don’t move.

The three machines held position.

One repeated again:

“Temporal anomaly. Civilian exclusion protocol active.”
