Chapter 14. There Is No Short Route

Damir pulled the map toward himself.

Asgar took his hands away so he could flatten the curled edge. Buildings, passages, and several crossed-out areas were marked on the dense material. Some markings had been drawn over older ones: the lines differed in thickness and color.

Damir ran a finger north from the shelter, went around a large empty area, and returned to the place where the booth stood.

"Can we go this way?"

Radmila answered before he did:

"Too long."

She showed the northern detour. More than a day's travel; the old road was blocked, farther on they had to descend to the lower collector, and beyond that another active sector began. The last group had returned from there without getting past the pumping station.

Asgar pointed to the short line between the shelter and the booth.

"And here?"

"Less than two kilometers," Damir said.

Radmila tapped the endpoint and named it: the old metro entrance. Then she pointed to three areas on the map. One belonged to maintenance complexes, another to the old defense system, the third to the transport system. Red marked active sensors, gray marked places whose condition no one had confirmed, green marked routes people had recently traveled.

"Green means it's safe?" Asgar asked.

"It means someone came back," Radmila answered.

She pulled the map toward herself.

"We don't go anywhere before evening. First we see whether the cabin stays."

A passive camera checked the booth every hour. The cabin remained in place.

"Why are you helping me at all?" Asgar asked. "You've known me less than a day."

Radmila moved the map and jabbed a finger at the route from the settlement to the booth.

"Send you alone?"

"For example."

"Then you'll either die or run back here from a machine. Neither option works for me."

Asgar looked at her.

"So this isn't charity."

"No. I need you not to bring the problem to our entrance."

Radmila nudged the map slightly toward him.

"If you go, someone who knows the surface goes with you."

"Damir."

"Yes."

Asgar looked at the route again.

"And if I change my mind?"

"Then you stay here. No one will drag you."

Radmila reduced the preparation to one principle: give the old systems as little reason as possible to notice them. Asgar was given clothing with a low thermal profile and footwear with no active electronics. Nazar checked the bandage on his right knee and covered it with a thin waterproof layer: part of the tunnel was flooded. The dead phone stayed in his pocket. Damir immediately rejected the first cloak: there was a working tag in the collar. The translator had to be left behind too. Nazar would accompany them only as far as the tunnel exit.

Before they left, Radmila put him in front of the map and made him repeat the route by landmarks. Asgar got halfway and lost it. She sent him back to the beginning.

"Again."

The second time he named everything.

"Now the rules. First."

"Damir stops, I stop."

"Second."

"Turn nothing on."

"Not 'turn nothing on.' Don't turn anything on out of curiosity. Those are different things, and the second one is the one you'll break."

Asgar said nothing.

"Third. A machine that let you pass once did not become safe. It let you pass. That's all."

Radmila ran a fingernail along the green line.

"And remember this. Green doesn't mean 'safe.' Green means 'someone came back alive from here.'"

"What if we lose each other?"

"You do not come back here from memory. Find a quiet place and sit. Otherwise you'll lead everything that was following you to our entrance."

She waited until he nodded.

"Last one. If any machine follows you and won't break off, neither of you comes back. Damir leads it into the industrial sector. You go to your cabin or find shelter. Not after him."

Asgar looked at Damir. He stood by the exit, already dressed, looking toward the stairs.

"He knows that?"

"He came up with it," Radmila said.

"Why are you even going with me?" Asgar asked.

Damir tightened a strap.

"You don't know the route."

"I already understand it's dangerous."

"Understanding isn't enough. On the surface, sometimes you notice a mistake only after the machine has noticed you."

"Very reassuring."

Damir checked the fastening on his bag.

"If you change your mind, say so now. After that we'll walk, not discuss."

"How many people died there?"

Damir looked at the red sector.

"I didn't count. Enough that people stopped taking the short route."

The service tunnel ran beneath the ruined district for almost seven hundred meters. Cable channels, pump shafts, and technical rooms still remained here. In several places they had to climb over collapsed concrete. Every few dozen meters, Damir touched the wall with a small passive indicator. When the strip turned yellow, they stopped. Once, a heavy vibration passed through the ceiling: something large was moving along the street above them. Only when the shaking was gone did Damir signal to continue.

Without the translator, the route became quieter and at the same time easier to understand. Palm down—stop. Two fingers toward the wall—do not touch the equipment. A short motion backward—go back several steps. In the tunnel, Asgar did not need to understand the history of the machines; he only had to put his foot in time where the person who had walked here before put his.

At one section, Damir moved onto a metal grate along the wall. The passive indicator showed a weak signal beneath the ordinary floor. They waited for the signal to disappear, but Damir did not return to the previous path. He pointed to the yellow mark on the indicator, then to the bypass grate by the wall. Asgar followed him.

At the pump shaft, the lower passage was partly blocked. They could squeeze through a narrow gap or go up one level, where the map marked an unknown zone. Damir examined the concrete, pointed at Asgar's shoulders, then at the gap, and gestured for him to go first. Asgar took off the outer jacket, squeezed sideways through, and for a second trapped his shoulder. The automatic impulse was to jerk. He stopped, breathed out, turned his body, and got through without bringing down the loose edge. Damir followed and silently gave him a thumbs-up.

Farther on, the tunnel was flooded almost to the knee. The water smelled of metal and stale dust. Asgar pointed to a neighboring technical room. Damir held the indicator to the door; the strip turned yellow. He pointed along the wall where a cable disappeared behind the facing and shook his head. They went through the water. By the end of the section, their feet were completely soaked. Asgar walked more carefully, feeling cold water inside his shoes with every step.

At one branch, they stopped before an old service door. Damir ran a finger along the short branch on the map, then along the long detour; by the scale, the difference was almost two hundred meters. Then he tapped the date of the last passage—seven months ago—and shook his head. They took the longer route.

At the exit, Nazar stayed below. Beyond it began the open surface. Asgar and Damir climbed an emergency ladder into the basement of a building missing half its facade. In the gray evening light several blocks away, a maintenance machine hovered beside a ruined bridge and printed a new section of support over a structure that had led nowhere for a long time. It worked perfectly and pointlessly at the same time.

Two hundred meters farther on, an old panel came alive. Damir instantly dropped to the concrete and pulled Asgar down with him. A small scanning device appeared above the street. A band of light passed over them, paused for a second, and moved on. Damir did not rise for another minute, then gestured to stay closer to the wall and led on.

At the next intersection, Damir stopped before any light appeared. He was looking not at the road but at light strips of plastic by the wall. They lay parallel, as though a single gust had blown them there recently, even though there was no wind. Damir indicated a direction, and they changed streets. Several minutes later, a white flash lit the route they had abandoned. Asgar looked at the strips of plastic again.

Farther on, they encountered a security machine. It stood at an invisible boundary and did not react to small cleaning devices. A heavy repair platform crossed the line—a white pulse cut through its body. A minute later, another machine was already collecting the debris and rebuilding the damaged device. Damir did not hurry. For ten minutes they watched which objects passed without a reaction. Small devices—yes. An empty cargo platform made the sensor turn, but no shot followed.

Another repair machine of the same type as the destroyed one came out from the far side. It gathered some of the parts and tried to push the restored body across the boundary. The sensor turned again. The first pulse came about two seconds later, the second three seconds after that. Damir showed Asgar the route along the dead transport platform and the next shelter. There were about forty meters between them.

Asgar asked with a gesture: wait again? Damir nodded. They watched another cycle. The sequence repeated. Damir pointed once more to the route along the platform. When the next repair machine came out at the far edge and the sensor turned toward it, he waved Asgar forward.

He ran.

Twenty meters. His soles hammered the concrete, the bag beat against his back, his breathing broke on the third step. Nothing happened.

At thirty, there was a click.

Dry, brief, to the right.

Damir struck him down across the shoulder. Asgar dropped, threw out his hands, and hit his left hip. A white line passed above the platform. His right leg jerked, and beneath the bandage it burned.

He lay there with nothing in his ears but his own breathing.

A clean strip of melted metal remained across the top of the platform.

In the shelter, Damir checked whether the bleeding had started again and showed two signs: continue or go back.

Asgar stood, took several steps along the wall, crouched, then stood again. His right knee hurt more, but it held his weight. Going back meant crossing the same open area again and retracing the entire route to the tunnel; less than half the route remained ahead. He pointed at the leg, then forward, and finally raised a palm: stop. Damir checked how Asgar placed his foot and nodded.

The next hundred meters went more slowly. No machines followed them. The knee gradually stopped occupying all of his attention. Farther on, the route passed through a former energy district. Cable recesses smelled of ozone and burned plastic; identical dark stains covered the walls. Between buildings, panels from different systems appeared layered over one another.

Dozens of hand-painted marks appeared on the walls. At one, Damir touched his flask; at another he pointed to his pocket and shook his head; he gave a third, near a reflective facade, a wider berth. Asgar began connecting the signs with actions. Beside one mark was a row of local symbols and a date. Damir touched them with his fingers and pointed back toward the settlement. Asgar lingered by the wall but asked nothing.

In the middle of the energy district, they had to wait almost forty minutes. A heavy machine moved slowly along an old cable line, stopping at equal intervals and pressing new connection nodes into the ground. Damir sat in a concrete recess and told Asgar to wait. They did not come out until the machine disappeared behind the far building.

While they waited, Damir took out a small metal token and placed it at the edge of the recess. Five minutes later, a service drone passed along the street. It did not change course. Damir took the token back. Asgar gestured to ask what that had been. Damir placed the token on his palm, pointed after the drone, and traced its straight path with a finger. Then he showed the token again, moved his finger sideways as though changing the trajectory, and tapped the next section of the map. The drone continued straight; Damir put the token away and signaled to continue.

From the final shelter, they watched the booth for almost ten minutes. Two small machines passed along the neighboring street and did not approach. The red cabin looked wrong even from here: the concrete canopy over the old metro entrance cut through its top but did not press on the body or crumble. Damir indicated that he was going no farther.

Asgar pointed at the booth, then stopped Damir with a palm: alone from here. Damir stayed in the shelter. Asgar walked toward the cabin.

The closer he came, the less the geometry made sense. The top of the red cabin passed through the concrete canopy. From outside, it looked as though part of the canopy was missing where the body intersected it; through the side glass, the same concrete continued inside at a different height. The lower edge of the door sat slightly below the broken paving, although the interior floor remained level. The telephone was ringing.

Asgar opened the door, kept both feet outside, and lifted the receiver.

"The cabin is holding, but the transition isn't ready yet."

"Why?"

"Something outside is constantly changing the environment around it. The boundary doesn't have time to stabilize."

"What system?"

Behind him, Damir called out sharply. Asgar turned: Damir raised a hand abruptly.

Three low dark machines appeared at the far side of the street. They moved in sync and blocked the roadway but did not open fire. One stopped about fifty meters from the booth. For the first time, a human voice came from its body—old, distorted English. Asgar knew the language well enough for work and travel; he understood the phrase without a translator.

"Temporal anomaly. Civilian exclusion protocol active."

Asgar froze. The machine repeated the same phrase without changing position or approaching him.

"Keep the door open."

"Why?"

"If you close it, the transition may begin. The boundary is still unstable."

The first machine repeated the message, then added: "Civilian route unavailable. Maintain exclusion boundary."

The machines kept their distance and blocked approaches from the street—almost like Inessa's service in 2191, only with no people nearby. The words temporal anomaly caught in Asgar's memory. Someone had once put such a category into their protocol; he could extract nothing else from the message.

With every new pass of the scanning beam, increasingly divergent boundary parameters were called out through the receiver. The numbers shifted, but each time around one distance—slightly less than twelve meters from the red body. Beyond that, the sensors began agreeing with one another again. Asgar fixed on the number, but another beam passed over the frame and the readings diverged again.

He raised his voice:

"Can you hear me?"

A white light blinked on the front panel of the machine.

"Civilian voice channel active."

"Can you disable scanning?"

"Authorization required."

Then the machine recited a set of codes and an authority domain. None of the codes meant anything to Asgar.

Asgar asked whether the object itself was considered a threat to civilians.

"Threat assessment unavailable without command authority," the machine answered.

After that, the demand to maintain the exclusion boundary sounded again.

Damir raised a palm from the shelter: don't move. The three machines remained where they were. Every new sweep of the scanning beam coincided with another jump in the readings being called out through the receiver.

Asgar pressed the receiver to his ear.

"They call it a temporal anomaly. Do you know that protocol?"

"No. I've never heard that name before."

"Does it help?"

"Not yet. Their scanning is interfering with the boundary."

At that very moment, one of the old sensors swept a beam across the red edge again. Someone in the receiver sharply demanded that the active influence be removed. Without lowering the receiver, Asgar repeated the demand to the machine in English. It answered with its own warning.

The blue beam passed across the red frame again. Other voices spilled through the receiver at once; one called out parameters, another demanded that the external influence be removed.

Asgar shifted his gaze from the machine to the telephone.

"Wonderful. You're both trying to make it safer."

A brief pause came on the line.

"Yes."

The red edge in front of him drifted almost imperceptibly.

"And together you're making it worse."

"Right now—yes."

The red door trembled on its hinges and remained open.

"Do not enter. Keep the door open. As long as they're scanning, the transition isn't ready."

Asgar stayed outside without crossing the threshold and placed his left palm on the edge of the open door. Damir kept his palm raised from the shelter: don't move. The three machines did not move; one repeated again:

"Temporal anomaly. Civilian exclusion protocol active."
