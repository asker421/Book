Chapter 12. A Crack in the Order

They left together when the car arrived.

Asgar locked his room and automatically tested the door with his palm. Martin was already waiting at the turn. A display was tucked under his arm and the translator was clipped to his chest; he had brought nothing else.

The covered passage smelled of wet flooring. Someone had just washed the floor somewhere nearby. Asgar stepped over a dark strip along the wall and thought for a second that he would have to go around it again that evening.

The car was waiting at the service exit.

On the way, Martin read through Inessa’s messages. Asgar watched outside. The buildings that had become familiar over the past few days quickly fell behind; beyond them came long structures divided by access roads and strips of greenery. Above one intersection, a large construction turned slowly, throwing a broad shadow across the road.

“Is it still there?” Asgar asked.

Martin turned the display toward him.

The booth stood beside a closed freight gate. A pale line ran along the floor. One branch of it reached the red structure and stopped.

“Is that live?”

“Delayed by a few seconds.”

Asgar leaned closer.

He wanted to see the handle. Work out whether the door would open, which side he should approach from.

No one moved in the image.

“They evacuated people?”

“From that passage, yes. They’re checking the other exits.”

Martin put the display away when the car turned.

Barriers stood ahead. Several service vehicles were parked beyond them. A person at the entrance raised a hand, waited for the car to stop, and looked inside.

Martin showed him the message.

They were allowed through, but from there they had to continue on foot.

Inessa met them at a side entrance.

“The booth is two turns from here,” the device on her chest translated. “We wait here for now. The main passage is clear, but not all automatic barriers have been disabled.”

Asgar looked at the door.

“They can close?”

“They can. We don’t go in until every one is checked.”

She turned toward an approaching employee.

They spoke quickly. Asgar caught a familiar word meaning *exit*, then lost the thread.

Martin’s translator supplied the end:

“…not according to the plan. Have someone walk all the way to the stairs.”

Inessa waited for confirmation and led them inside.

---

The observation room was crowded.

Several displays stood on a table against the wall. One showed the booth from the front, another from the side and above. A third was occupied by a plan of the complex. Colored areas shifted across it, and Asgar could not tell what they represented.

He stopped behind Martin.

In the side view, the booth almost touched the freight gate.

In the front view, there was enough space between them for a person to pass.

Asgar pointed at the screens.

“Different times?”

The employee at the table looked at him, then at Martin.

Martin repeated the question.

The man checked the timestamps.

“Same interval,” came the translation.

Asgar looked at both screens again.

On one, a passage remained between the booth and the gate.

On the other, almost none.

The cameras showed the same moment—

and different space.

Inessa came closer.

“Keep both images separate. Do not update the combined model.”

Part of the markup disappeared from the plan.

The employee was explaining something, moving a finger between two areas. Inessa interrupted him; the translation arrived late and overlapped the beginning of the next sentence.

Martin touched the device on his chest.

“Wait. I’ll isolate her channel.”

The speaker said:

“I don’t need the most probable route. I need to know where the people are. Confirm them by voice.”

If two systems could not even agree on where open space existed, a safe route on a screen guaranteed nothing.

Asgar stepped away from the table to make room.

A low rumble rolled through the corridor beyond the door.

One employee looked up.

On the front view, a broad gray shutter above the booth had begun descending. It moved slowly, closing off the upper part of the passage.

Inessa was already speaking into the link.

“Stop the drive. Local disconnect.”

The shutter jerked on-screen.

Asgar heard a metallic impact—first from the corridor, then, with a delay, through the speaker.

The lower edge of the barrier twisted sideways. A mounting tore out of the side rail and struck the floor.

Someone at the table swore.

The shutter stopped, leaving the passage open beneath it. Dust hung around the damaged edge.

Inessa looked at the plan.

“Everyone out of the inner zone. Use the side stairs. Confirm by people, not by the counter.”

Martin took Asgar by the forearm and guided him toward the door.

They went into the corridor. Two employees carrying portable displays followed them. One tried to turn back, but Inessa stopped him with a gesture.

“The recording stays. People leave.”

Asgar understood her before the translation.

---

The cold outside went straight through his T-shirt.

They stopped beyond the barrier beside a service road. People continued emerging through the side door.

Inessa stood where she could see each person and checked something on her device.

Asgar counted with her, though he did not know how many there should be.

When the last one came out, she put the device away and looked at him.

“You asked what difference another week would make.”

He did not answer.

Inessa turned toward the door, now empty, and gave someone the number of people who had exited.

Asgar stared at the door.

“Why did the shutter come down?”

Martin listened to messages arriving for Inessa, then unclipped the translator and set it on a high concrete ledge between them.

“The protection system decided the hazardous zone had expanded and tried to isolate it with the shutter.”

“And the booth underneath it?”

“Another system showed that location as open space.”

“So one system tried to close off an area another system believed was clear?”

“Yes.”

Asgar remembered the two images of the gate.

Inessa came over and heard the end of the translation.

“The actuator never received a central command. It was running on local backup. They’ve physically disconnected it now and are securing the shutter from outside the zone.”

“You’re switching everything off?”

“Anything that can move, close, or turn by itself. Ventilation and fire protection stay on, but we’ll isolate them from the rest of the control network.”

She looked at Martin.

“No research today.”

He nodded, then immediately turned his display toward her.

“We can leave the passive channels. The clocks on the different systems—”

“Keep recording. Don’t initiate anything to clarify.”

Martin lowered the display.

Asgar heard another metallic strike beyond the wall.

Inessa raised a hand and listened to a message.

“They’re securing the shutter,” she said. “Don’t go back.”

Asgar looked down.

During the conversation he had moved one step closer to the building.

---

While they waited, Asgar received a call on the work tablet.

A message from the phone specialist appeared: the inspection time was confirmed, the device could be brought in after lunch.

Asgar read it and closed it.

Martin stood beside him but did not ask.

The last employee from the interior team came out of the complex. White dust marked one sleeve.

Inessa stopped him, listened, then called someone over the link.

The passage appeared again on the portable display.

The shutter was no longer moving. The torn mounting lay beside the wall. The booth stood beneath the twisted edge without touching it.

Asgar leaned closer.

“That line on the right was straight.”

He pointed at the seam between floor panels.

Now the seam split into two branches. A narrow wedge of surface lay between them, as though part of the floor had been turned through a small angle.

Inessa enlarged the area.

The image trembled, recovered.

The wedge remained.

“After the shutdown?” Martin asked.

The employee at the screen checked the timestamp and confirmed it.

Asgar straightened.

“So it isn’t the automation.”

Inessa requested the second camera.

From the other angle, a thin crack was visible at the same location. Its edge was still pale from the fresh break.

She ordered the perimeter expanded.

Behind them, vehicles began moving farther away.

Asgar watched a driver remove a cup from a roof, climb in, and reverse.

People moved calmly. No one ran.

But the road was becoming emptier.

“Is the telephone ringing?” he asked.

Martin looked at the employee.

The man activated the camera audio.

Through the ventilation noise came the familiar ring.

Asgar exhaled and rubbed both hands over his face.

“I want to talk to her.”

---

Inessa first called the people who had checked the side route and asked them to show it using a hand camera.

Asgar stood beside her and watched.

A door.

Short corridor.

Turn.

Open area in front of the booth.

They would not have to pass beneath the damaged shutter. The telephone could be approached from the side while remaining on the intact part of the floor.

“If anything changes under your feet, you turn back. No ‘one more step,’” Inessa said.

Asgar nodded.

Martin took his tablet and checked the link.

“Keep translation on while you walk. If Inessa speaks, you’ll hear her.”

“I’ll have the receiver once I’m there.”

“Only while you’re walking. Put the tablet away at the door.”

Asgar looked at the thin device.

“I’d rather call from here before I approach. I don’t want to leave anything beside it.”

Inessa heard and nodded.

“Good. If you lose the link before reaching the open area, you turn around. Once you’re there, we watch by camera. If anything changes, move away immediately. Don’t wait for an instruction.”

Then he went toward the side entrance.

Inside smelled of dust and warm metal.

His own footsteps were too distinct.

At the turn, he stopped and looked back.

Martin was visible in the open doorway.

He went no farther.

Asgar raised one hand and continued.

The booth came fully into view.

The shutter hung to the left over the main passage. A bent rod projected from the torn mounting.

Asgar tried not to look beneath it.

His eyes kept going back anyway.

Only a few steps of level floor remained between him and the red door.

He reported that he had arrived, put the tablet away, and pulled the handle toward himself.

The door opened.

Asgar lifted the receiver without crossing the threshold.

“I’m here.”

“Asgar. I hear you.”

“Nineteen days.”

The woman asked what he meant.

“I’ve lived here for nineteen days. Now it appeared somewhere else. They evacuated everyone, but the building is breaking.”

He told her about the shutter, the crack, the disconnected drives.

The woman asked him to clarify whether the changes had continued after shutdown.

“Yes. I saw it myself on two cameras.”

An indistinct voice sounded on the line. She answered someone, but Asgar could not make out the words.

“Yes,” she said at last. “We’re seeing the same thing here.”

“Can you remove the booth?”

“Not now.”

“Do I have to go inside?”

“After your previous entries, the changes stopped. We don’t know why. I won’t tell you your entering is certain to help. It won’t repair the damage in any case.”

Asgar looked at the door.

The same scratch remained beside the handle.

“What if I don’t go in?”

“We can wait. The booth may leave by itself. But I don’t know what may change before that.”

“So we can simply wait.”

“We can. If the people have already been moved away.”

He listened to her breathing between the words.

“You still haven’t reached the source?”

“No. There’s still no connection to it.”

Asgar closed his eyes.

Something small clinked behind him.

He turned at once.

Dust was falling from the ceiling by the far wall.

The red structure remained motionless.

“I need to move away,” he said.

“Replace the receiver. Don’t pull the cord.”

He put it back on the cradle and stepped away from the door.

He walked quickly on the return route, but stopped at the turn to check the floor.

Beyond that, the surface remained intact.

Martin was waiting outside.

---

“We’re evacuating people either way,” Inessa said after hearing him out. “That’s our responsibility, not yours. Don’t stand here because you think everyone is waiting for your decision.”

Asgar looked along the access road.

A service vehicle stood well beyond the new barrier.

He could walk to it, sit down, go back to his room.

The bread would still be on the table.

The specialist would connect his phone.

That evening, a message might arrive saying the booth had disappeared by itself.

Or another kind of message.

“How far can you move people?” he asked.

“As far as necessary. But if the boundary starts changing faster than we can move people, that becomes a problem.”

She spoke evenly, neither persuading nor rushing him.

Martin stood a little apart.

“Can you do anything else?” Asgar asked him.

Martin shook his head.

“There’s nothing left to switch off without causing an accident ourselves. And we don’t know how to close this boundary.”

Asgar looked at the display.

The crack had grown longer. An employee compared two frames, switching back and forth. In one, it ended at the seam. In the other, it crossed it.

On the plan, another area flashed on the opposite side of the booth. Inessa asked that both remain visible.

The ends of the changes lay at almost the same distance from the red structure even though the cameras still disagreed about where exactly the floor was.

Asgar watched until the switching stopped.

He thought about Katya.

The last time he had entered, she had been somewhere close—behind glass, in the city, in the same time.

Now more than a century and a half lay between them.

There was nowhere to call.

The choice was still his.

“I’ll go in.”

Martin lowered his head, then looked at him again.

“You really think it’ll help?”

“After my previous entries, the changes stopped. Maybe that’s coincidence. But I don’t know what else we can do.”

“Has she been wrong before?”

Asgar remembered four days.

Twenty-five years.

Katya behind the glass.

“She never promised me I’d get home. I was the one who kept hearing a little more than she actually said.”

He took the work tablet from his pocket.

The message confirming the phone inspection was still on-screen.

Asgar handed the device to Martin.

“Return this for me, please. It was issued in my name.”

Martin took it but kept looking at him.

“Your room stays as it is. I won’t remove anything.”

Asgar nodded.

“And the recordings. I marked one of them but didn’t finish.”

“We’ll sort it out.”

Martin said it in the local language.

Asgar understood before the translator.

They both noticed and smiled for a second.

Then Martin held out his hand.

Asgar shook it.

The palm was dry and warm.

He wanted to say something ordinary, so he would not leave Martin standing there with that expression.

“Water the plant less.”

Martin gave a short breath through his nose.

“Come back and check for yourself.”

Asgar did not promise.

---

This time he walked without the tablet.

Inessa stood by the side entrance with Martin. Before Asgar turned the corner, she raised one hand to show that the route was still clear.

He returned the gesture.

Beyond that, there was only the ringing.

At the booth, Asgar stopped.

The crack ran to the left of his route through the middle of the main passage.

Beside the wall, an intact strip of floor remained.

He opened the door and lifted the receiver.

“I’m going in. The floor on this side is still level.”

The woman asked him to wait.

Asgar stood before the threshold holding the door.

In that time, he managed to remember the mug on the table, the clean clothes on the shelf, the route to the sea.

He had not chosen this city.

But now he knew how to walk through it in the evening.

“You can enter,” the woman said. “Once you’re inside, close the door completely.”

He crossed the threshold.

The cord brushed his wrist. Asgar shifted the receiver, freed his hand, and pulled the door toward him.

Just before it closed, he looked down the passage one more time.

Empty.

Light on the floor.

Far beyond the turn were people who knew him by name.

The door settled into the frame.

Asgar braced a shoulder against the wall, waiting for the jolt.

“Closed.”

The woman began to answer, but her voice cut off.

The floor rocked.

Asgar bent his knees and stayed upright with his free palm pressed against the frame. For several moments the lit passage remained before him, then the lamps stretched into bands and went dark.

He remained standing in the same position.

Beyond the glass was darkness.

Then his eyes began to distinguish a pale sky above the black edge of a ruined wall.

Wind drove dust across the ground.

Where there had been level flooring a second ago, fragments of debris protruded.

Asgar raised the receiver to his ear.

“Are you there?”

Only faint static remained.

He slowly took his hand off the door handle.
