Interlude III. An Unfinished Lunch

She heard the click when he replaced the receiver.

The voice channel closed.

On the adjacent field, the movement marker shifted away from the apparatus.

“He stepped away,” she said.

The engineer did not look up.

“You cleared him to enter?”

“After the checks. He wants to say goodbye.”

Now the engineer looked at her.

“For long?”

“I told him not to take too long.”

The neighboring panel continued updating.

The external interaction that had prevented them from confirming transition stability had decreased.

So far it had not returned, but the woman kept watching the same region as though looking away for a moment would make her miss the beginning of another rise.

A food container lay on the desk.

Someone had set it beside her cup while she was speaking with Asgar. One edge had already grown damp.

The woman pushed the container aside to make room for her elbow.

The engineer went back to checking the external interaction.

She left that section to him and pulled the voice channel closer.

Asgar had not returned yet.

The food beside her elbow was already in the way, but she still did not get up to move it.

Last time he had asked where he would arrive.

She had answered immediately: the exit could not be determined.

The silence that followed had made her want to fill it with something useful.

To say that the state looked better now.

That the external interaction had dropped.

That they were still working.

She had stayed silent.

None of those things answered his question.

The door behind her opened.

Corwin came in with Mirena.

Mirena stopped beside an empty station without touching the controls and looked first at the channel, then at the cup and untouched food.

“What stage?”

“Outside. Entry is admissible. He stepped away to say goodbye.”

Mirena pulled a chair closer but did not sit yet.

Corwin remained behind them.

“You hand over after closure,” he said.

“I can finish the sequence.”

“After closure.”

The woman was about to say this was not about fatigue.

Then she noticed how tightly her fingers were gripping the edge of the desk and let go.

“If the next exit is habitable, I have to tell him he can stay there.”

“You will.”

“And if he refuses because he still thinks we’re taking him home?”

“Then first you tell him plainly: we are not taking him home. We don’t know how.”

She turned toward Corwin.

“And if after that he still wants to continue?”

“Then he decides.”

“Even though the next transition may be worse?”

“It may.”

“And we let him go?”

Corwin looked at her.

“Would you rather lock him in the first place where a person can survive?”

The woman looked at the empty channel.

“We know what he wants.”

“To go home. We don’t know where, for him, the attempt to get home ends.”

“That isn’t enough?”

“No.”

She ran her thumb along the edge of the desk.

“If a place appears where he can live, and he continues only because he thinks we are still guiding him home, I don’t want to pretend that’s a neutral choice.”

“Then don’t. Tell him he can stay. Tell him the next place may be worse. Tell him we never promised home.”

“And after that let him go?”

“If he says ‘I’m going’—yes.”

“And if continuing is dangerous?”

“Then I won’t let him. That’s why I’m here.”

The woman breathed out briefly through her nose.

“Are you defending his choice right now?”

“No. I’m not letting you make it for him.”

“Convenient wording.”

Corwin looked at the engineer, then back at her.

“I’ve already watched an operator decide he knew what was best for a person. He pushed the procedure forward. We lost the person. Part of the circuit too.”

For the first time in the conversation, she looked away.

“That wasn’t here.”

“Once was enough for me.”

“I haven’t violated a single limit.”

“Not yet.”

“Then why are you taking me off?”

“Because you’ve already decided what his answer should be. And we don’t even know where he’s going to emerge.”

She opened her mouth and said nothing.

Corwin nodded toward Mirena.

“Your shift is over.”

“I’m not talking about fatigue.”

“Neither am I. You’re attached to him. That’s normal. At the console, it’s starting to interfere.”

“I’m not talking about the system.”

“And I have to think about both the system and him. That’s why Mirena takes over.”

The voice channel appeared on the panel again.

Almost immediately, the door state changed.

All three of them went silent.

The woman moved closer to the link.

Something knocked in the noise.

There was breathing—short and tense—then the sound became muffled.

The door locked.

She instinctively reached to call Asgar, but the engineer raised a hand.

“Wait.”

The transition measurements came in unevenly.

She saw the beginning of the familiar sequence, then several values disappeared.

The adjacent field retained only the previous data.

Its age began increasing.

“Is that current?” she asked.

“Not anymore.”

She took her hand away.

The connection died in the middle of a faint rustle.

The woman straightened and checked the audio level even though she could see the link was gone.

Behind her, Corwin moved closer to the engineer.

The first updates returned separately.

The engineer waited for one more, magnified part of the record.

“Door remains locked. Continuing checks.”

She stared at the empty channel field.

Until calling Asgar became possible again, there was nothing to do there.

She still could not make herself move back.

Mirena sat beside her.

“Tell me what happened before this.”

“The record is open.”

“I’ll read it. Tell me now.”

The woman turned toward her, intending to say that everything necessary had already been logged.

Instead she picked up the cup.

A little cold liquid remained at the bottom.

She did not want it.

“He approached the telephone from outside. Reported that the external system called the state a temporal anomaly. There was active interaction with the boundary, so I prohibited entry. After his access review, the interaction decreased. Then he stepped away to say goodbye. I cleared entry only after another assessment.”

Mirena listened without interrupting.

“What does he know about the next exit?”

“That we can’t determine it.”

“He asked again?”

“Yes.”

The woman set down the cup.

Its edge landed on the food container and tilted; she had to catch it with both hands.

Mirena moved the food to a dry spot.

“What about him physically?”

“After the previous exit he was ambulatory. He approached on his own just now and spoke coherently. He reported no injuries.”

“Then I’ll ask the rest.”

She nodded.

Suddenly she wanted to warn Mirena that Asgar disliked being spoken to as though his only role were to obey instructions.

But after the conversation with Corwin, the thought sounded different.

She did not know whether he liked any kind of conversation anymore, or whether she had the right to turn his irritation into a ready-made instruction for another person.

“When he asks where he’s going,” she said, “give him time after the answer.”

Mirena looked at her.

“All right.”

“And if there’s a place where he can stay, don’t lead him toward an answer.”

Mirena frowned slightly.

“You think I would?”

“I thought I wouldn’t.”

For several seconds they looked at each other.

“Understood,” Mirena said.

She opened the summary.

Read the first lines, scrolled farther down, then returned.

“It says: ‘External system classifies state as temporal anomaly.’”

“According to Asgar,” the woman said.

“Then I’ll leave it that way.”

She added the qualification and continued reading.

Corwin stepped away from the engineer.

“That’s enough for you today.”

The woman looked at him, then at the still-unavailable channel.

“I want to hear what he answers.”

“They’ll hear it here.”

The woman pushed back her chair.

Her legs had gone stiff; she had to stand for a moment holding the backrest.

Mirena took the vacated place and drew the controls closer.

The woman’s eyes lingered on the cup.

It still stood where she had left it.

A damp mark from her sleeve remained on the surface beside it, and another person was already preparing to work there.

The woman collected the food container and cup.

At the door, she stopped.

“He may think something happened to me.”

Mirena turned.

“I’ll tell him you were relieved.”

“Tell him immediately.”

“I will.”

She went into the corridor.

It was cooler there.

Voices came from the neighboring room; someone laughed quietly and went straight on with the conversation.

The woman walked several steps, stopped beside the wall, and finally opened the food container.

The meal had gone cold and stuck to the lining.

She separated a small piece but could not swallow immediately.

She had to wash it down.

---

Inside the room, the engineer finished his check.

“Channel available. You can call.”

Mirena looked once more at the final conversation record.

Then she moved it aside and initiated the call.

While the signal sounded, she waited with one hand beside the volume control.

The receiver was lifted at the other end.

“Asgar?” Mirena said.

“I’m here,” he replied. “What happened?”
