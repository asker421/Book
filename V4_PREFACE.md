# Preface

When we say "I'll be there soon," we rarely think about time. We picture the road, the familiar turn, the key in the lock. The person at the other end of the conversation will stay in the kitchen a little longer, the children will not have time to fall asleep, whatever was left on the table will wait for us. Those two words contain an entire agreement with the world that we never actually made.

The future usually arrives in small portions. A light bulb needs replacing, a message needs answering, a child needs new shoes. Gradually the voice of someone close to us changes, a familiar gesture becomes a little slower, a name that no one knew before appears in conversation. We notice far from everything. Life allows us to be inattentive, and sometimes that looks like a promise that one day we will be able to catch up.

Science fiction allows us to step beyond the human scale. To let centuries pass between two steps, to see Earth beneath an unfamiliar sky, to find ourselves among people who have had to define the simplest words all over again. But a person carries their own measure of time into that distance. How long since they last heard a familiar voice. When they last ate at a shared table. How long since it mattered to anyone whether they would be home by evening.

I wanted the enormous distances of time in this book to preserve that closeness. For every device to leave behind someone who repairs it, every answer someone who takes responsibility for answering, and every person encountered in the middle of the impossible to have their own things to do. Because even where our imagination ends, someone may still need to put their work aside, pull over a chair, and listen to a stranger.

The Red Booth begins on an ordinary evening. The stadium can still be heard, people at home are waiting at the table, and a phone conversation cannot quite end because of the noise. A man only needs to find a quiet place to hear the last words.
