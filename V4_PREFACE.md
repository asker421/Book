# Preface

When we say, “I’ll be there soon,” we rarely think about time. We picture the road, a familiar turn, a key in the lock. The person on the other end of the call will stay in the kitchen a little longer, the children will not have fallen asleep yet, whatever was left on the table will still be waiting for us. Those few words contain an entire agreement with the world—one we never actually made.

The future usually arrives in small portions. A lightbulb needs changing. A message needs answering. A child needs new shoes. Little by little, the voice of someone close to us changes; a familiar gesture grows a shade slower; a name no one used to know appears in conversation. We miss much of it. Life allows us to be inattentive, and sometimes that feels like a promise that one day we will have time to catch up.

Science fiction lets us step outside the human scale. It can put centuries between two footsteps, show us Earth beneath an unfamiliar sky, place us among people who have had to redefine the simplest words. But a person carries their own measure of time even that far. How long since they heard a familiar voice. When they last ate at a table with others. How long it has been since anyone cared whether they would be home by evening.

I wanted the immense distances of time in this book to retain that closeness. For every machine to have someone behind it who has to repair it; every answer, someone willing to take responsibility for giving it; every person met in the middle of the impossible, a life and obligations of their own. Because even where our imagination runs out, someone may still need to set their work aside, pull up a chair, and listen to a stranger.

*The Red Booth* begins on an ordinary evening. The stadium can still be heard, dinner is waiting at home, and a phone call refuses to end because of the noise. A man only needs to find somewhere quiet enough to hear the last few words.
